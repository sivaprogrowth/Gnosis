## Summary.

For decades, firms have sharpened competitive advantage by observing their customers through surveys, user groups, complaints, and usage data—all of which has increasingly been enabled by the rise of the internet.

What is new today is that neither side of this relationship is purely human any longer. Both B2C and B2B customers are beginning to research potential purchases with the help of prompts and agents, [forming their opinions of companies through AI before they even engage with them.](https://hbr.org/2026/03/llms-are-overtaking-search-heres-how-to-adjust-your-online-presence) According to a McKinsey survey, shopping-related usage is now [the third most popular application of generative AI](https://www.mckinsey.com/featured-insights/week-in-charts/shopping-gets-an-ai-assist?utm_source=chatgpt.com), and the tool’s input is decisive for many. Around 50% of consumers use AI for researching products and services, [according to a recent report by Semrush](https://www.semrush.com/blog/ai-tools-the-modern-buyer-journey-study/), a brand visibility platform. Categories in which AI plays a key role in decision-making include not only consumer goods (39%) but also travel (21%) and financial services (13%).

The emergence of this new intermediary changes where competitive advantage resides, how firms recognize it, and how they can manage it. In this article, we look at how three small to medium sized businesses (SMBs) we have advised or studied have adapted to three different but related challenges in this new environment. (The company names are disguised throughout.)

## The Problem: Qualifying Inbound Demand

For many SMBs, a rise in inbound inquiries reads as good news. More requests for pricing quotes (RFQs), more inbound emails, more supplier-comparison requests—all of it suggests the market is paying attention. AI-assisted buying complicates that signal.

B2B customers can now use AI tools to generate supplier lists, draft procurement emails, prepare requests for quotation, and fire polished enquiries at a dozen firms in minutes. The buying process becomes faster for the customer and noisier for the supplier. A firm can receive more RFQs without receiving more real opportunities, and its sales team ends up spending more time quoting and less time diagnosing, advising, producing, and serving the customers.

The danger is sharper for specialist firms—companies whose advantage depends less on supplying a standard product and more on diagnosing a customer’s particular problem. These include precision manufacturers that advise on materials and tolerances, specialist software firms that configure systems around a client’s workflow, engineering consultancies that identify risks hidden in a brief, and professional service firms whose value lies in interpreting a client’s situation before proposing a solution. Their expertise is in asking the questions the customer may not yet know to ask, identifying constraints the customer has not recognised, and improving the specification before work begins.

Respond too quickly to every AI-generated request and you compete on price before you have shown that your expertise will add value. AI, in other words, can manufacture a race to quote faster rather than better—and faster is the race a specialist least wants to win, because its value-added lies in helping the customer improve their own value through the expertise they bring to the customer’s job-to-be-done.

### What Meridian Mouldings did.

A company we’ll refer to as Meridian Mouldings, a small, specialist UK manufacturer of precision moulded plastic components for industrial customers, was among the first of the companies we studied or advised to notice the shift. Historically, its inquiries had arrived through referrals, repeat relationships, and trade contacts—buyers who had already identified a manufacturing problem and wanted help solving it. Volume was manageable and most inquiries led to a useful technical conversation.

Then the pattern changed. Meridian began receiving far more RFQs. At first this looked like success: more buyers were finding the company, and the inquiries were neatly written, professionally formatted, and often accompanied by drawings, comparison tables, or short technical summaries. But conversion did not improve. The team spent more hours preparing quotes while fewer of those quotes turned into valuable work.

When the managing director and commercial team reviewed the pipeline, three things stood out. Many inquiries had clearly been sent to a long list of suppliers at once—some openly addressed the wrong supplier by name or named several suppliers in the same message. The requests used generic language that made each job look simpler than it was. And the documents routinely omitted the context needed to quote responsibly: what the part was for, how it would be used, what stress tolerances would be required, whether earlier versions had failed, whether the customer wanted design advice, and whether the buyer was ready for production or merely collecting prices. AI had not produced better demand. It had made low-quality demand easier to generate.

For Meridian, this was costly because quoting was not clerical work. A responsible quote needed engineering input—someone to review the drawing, weigh material choice, consider tooling, assess feasibility, and, often, spot risks in the customer’s own assumptions. A poorly formed RFQ therefore drained time from exactly the people whose expertise the firm most needed to protect.

Meridian’s first move was to measure the problem rather than absorb it. The team reviewed recent inquiries and sorted them into three categories. “Ready to quote” inquiries carried enough technical and commercial information to support a responsible estimate. “Needs clarification” inquiries might become valuable but were missing something important. “Quote fishing” inquiries were broad, generic, or obviously mass-distributed, with little sign that the customer understood the requirement or intended a real technical conversation.

The exercise changed the team’s default. The reflexive response to an RFQ had always been to quote; now it was to qualify. Before anyone prepared an estimate, the inquiry passed through a short qualification screen. Does the customer explain the application? Do we know the operating environment? Is there a drawing or sample? Are the material requirements specified, and do they make sense? Is the expected volume clear? Is the customer asking for advice, replacement, redesign, or production? Do we understand the problem they are trying to solve? Is there evidence this is a serious buying process rather than a broad market scan?

When the answers were there, the inquiry moved quickly to quotation. When they were not, but the opportunity looked promising, Meridian sent a short, polite, technical clarification note explaining that it could quote more accurately once the customer answered a few questions—typically on application, exposure conditions, expected volume, usage stress tolerances, material assumptions, previous failures, and timing.

This did two things at once: It cut wasted quoting time and it produced a better sales conversation. Serious buyers usually answered: some supplied the missing detail, others arranged a short call. Those exchanges tended to surface the real commercial issue. A buyer who had asked for a cheap plastic component turned out to be trying to reduce field failures. One requesting a like-for-like replacement actually needed design-for-manufacture advice, because the original part could not be produced consistently. A third, who had sent the same AI-polished RFQ to several suppliers, realized from Meridian’s questions that the specification was not yet ready to be quoted at all.

Less serious prospects often simply went quiet—and Meridian learned to treat that as a result rather than a loss. A customer unwilling to answer basic application questions was unlikely to value the firm’s expertise; losing them early freed engineering time for work that fitted the company’s strengths.

Meridian paired their screen with a simple triage rule. Inquiries from existing customers, technically complex opportunities, and anything involving failure, redesign, or production scale-up were prioritized. Generic lowest-price requests were deprioritized unless they met clear commercial criteria. The firm did not abandon price-sensitive work; it simply stopped letting poorly qualified RFQs set the rhythm of the business. The last change was internal, and it came down to what the company chose to measure. The monthly sales review had always opened with a single number: how many RFQs came in. It now opened with better questions: How many were worth quoting? How many needed clarification? How many were quote fishing? Which questions helped us spot the serious customers? Rising inquiry volume no longer automatically signaled rising opportunity, and leadership gained a far more accurate read of real demand.

Meridian bought no new software to do any of this. The criteria, the clarification template, and the triage rule were built and run by hand. The discipline lay in the default, not the tooling: in an AI-mediated market, where any buyer can generate and distribute a credible-looking enquiry in minutes, the winning response to a surge of inbound demand is often greater selectivity, not greater speed. You compete not by quoting faster but by qualifying better—protecting expert time, and winning the opportunity to have the technical conversation in which their expertise can be demonstrated.

## The Problem: Interpreting Feedback

As the saying goes, the squeaky wheel gets the grease. When it comes to customers, that means attention goes to the loudest complaints and the most frequent themes in the most profitable segments. But that data is no longer coming quite so directly from the customer.

Increasingly, customer feedback may be AI-written, AI-assisted, or AI-polished. A [peer-reviewed study of hotel reviews](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0306457324002012) used 400 original TripAdvisor reviews to generate 6,000 ChatGPT-paraphrased reviews, showing that AI-paraphrased review text can create new detection challenges because it remains connected to human-written source material while obscuring its origin. Separately, [an analysis of 245,000 reviews](https://originality.ai/blog/ai-generated-reviews-case-study) across Capterra, G2, and TrustRadius estimated that, depending on the site, 10% to 30% of reviews posted after ChatGPT’s launch were likely AI-generated. The latter estimate should be treated cautiously because it comes from a commercial AI-detection provider rather than a peer-reviewed study, but together the two sources point to the same emerging managerial problem: customer feedback is becoming less straightforward to interpret. [Research analyzing over 700,000 reviews](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0969698925001791) shows that AI-generated fake reviews were easier to understand but less specific than authentic reviews, suggesting that polished review language may not always signal genuine customer experience.

The implication for weighting feedback data is uncomfortable. If you only consider the opinions you can see—reviews, complaints, survey responses—not only are you likely evaluating AI-generated content, you could also miss the much larger pool of customers whose choice is based on AI’s description of you in the first place. AI’s framing of your business has become a customer voice, and one most firms have no system for hearing.

### What Ashcombe Manor did.

We recently advised the leadership of a boutique heritage hotel and wedding venue in the UK, which we’ll call Ashcombe Manor. The business depends on customer co-creation in many ways: every wedding is partly designed by the couple, every stay is shaped by guest preferences negotiated through the planning team, and every post-stay review feeds the next prospective guest’s decision.

Like most managers in his position, the marketing director was focusing on reviews from TripAdvisor, Google, and Booking.com, and weighing feedback by volume and emotional intensity. When we audited how the venue was being seen—both in its reviews and in how AI tools described it—we found that: a) while polished, the reviews on the three websites no longer communicated an authentic sense of a given guest’s experience; and b) when a prospective couple asked ChatGPT or Perplexity for “the best wedding venues in \[region\],” Ashcombe Manor appeared in only two of 10 results and was described inaccurately in half of those. An entire layer of customer opinion-formation about the venue was happening in conversations that the venue could not see.

The work Ashcombe Manor undertook was as much about listening differently as listening more. It ran on three tracks. First, they separated reviews by likely degree of AI mediation. The team then developed a simple set of criteria for reading reviews and selecting which to feature on the hotel’s website. Long, fluent passages using stock hospitality phrasing like “a magical experience from start to finish” or “impeccable service in a stunning setting” were flagged as low-signal as they were likely AI-polished—useful for star ratings but not for strategy. However, short or imperfect reviews naming specific staff, specific dishes, or specific moments from the day were flagged as high-signal and discussed in detail at strategy reviews. Themes that recurred only in the high-signal pool, not the polished pool, were treated as the real customer voice.

Second, the Ashcombe Manor team ran regular audits of how AI tools described the property. Every few weeks the team ran a fixed set of prompts through ChatGPT, Claude, and Perplexity such as “best wedding venues in \[region\],” “romantic boutique hotels near \[city\] for a small wedding” and “heritage wedding venues with on-site accommodation.” They logged whether the property appeared, in what position, alongside which competitors and with what description. Each inaccuracy or omission became a specific item to fix—not in the AI (which Ashcombe Manor cannot directly edit) but in the public content the AI was drawing on. When ChatGPT described the venue as a “country house hotel” rather than as a heritage estate with a working farm, the team traced the language back to a particular third-party write-up and prioritized getting more accurate descriptions placed where AI tools would find them.

Based on these findings, Ashcombe Manor rewrote their public content to give AI tools something specific to work with. Generic hospitality language like “charming,” “romantic,” and “attentive staff” gives AI nothing distinctive to say. The team rewrote the venue’s website copy, directory listings, and press materials to replace generic phrases with concrete particulars, naming the estate’s heritage features, the venue’s most famous guest moments, the named chef and his approach, the fact that the hotel sources food from its own working farm, and the specific wedding traditions the venue accommodates. The principle was simple: if your public content sounds like every other venue’s public content, AI tools will describe you like every other venue. Specific content gives the AI something specific to say.

Just as we saw at Meridian, the Ashcombe Manor team did this manually. There was no custom software, no machine learning pipeline, no dedicated analyst. Two managers spent a few hours every two weeks reading reviews against the team’s criteria, running the prompt set through ChatGPT, Claude, and Perplexity, and logging results in a shared spreadsheet. The discipline was in the cadence and the criteria, not in the tooling. This shift recognized that an inaccurate description in a ChatGPT shortlist now carries as much strategic weight as a critical review on TripAdvisor because both shape what prospective customers believe before they make contact.

## The Problem: Keeping Up with Frequent Changes

Most companies still treat strategy co-creation as episodic. They run a strategy offsite, launch a major redesign, or convene a customer advisory board once a year. They then return to their operating mode. Episodic co-creation works when customer expectations change in months. It struggles when expectations shift in weeks—and it fails completely when they shift daily, which is increasingly the case in AI-mediated markets.

The reason is straightforward. When customers research, compare, and form judgements through AI tools that themselves update constantly, the baseline against which they evaluate your business is constantly moving. The same product that delighted a customer in March may underwhelm a prospective customer in November. This is not because the product has changed but because the AI that describes it has been retrained, the competitors it is compared against have updated their content, and the implicit standard for “good” has drifted.

Continuous co-creation does not require enterprise-scale technology. [Many B2B SaaS product teams](https://blog.buildbetter.ai/voice-of-customer-in-2026-how-b2b-product-teams-actually-use-voc/) now use AI to synthesize customer feedback. [Customer feedback platforms](https://www.zonkafeedback.com/blog/customer-feedback-tools) with AI-powered thematic analysis, sentiment detection, and closed-loop workflows now run from $20 to $200 per month for small to mid-market businesses and free tiers are sufficient to get started.

### What Runtime Harbor did.

A company we’ll call Runtime Harbor is a small, early-stage B2B SaaS firm with fewer than 20 employees. It sells developer IT infrastructure tools to engineering teams at growing technology companies. The category is crowded with dozens of comparable offerings ranging from small startups to established platforms. Their customers are technical buyers who increasingly shortlist tools by asking ChatGPT and Perplexity “what are the best \[category\] tools for a series B SaaS company.” They also read AI-summarized reviews on Capterra and G2 and submit support tickets and feature requests that are themselves often drafted with AI assistance.

Runtime Harbor’s traditional product process, which follows quarterly customer interviews, an annual roadmap review, and a major release cycle of six months, cannot keep pace. By the time management sits down for its Q3 planning, the AI tools that describe their category have updated twice. In response, Runtime Harbor has shifted to a continuous co-creation system, running three interlocking loops.

The first loop is continuous customer signal capture. Runtime Harbor consolidates support tickets, sales call recordings, in-app feedback widgets, and review-platform mentions into a single AI-powered feedback tool costing in the range of $100–$200 per month. The tool is designed to extract themes, requests, objections, severity, and business impact from each conversation. This alerts a team when feedback patterns cross thresholds; e.g., when five enterprise accounts mention the same issue within 14 days. Where the product team previously read tickets manually and discussed them monthly, they now employ an AI agent that aggregates all this information and sends a Monday morning digest to the team, informing them of changes in customer voice, ranked by intensity and account weight. The signal has gone from periodic to ambient.

The second loop is continuous AI-representation monitoring. Every two weeks, a designated team member runs a fixed set of prompts through the leading AI tools: “best \[category\] tools for series A startups,” “compare top three \[category\] platforms for a 50-engineer SaaS team,” and “what tools help reduce \[problem\] for an early-stage startup.” The results are logged in a shared sheet to answer: Where does Runtime Harbor appear? How is it described? Which competitors appear alongside it? Where are the inaccuracies? These can be important: in one case, ChatGPT consistently described the firm as suitable only for very small startups, when in fact the firm serves teams of 50 to 200 engineers.

The third loop is between signal and action. Runtime Harbor has a standing process to act within a working week. When the company sees customers requesting a new feature or service, it gets immediately prototyped and released to a small cohort before being approved for general release. And any misrepresentation in AI tools triggers an update to the firm’s website, comparison pages, and third-party listings—the public material AI tools draw on when describing the category. Each change feeds back into the next feedback cycle and the next AI audit, thus closing the loop.

The combined effect is that Runtime Harbor’s strategy is being continuously co-created by three parties: the firm’s product team, the firm’s customers (themselves AI-augmented in how they evaluate, choose, and describe the product), and the AI tools that mediate between them.

### ...

The companies that win in AI-mediated markets will not be those with the largest technology budgets. They will be those that have made customer listening and adaptation continuous. AI has not just helped firms co-create strategy with customers more efficiently—it has changed how strategy is designed. Your customers now research, compare and choose your business with the aid of AI agents and assistants at their side. You need to interpret signals, weigh customer voices, and adapt strategy with AI on your own. Design for this deliberately—define customers as actors of human-AI interaction, weight voices by authenticity rather than polish, and make listening continuous rather than episodic.
