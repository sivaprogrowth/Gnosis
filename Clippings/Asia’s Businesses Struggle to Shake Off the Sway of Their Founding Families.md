A storied Indian conglomerate that owns the country’s biggest outsourcing firm and makes millions of iPhones for Apple is in the middle of a leadership shake-up that has highlighted the challenges facing India’s family-founded behemoths.

Last week Natarajan Chandrasekaran announced he would step down as chairman of [Tata](https://www.wsj.com/market-data/quotes/IN/XBOM/532540) Sons. The highly regarded veteran was the first chair of the company with no ties to the family that founded the group’s first ventures over a century ago. He was widely expected to be reappointed to a third term.

Tata Sons is seen in India as a well-run firm that has moved away from family management toward professional leadership. But experts see the exit of Chandrasekaran as evidence of lingering succession and structural issues that still give the Tata family significant heft in the boardroom.

The group has two power centers: The board of Tata Sons, the group’s principal investment holding vehicle, and Tata Trusts, a group of charities that hold a two-thirds stake in Tata Sons.

For decades, both the Tata Trusts and Tata Sons were headed by a member of the Tata family. In 2012, Ratan Tata stepped away from the chairman role at Tata Sons after two decades at the helm, while remaining chairman of the trust until his death.

Chandrasekaran said the decision to leave in February was because he failed to secure the backing of a board director, whom he didn’t name, despite having the support of two key charities in Tata Trusts.

Business experts say the departure suggests a rift with the family, particularly Noel Tata, the head of Tata Trusts since 2024. “Noel Tata has been in some sense pushing for change, asserting himself,” said Kavil Ramachandran, a professor at the Indian School of Business in Hyderabad.

A representative for Noel Tata didn’t immediately respond to a request for comment.

In a statement last week, Tata Trusts said it would support Tata Sons efforts for an orderly leadership transition.

A committee will look for Chandrasekaran’s successor and is expected to consider veterans of the group’s more than two dozen listed companies. Noel Tata is expected to play a major role in the final selection.

The transition in the boardroom comes at a delicate time for the company. It is contending with heavy losses at Air India and the plane crash that killed 260 people last year. Tata Consultancy Services, the cash cow of the empire, is facing the challenges wrought by artificial intelligence, and production at [Tata Motors’](https://www.wsj.com/market-data/quotes/IN/XBOM/544569) Jaguar Land Rover in the U.K. [was offline for several weeks](https://www.wsj.com/business/earnings/jaguar-land-rover-sales-plunge-following-cyberattack-dbc95bae?mod=article_inline) after a cyberattack last year.

“It isn’t during this kind of crisis that we change the captain,” said Ramachandran, who specializes in family businesses.

The drama at Tata is a reminder of the looming corporate governance challenge facing other family-owned companies in Asia. Morten Bennedsen, a professor at the University of Copenhagen, said much of the family wealth built up in Asia during the decades after World War II is ready to be passed on. And “the more wealth that has to be transferred, the more potential for conflict,” he said.

In South Korea, heavy inheritance taxes have caused family members to invite new investors into their businesses, triggering resistance from relatives, while the property downturn in China has complicated the succession plans of major Hong Kong real-estate families.

The stakes are heightened because in countries like India, South Korea, China and Indonesia, family businesses are large employers that make internal family decisions a public matter. Tata Group is one of the largest private sector employers in the world, with over a million employees.

These firms also drive national industrial policy. Under Chandrasekaran, Tata Sons has invested heavily in semiconductor and battery plants, and it has turned itself into a key part of Apple’s [iPhone](https://www.wsj.com/topics/subject/iphone) assembly operation in India.

In India, the most famous succession drama played out within the Reliance conglomerate after the death of its founder, Dhirubhai Ambani, in 2002. After a bitter battle that saw the then finance minister urge the brothers to settle their differences out of the public view, an asset split was brokered, with Mukesh Ambani, the older brother, walking away with the prized refinery business.

Ambani is now routinely ranked now as the richest man in Asia, while his younger brother is no longer a billionaire.

Even in firms that appeared to have managed the transition to professional leadership, family members can reassert their sway.

Bennedsen said that steering relations between a professional chief executive and the family is inherently fraught. A successful outside manager must manage family members’ expectations and understand “that there is an institution called the family,” he said.

Chandrasekaran’s predecessor, Cyrus Mistry, publicly fumed that some Tata Sons board directors would hold up board meetings to consult with Ratan Tata. He was ousted in 2016, four years into the job. One director at a Tata unit said it was difficult to retain Mistry after he lost the confidence of the family.

Chandrasekaran, who was appointed in 2017, “played the game well with Ratan Tata,” said Ramachandran. “He kept him in the loop.”

But that setup changed two years ago when Ratan died and his half-brother Noel took over Tata Trusts. Since then, business experts say more friction has become apparent within the trusts.

Adding to the pressure at the group, the Tata Sons board faces a push from India’s central bank to go public due to its designation as a systemically important lender, a move that Noel Tata opposes.

The Tata family has long valued its ability to carry out a range of philanthropic activities through the Tata Trusts, funded largely by dividend income from Tata Sons. Chandrasekaran never publicly took a stance on the public listing of Tata Sons.

Mukund Rajan, who worked closely with Ratan Tata over two decades, has argued in his memoir that listing Tata Sons could turn the Trusts into the biggest philanthropic entity in the world.

At the same time, selling off or reducing Tata Trust’s stake would diminish its role—and by extension the family’s—in the firm. Listing Tata Sons could also make it harder for the company to undertake massive capital investments that will pay off only far in the future.

“Those kinds of risks could be unpalatable to many of your investors,” said Rajan. “It might potentially stop you from doing what may actually be a good thing for the group and the country.”
