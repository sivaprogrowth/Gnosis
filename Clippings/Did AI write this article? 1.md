## Perish the thought. But we tracked how artificial intelligence is changing five creative fields

ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE has turned everyone into a creator. With just one prompt, anyone can ask AI to write an article, generate code or create a doctored image of [himself as Jesus](https://www.economist.com/culture/2026/04/17/in-the-ai-propaganda-war-iran-is-winning). This high-volume, low-effort work has fuelled concerns of [“ AI slop”](https://www.economist.com/culture/2025/12/03/and-the-economists-word-of-the-year-for-2025-is) oozing across the internet. But is it a tidal wave or a trickle? To find out, we tracked changes to five fields.

Our first chart considers books. AI chatbots generate text that is not always *The Economist* ’s cup of tea, but it can rival that of [many human writers](https://www.economist.com/culture/2026/03/29/bots-are-often-bad-writers-but-so-are-most-humans). Imke Reimers and Joel Waldfogel, two economists at Cornell University and the University of Minnesota, found that the number of e-books published on Amazon rose sharply after November 2022, when Open AI released Chat GPT -3.5. By the end of 2025, some 300,000 books were released each month, up from around 100,000 before its launch. The researchers ran the books through an AI -detection tool and found that chatbots were largely responsible for the increase.

These writing tools can also serve less recreational purposes. Two other economists, Anand Shah of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and Joshua Levy of the University of Southern California, found that the number of civil lawsuits filed without a lawyer in America doubled to 41,000 between 2023 and 2025. The authors reckon that more people are choosing to file their own suits with the assistance of AI rather than forking out for lawyers—in a sample of 1,600 complaints, 18% of those filed in 2026 included language flagged as AI -generated. These self-filed cases still have the same success rate as before the launch of chatbots, suggesting that AI is helping more people to win claims.

<iframe src="https://infographics.economist.com/2026/20260620_WOC189/" title="" height="auto"></iframe>

Academics are also turning to large language models. The number of papers published each month on arXiv, a scientific preprint server, has risen consistently for decades. But their rejection rate has more than doubled since the beginning of 2023. One recent study found that 57% of papers published in 2025 included language that appeared to be influenced by AI, up from 12% in 2023. AI tools mean that scientists can write and share their ideas more quickly, but some academics warn that it might also be increasing the amount of low-quality papers—or outright nonsense—submitted to journals.

<iframe src="https://infographics.economist.com/2026/20260620_WOC190/" title="" height="auto"></iframe>

Some AI tools are used for a different sort of writing. “Vibe coding” allows someone with no background in coding languages to develop sophisticated software. The number of apps released each month on Apple’s i OS App Store has taken off since 2025, coinciding with the release of coding agents such as Claude Code and Codex. There are now more than 100,000 apps added to the store each month, up from fewer than 50,000 last May.

Our final chart considers the music industry. Deezer, a streaming service, estimates that some 75,000 AI -generated songs are uploaded each day, up from 10,000 in January 2025. AI music now makes up a staggering 44% of all new tracks uploaded to the platform. A survey by Deezer found that 97% of respondents could not hear the difference between AI and man-made music; some artificial tracks have received millions of streams.  
  
Similarly, blind tests have found that people often prefer AI -generated text to human writing (our Bartleby columnist tried [his own experiment](https://www.economist.com/podcasts/2026/01/29/s3-e1-fat-layer-of-humans) and managed to fool some of his colleagues).  
  
But ask most people for their thoughts on AI content and they will probably say they prefer the human touch. (Rest assured, this article was written and edited by people.) Books that appear to be authored by AI have fewer and worse reviews on Amazon. And quality aside, the quantity is itself a problem. Self-filed lawsuits are clogging up the courts; increased journal submissions are straining peer-review systems. From literature to law to music, AI has lowered the skills and effort required for some work. That, in turn, might lower the value of all content.■
