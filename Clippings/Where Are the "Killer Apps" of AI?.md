Most of today’s AI entrepreneurs and social media enthusiasts are young. AI is the first technology revolution they’ve lived through. They don’t remember how unbelievably exciting it was in the late 1970s to get your own computer, instead of struggling to get time on one of the vast machines chugging away in what used to be called “the glass house,” an air-conditioned room where from outside you could watch the nine-track tapes being accessed and the refrigerator-sized disk drives spinning. Input and output was on high-speed teletype printers (no screens) and magnetic tape (and before that on punch cards.) They also mostly don’t remember, as I do, writing assembly-language programs in which you moved data from one computer register to another, specified the arithmetic or logical operations to be performed on it, and then specified where to save the result.

They look at today’s AI and marvel at how virtually anyone can now generate code. They don’t realize that that’s also how it felt when you could use an interpreted programming language like BASIC to write your own applications instead of writing them in Assembler. They also don’t remember when Dan Bricklin used BASIC to write the first version of VisiCalc in 1979. He went into business with Bob Frankston, who rewrote it in 6502 Assembly Language for the Apple II. Dan was able to express the idea in Basic; Bob then wrote a tight, efficient version for widespread distribution. People rushed out to buy computers so they could use VisiCalc, and everyone predicted that the VisiCalc “spreadsheet” would put all the accountants out of work. It was the first program to be referred to as a “killer app.” It wasn’t the democratization of programming itself that was the killer app but what Dan and Bob did with it. In the entrepreneurial explosion that followed, databases, graphics, word processing, and other business applications came out of the glass house and, as Bill Gates put it, “onto every desk and into every home.”

We saw the same leap from astonishment to innovation to adoption with the World Wide Web. When O’Reilly demonstrated [GNN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Network_Navigator), the first commercial website, to people at a trade show in 1993, attendees couldn’t believe that clicking on a link could pop up an image or a document from another computer thousands of miles away. They didn’t understand the difference between the software application (the web browser) from the network-connected documents with clickable hyperlinks that it acted as a frame for. They didn’t understand that [web pages were a new kind of software](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/253671.253687) written in a simple markup language anyone could understand. It didn’t make sense that the web page was a document that in many ways could look and act like a kind of program. And when people did understand, they thought that might be the end of programming.

In 1993, everyone thought that the web browser itself was the killer app, and that Marc Andreessen, the young creator of Mosaic, the most popular web browser, which became Netscape, was going to dethrone Bill Gates as the new king of computing. They didn’t realize that the true killer apps of the web were not the web browser and the web server but applications “up the stack” built using the new programming paradigm the web enabled. Those applications weren’t software as we’d known software before. They were database- and application-backed websites delivering services that were previously impossible. I remember thinking of Amazon as the 2005 equivalent to VisiCalc when a friend told me she had just bought her first computer so she could shop on Amazon. Once again, it wasn’t the new capability itself that was the killer app but what developers did with it.

What makes a killer app? I think that Tom Stoppard nailed it in his play *Rosencrantz and Guildenstern Are Dead*:

> A man breaking his journey between one place and another at a third place of no name, character, population or significance, sees a unicorn cross his path and disappear. That in itself is startling, but there are precedents for mystical encounters of various kinds, or to be less extreme, a choice of persuasions to put it down to fancy; until—“My God,” says a second man, “I must be dreaming, I thought I saw a unicorn.” At which point, a dimension is added that makes the experience as alarming as it will ever be. A third witness, you understand, adds no further dimension but only spreads it thinner, and a fourth thinner still, and the more witnesses there are the thinner it gets and the more reasonable it becomes until it is as thin as reality, the name we give to the common experience.

That is, a killer app is at first a startling window onto an undreamed-of possibility, but one that so changes the paradigm that it rapidly gets taken for granted. In the meantime, everyone tries to make sense of the new world. Usually, their sensemaking is to try to reduce it to what they already know. The breakthrough entrepreneurs see more deeply and go farther.

In 1995 and 1996, almost everyone saw the future as a showdown between Microsoft and Netscape over who would own the web server and the web browser, and whether the web would supplant the desktop as the new platform. Microsoft won the browser wars, but it turned out to matter far less than anyone expected. The value went to the companies building on top of the browser, the ones that figured out what the network made possible.

The current narrative pitting AI model providers against each other sounds a lot like Microsoft versus Netscape to me. It is the platform war, the war for the layer that will become the common experience on which the revolutionary future will be built. ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini are the browsers of this era. That is not a small thing. But the browser wasn’t actually the killer app of the web. Netscape made money for a little while. Amazon and Google and the explosion of other sites were what made the web into something people couldn’t live without.

Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, and the other coding agents may be the BASIC of this era, or like the tools and frameworks that made database-backed web applications possible. They are plumbing. Important plumbing, and plumbing that has to be in place before the future can truly happen. But still just plumbing. Inventors tend to spend decades maturing it, and O’Reilly tends to spend decades teaching people to build it. I suspect that will be just as true this time around.

In short, I’ve started asking myself whether we’ve actually seen the killer app for AI yet. The obvious assumption is that ChatGPT was. It certainly got a LOT of people excited and onboard very, very quickly. But maybe Claude Code was, enabling the breakthrough in agentic coding. Or maybe Hermes or Dots or Muse or some other personal agent will be. But we should take seriously the possibility that the killer app is not yet on the scene. These initial candidates for the killer app look too much like advances in a new enabling platform and not enough like a new generation of applications.

Personal agents may turn out to be a breakthrough category, but right now they look to me like early ecommerce before Amazon got it right.[^1] For Amazon to work, the web had to become secure enough for people to use credit cards online. The apps were waiting on missing mechanisms. For Google’s economic model to kick into gear, pay-per-click advertising had to be invented. I suspect we are in a similar moment waiting for crucial pieces to be dumped on the table so developers can complete their part of the puzzle. For AI agents to act on our behalf in the world, they will need their own equivalents: ways to pay, to prove who they are acting for, to be held accountable, and for the counterparties to settle on the allocation of the value created by the transaction. Ilan Strauss and I have argued in “ [The Missing Mechanisms of the Agentic Economy](https://www.oreilly.com/radar/the-missing-mechanisms-of-the-agentic-economy/) ” that many of those mechanisms don’t exist yet. Maybe the killer apps are waiting on the equivalent of https and pay per click.

*In short, when you listen to the AI conversation and mostly hear about people’s programming pipelines and how much more productive their programmers are, and very little about what they are using that productivity to build, you can be pretty confident that the killer apps haven’t arrived yet.*

## Where are we in the cycle?

Let’s line up the calendars and see if that tells us something useful.

CP/M (Control Program for Microcomputers), the first widespread personal computer operating system, was developed in 1974, and the Altair 8800, the first mass-market personal computer, appeared on the cover of *Popular Electronics* in January 1975. Microsoft released a BASIC interpreter for it in June of that same year. A host of competing personal computer hardware platforms running CP/M were introduced soon thereafter. The Apple II was released in 1977, VisiCalc in October 1979, and dBase II in 1980. The IBM PC came out in August 1981 with Microsoft’s MS-DOS as its operating system. Lotus 1-2-3 (which included spreadsheet, graphing, and simple database features) was released in January 1983 and turned the PC into a business necessity.

In 1984, the revolutionary graphical user interface of the Macintosh was the next step function jump in the PC era. The GUI itself had been invented at Xerox PARC in the 1970s, but it wasn’t till Steve Jobs built his “insanely great” Mac that the world embraced it. Desktop publishing became the killer app of the GUI, with Aldus PageMaker and the Apple LaserWriter in 1985. The PC proper didn’t get the GUI till Windows 3.0 in 1990, but Microsoft’s embrace of it for applications such as Word (1983), Excel (1985), and PowerPoint (1987) as well as for the operating system and programming environment made it, for the time, master of the computing world.

So you can see a rough timeline. Four or five years from the introduction of the first version of what was to become the new platform to the first killer app. (Or two, if you count from the introduction of the Apple II, the platform for which VisiCalc was first written.) Then 10 years of invention, competition, and refinement, followed by fairly rapid consolidation and accumulation of market power.

Let’s do the same timeline for the web. Email and online forums might well have been the first internet killer apps, but they were really holdovers from previous generations of research networks. They were apps that had been evolving since the 1970s, though CompuServe and AOL had begun to bring them to the general public in the ’80s. The world really changed, though, when Tim Berners-Lee first wrote his proposal for what became the World Wide Web in March 1989. It was released to the world in August 1991. This was the new platform and the start of what would become the new programming paradigm.

Not too much happened outside physics labs and a few universities until 1993. (There were only about 200 websites when we published *[The Whole Internet User’s Guide and Catalog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whole_Internet_User%27s_Guide_and_Catalog)* in November 1992.) In 1993, NCSA, the National Center for Supercomputer Applications, released [Mosaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCSA_Mosaic) and we launched [GNN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_Network_Navigator), the first commercial website. Netscape shipped its browser, a follow-on to Mosaic, at the end of 1994. Yahoo! was launched that year too, Amazon, eBay, and the Altavista search engine in 1995, MapQuest in 1996, Google in 1998, and Wikipedia in 2001.

So, again about four years from the release of the platform to the appearance of the first killer app, and another 10 till the consolidation phase. (I see Gmail 2004, Google Maps 2005, Google Docs 2006, and Chrome 2008 as the equivalent of Microsoft’s release of Office on top of Windows as the beginning of the consolidation phase of the web.)

The timeline for mobile is not dissimilar. The iPhone in 2007, Android in 2008. Killer social apps Facebook and Twitter had gotten their start on the web at about the same time, but really came into their own on mobile over the next few years. The “could only have happened on mobile” killer apps of Uber, Lyft, and the on-demand economy didn’t arrive till 2011/2012.

A lot of people seem to think AI is moving way faster than previous technology revolutions. But if you line up the calendars, AI is moving at about the same pace as previous eras. Treat the transformer paper, “ [Attention Is All You Need](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.03762),” published in June 2017, as the AI equivalent of Tim Berners-Lee’s 1989 proposal, and ChatGPT’s first release on November 30, 2022, as the equivalent of Mosaic. The gap from invention to breakout is about the same, five years for the web and five and a half for AI. That’s just getting the platform in place. The AI-native “killer apps” haven’t yet arrived but are due soon. (And please, let’s avoid the jokes that the “killer apps” of AI first showed up in the Russia-Ukraine drone war. That’s true, but not on the direct line of my argument here.)

On what seems to be a fairly consistent timeline, where are we today, then? October 2026 corresponds to roughly the spring of 1997. Amazon was about to go public. Hotmail was a year old. Google was still a research project at Stanford called BackRub. SixDegrees had kicked off social networking, but Facebook was still seven years off. Napster was still two years away.

Think of all those amazing application categories that had been pioneered, though. Search, ecommerce, email, and social networking were all in their infancy, but they were out there, delivering real value. I’m not sure that I see the equivalent time to value in AI. AI didn’t even get to Claude Code, its Microsoft Basic equivalent, till 2025! By some measures, AI is moving more slowly rather than more quickly. There’s more change to digest, and developers are still struggling with the tooling and developing the new knowledge that is needed.

## Why so slow, with so much money?

The four largest hyperscalers are on track to spend [roughly $700 billion](https://om.co/2026/04/30/what-i-learned-about-hyperscalers-ai-spend/) in capital expenditure this year, nearly double what they spent in 2025. That is orders of magnitude more capital than went into the web in the 1990s. If capital and capability were the limiting factors, you’d think we should be well ahead of the earlier calendar, not behind.

I suspect that at least part of the answer is that [Silicon Valley’s quest for premature monopoly](https://web.archive.org/web/20190205184624/https://qz.com/1540608/the-problem-with-silicon-valleys-obsession-with-blitzscaling-growth/) may be making it less efficient at finding breakthroughs rather than more. Starting with the era of Uber and Lyft, massive VCs have canonized the winners at the beginning of the race, crowding out alternate technologies and business models. That may be happening here. But it’s also just in the nature of bubbles, which depend on groupthink to flourish.

And part of that groupthink is about where the value is going to be captured, rather than where it is going to be created. That is almost always backward looking. During the railroad boom, capital was pouring into building the tracks, and the new business models (think Montgomery Ward and Sears) that railroad enabled didn’t come till later. The dot-com bubble was far less capital intensive than people think. The telecom buildout that ran alongside it was where the money got spent. US carriers spent about $97 billion on capital in 2000 alone.

By 2002, according to an [FDIC analysis](https://www.fdic.gov/bank/analytical/bank-trends/bt0205.pdf), only 2% to 5% of the fiber in the ground was actually lit. More than 60 telecom companies went bankrupt. Once the data centers and dark fiber of the telecom bubble could be bought for pennies on the dollar, they enabled a generation of new applications like streaming video. YouTube was founded in 2005, after the crash, on cheap bandwidth that someone else had paid for before they went out of business. There were some visionary companies (think Google, Amazon) that had been started before the abundance arrived, but many more were founded after the bubble burst.

Carlota Perez’s book *[Technological Revolutions and Financial Capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technological_Revolutions_and_Financial_Capital)* documented this pattern across every technological revolution since the canal era. Financial capital pours into the installation of the new infrastructure, a bubble forms and bursts, and the deployment period that follows, when the technology actually reshapes the economy, is built by different people on top of what the crash left behind. If that is where we are in the cycle, then the absence of killer apps is not a puzzle at all.

The current data center buildout and the massive investments in frontier AI may not be the future. They are the infrastructure on which the future will be built. We have to ask ourselves what will we build once competition and overshoot and the bursting of the data center bubble make intelligence infrastructure cheap.

## Help me fill in the picture

I see several possibilities. Either we are still early and the killer apps simply haven’t shown up yet, the way Google hadn’t shown up in 1997. Or AI is going more slowly than its boosters claim, in spite of all the capital, because capital and model capability are not the limiting factors. But it’s also possible that the killer apps are here, and I’m not hearing about them because the AI conversation is so focused on AI capability and nobody is telling the story of what they are accomplishing with it beyond building better developer tools.

I would like to know which. We all recognize a killer app when we see one. Amazon was a bookstore with more books than any physical bookstore could hold. Wikipedia was an encyclopedia written entirely by volunteers and was updated in real time within hours and sometimes even minutes of major events. Google Maps meant you always knew where you were and how to get anywhere else. If you know of an AI *application*, beyond the chatbot or agentic coding environment itself, that changes what ordinary people can do on that scale, or a business that does something an order of magnitude cheaper or better than the one it replaces, I want to hear about it.
