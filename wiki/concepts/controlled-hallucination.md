# Controlled Hallucination

> The world we experience as "out there" is actually a reconstruction of reality that is built inside our heads. It's an act of creation by the storytelling brain.
> — Will Storr, *The Science of Storytelling*

The brain does not see, hear, or know the world. It receives sparse sensory signal — lightwaves, pressure changes, chemical traces — and constructs a model of reality so convincing that we mistake the model for the territory. That model is the **controlled hallucination**. We live inside it from birth and have no access to anything outside it. There is no sound "out there"; sound is the brain's interpretation of pressure changes. There is no colour "out there"; colour is the brain's labelling of wavelengths. The same applies to people, products, brands, status, meaning — all are model-objects assembled from inputs.

## The mechanism

The senses pick up clues; those clues are converted into electrical signals; the brain runs a best-guess simulation built on the signals plus prior models. The simulation has gaps the brain fills in automatically — which is why iconic experiments (counting basketball passes while a gorilla walks through the frame) reveal that we miss things that are physically present, and why we "see" things that aren't there.

The model is **honed to survival needs**, not truth. We perceive the narrow band of reality our ancestors needed to act on. Everything else is invisible.

## Why story works

Story works because it hijacks the same machinery. The neural processes that build the model of "the room I'm sitting in" are the processes that build the model of "the world of *Anna Karenina*". From the brain's point of view there is no clean separation. A well-told story isn't experienced as "information about a fictional world" — it's experienced as a temporary additional hallucination, run on the same hardware, often more vivid than what's nominally outside the window.

This is why:

- **Specific sensory detail beats abstraction.** "A cabbagey brown sock" gives the model two pairs of inputs to assemble (smell + colour). "A smelly sock" gives one. The richer the assembly material, the more present the hallucination.
- **Active voice beats passive.** Word order controls assembly order. "Jane kissed her Dad" lets the brain place Jane, then watch her move. "Dad was kissed by Jane" forces a re-render mid-sentence.
- **Fantasy worldbuilding works at all.** Naming a fictional planet, war, technology, or framework gives the brain hooks to assemble around — even when the user knows the referent is made up.

## Why this matters for marketing and brand

The "brand-in-the-customer's-head" is not a description of the company — it is a model the customer constructs from every signal they receive. The customer's brand is a controlled hallucination, and the company is one of several inputs (alongside reviews, competitors, third-party media, peer mentions, and AI-engine summaries). Two consequences:

1. **You cannot directly install a brand.** You can only adjust the model's input mix. This is why [[earned-media-bias]] matters disproportionately — the third-party signal weighs heavily in the model's assembly.
2. **Specific detail in marketing copy beats abstraction.** "Helped a 12-person SaaS team add $4.2M in pipeline in 90 days" assembles into a vivid model; "drove growth for B2B clients" assembles into nothing.
