# Information Gap Curiosity

> Brains seem to become spontaneously curious when presented with an "information set" they realise is incomplete. "There is a natural inclination to resolve information gaps."
> — George Loewenstein, summarised in Will Storr, *The Science of Storytelling*

Curiosity is not a stable trait, a vague interest, or a personality variable. It is a specific cognitive reflex: the brain detects that an information set it has been shown is incomplete, and it cannot rest until the gap is closed. The gap is the engine. The content of the gap is secondary — what matters is that the recipient can *see* that something is missing.

This is the mechanism behind every effective hook, headline, cold-email subject line, trailer, cliffhanger, and serialised piece of content.

## The four routes (Loewenstein)

1. **Pose a question or present a puzzle.** "Why do most B2B SaaS companies miss their first AI-search ranking by 18 months?" — the question itself opens the gap.
2. **Expose to a sequence of events with an anticipated but unknown resolution.** Procedurals, mystery novels, "we tried X for 30 days" content. The reader knows there *will* be an outcome; they just don't know what it is.
3. **Surprise with information that suggests a hidden whole.** "73% of the AI citations our client received came from sites we had never heard of." The fragment implies a larger pattern the reader is now obligated to learn.
4. **Remind the subject of something they once knew but forgot.** "You already know your competitor outranks you on the queries that matter — but you've been measuring the wrong queries." Returns access to a partially-cached memory.

## Why this beats "interesting content"

Material described as "interesting" is unreliable as a hook because *interesting* is a property of the recipient's state. Information gaps are mechanical — they trigger the curiosity reflex regardless of whether the recipient was previously interested in the topic. The brain treats the gap as an itch it cannot decline to scratch.

This is why headlines like *"What we learned after spending $40k on a botched migration"* outperform *"Migration best practices"* — the former opens a gap (what specifically went wrong, in detail), the latter announces a content category.

## Practical application

For **cold outreach**: the subject line should make the reader perceive an information set they didn't know was incomplete until you mentioned it. ("Your three GEO citations last week were from sites you don't currently track.")

For **content**: the first paragraph should make the reader feel they walked into the middle of a sentence, not the beginning of an article. They will read on to close the gap.

For **product positioning**: the gap is the prospect's discovery that the metric they have been optimising is measuring the wrong thing. ([[mirage-pmf]] works this way — it opens a gap between the revenue dashboard and what the dashboard is actually measuring.)

## Anti-pattern: closing the gap in the headline

The standard mistake is to deliver the resolution in the subject line or first sentence. "We helped a client improve AI visibility by 240%" — the gap is closed before it opens. No reflex fires; nothing pulls the reader through.

The skilled version names the *territory* of the gap without resolving it: "We rebuilt one client's AI visibility around three citations they didn't know existed."
