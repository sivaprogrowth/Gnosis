# Sacred Flaw Approach

> The "Sacred Flaw Approach" is a character-first process, an attempt to create a story that mimics the various ways a brain creates a life, and which therefore feels true and fresh, and comes pre-loaded with conflict.
> — Will Storr, *The Science of Storytelling*

Storr's headline craft framework. Most popular storytelling advice starts with plot ("Inciting incident, rising action…") or with archetype ("the hero's journey…"). The Sacred Flaw Approach inverts both. It starts with **one specific, foundational, partly-true-but-distorting belief** the protagonist holds about reality, and derives everything else — values, behaviours, desires, allies, antagonists, choices, plot — from there.

The flaw is **sacred** in two senses. First, the protagonist treats it as untouchable — it is the thing they would not allow you to question if you asked them directly. Second, it is the load-bearing piece of their identity; if it cracks, the whole self has to be rebuilt, which is exactly what story is *for*. (Storr borrows Jonathan Haidt's "follow the sacredness — and you will find rampant irrationality" to point at the diagnostic.)

## The method

1. **Identify the protagonist's foundational flawed belief.** Not a personality trait. A specific, hard-edged proposition about how reality works. Examples: *"If I'm in control, nothing bad will happen."* *"Showing weakness is the worst thing a person can do."* *"Money is the only honest measure of worth."*
2. **Derive their [[theory-of-control]] from the belief.** How does someone who holds this belief navigate the world? What do they invest in? What do they avoid?
3. **Derive the [[wants-vs-needs|want and the need]].** The conscious *want* is whatever the flawed belief tells them will solve their problem. The unconscious *need* is whatever would actually solve it — almost always something the sacred flaw cannot allow them to see.
4. **Build the plot as a series of escalating tests of the flaw.** Each scene is engineered to crack the belief a little more, until the protagonist cannot avoid choosing between defending the flaw and becoming a different person.
5. **The climax is the moment of choice.** Story endings are essentially this single move — keep the sacred flaw and become tragic, or surrender it and become someone new.

## Why this matters for brand and founder positioning

Most B2B brands describe themselves around their *strengths*. The Sacred Flaw lens suggests the opposite: a memorable brand is built around the **one foundational belief** the founder holds that most people in the category do not. The belief drives every product, marketing, and hiring choice — and if it ever cracks, the brand has to be rebuilt.

Examples in adjacent space:

- 37signals' sacred flaw: *"Bigger is worse."* Drives Basecamp's small-team focus, ICE-cold attitude toward enterprise sales, "It Doesn't Have To Be Crazy at Work" positioning.
- Patagonia's sacred flaw: *"Profit must serve the planet."* Drives every supply chain, marketing, and ownership decision, including selling the company to a trust.
- A useful diagnostic question for a founder: **"What is the one thing in your category that everyone else believes is true, that you think is wrong?"** The answer is usually the sacred flaw — if it is genuinely held, it is also the most defensible positioning the brand has.

For [[progrowth]], a candidate sacred flaw: *"Marketing services scale through AI leverage, not through hiring more humans."* Every operational and pricing decision follows from defending that belief. The day the belief stops being defensible (or stops being differentiating) is the day the brand has to be reauthored.

## Why this method holds up

Plot-first writing produces stories where characters behave as the plot requires. Character-first writing — especially flaw-first — produces stories where the plot *emerges* from who the people are. The reader cannot quite tell why, but the second kind feels true and the first kind feels mechanical. The same asymmetry applies to brands: positioning that emerges from a load-bearing belief feels like conviction; positioning that emerges from a competitive matrix feels like positioning.
