# The cost of saying yes is hidden

Saying no to a paid opportunity has a clear, quantifiable cost, but saying yes costs creative work, household rhythm, and happiness that are hard to measure and show up later.

> “Opportunity costs are sometimes clear, but often not clear. If I
> get offered to do a speaking gig, whatever they’re offering [to
> pay] is the opportunity cost of saying no. The opportunity cost
> of saying yes is whatever creative work I might’ve done had I
> stayed home, and then also intangibles like the rhythm of our
> households, my personal happiness, how easy things are. So the
> downside, in one sense, is very quantifiable, and the upside, in
> the other case, is very hard to quantify. And in some cases, the
> consequences of it are quite lagging.”
>
> — Ryan Holiday, The Knowledge Project Podcast

> Why are otherwise smart people trying to do too many things? The answer might surprise you. Saying no to something is hard because you immediately see what you might lose: the money, the connection, the possibility that something might come of it. So we say yes ... because the cost is hidden. Every yes quietly steals time from the most important thing you're already working on.
>
> — [[shane-parrish|Shane Parrish]]
