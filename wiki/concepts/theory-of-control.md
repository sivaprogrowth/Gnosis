# Theory of Control

> When designing a character, it's often useful to think of them in terms of their theory of control. How have they learned to control the world?
> — Will Storr, *The Science of Storytelling*

Every functioning human carries a learned model of how the world works that lets them get what they want. This model — their **theory of control** — was assembled over a lifetime from the rewards and punishments their environment delivered. It tells them who has power, what to say to whom, when to push and when to retreat, what to invest in, what to ignore. Most of the time it works well enough that they don't notice they have one.

The theory of control is the operating layer that sits beneath conscious belief. People don't usually know their own theory; they live inside it the way a fish lives inside water.

## Why this is the engine of drama

Story works because the protagonist's theory of control breaks. The **ignition point** is the moment unexpected change strikes and the old theory fails to predict the world. Everything that follows is the protagonist negotiating between the old theory (which is comfortable, familiar, and now wrong) and whatever the new theory will need to be.

This is also why **plots derive from character, not the other way around**. A given event — say, the boss makes an unreasonable demand — produces wildly different stories depending on which character is in the chair. The conscientious one builds a spreadsheet; the disagreeable one quits on principle; the extrovert calls eight friends for advice; the neurotic one spirals into rumination. The event is the same. The plot is whatever the theory of control does with it.

## Why this matters for customer research and positioning

The single most useful question to ask a B2B buyer is some version of *what is your current theory of control over this problem?*

- What do you currently believe makes the difference?
- Where do you currently put your time when you want to move this metric?
- Who do you currently trust to tell you the truth about it?
- What have you tried that didn't work, and what did you conclude from that?

Their answers reveal the theory. The theory then tells you:

1. **What they will buy.** Buyers buy from inside their theory of control. A founder who believes the bottleneck is "we need more leads" will buy a lead-gen tool, even if the actual bottleneck is positioning. A challenger product that requires the buyer to revise their theory before purchase will lose to an incumbent that fits the current theory — *until* the buyer hits their own ignition point.
2. **How to write to them.** Content aimed at a buyer's current theory of control feels like clarity. Content aimed at the theory you wish they had feels like sales. The former gets shared; the latter gets ignored.
3. **When they're convertible.** Buyers are most open to changing tools, agencies, and frameworks immediately after their theory of control cracks (a missed quarter, a fired CMO, a competitor leaping ahead in AI search). The ignition point is the only moment the old theory is up for negotiation.

## Relationship to wants-vs-needs

The theory of control generates the *want* — what the protagonist (or buyer) consciously believes will solve their problem. The **[[wants-vs-needs|need]]** is what would actually solve it, which is almost always something the current theory of control can't see. Closing the gap is what the second half of any story (or any worthwhile customer engagement) is for.
