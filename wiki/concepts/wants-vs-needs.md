# Wants vs Needs

> The conscious and unconscious desires of a multidimensional protagonist contradict each other. What he believes he wants is the antithesis of what he actually but unwittingly needs.
> — Will Storr, *The Science of Storytelling*

A multidimensional character — and a multidimensional buyer — does not have a single coherent goal. They have two, and they contradict each other.

- The **want** is conscious. It is what the character (or buyer) will tell you they're after if you ask them directly. It is internally consistent with their [[theory-of-control]] — it is the thing their current model of the world says will solve their problem.
- The **need** is unconscious. It is what would actually solve the problem. The character cannot see it, because seeing it would require revising the theory of control they have built their identity around. (See [[sacred-flaw-approach]] — the want is the sacred flaw's official position; the need is what's true on the other side of giving it up.)

Stories work because the plot exposes the gap. Worthwhile B2B engagements work the same way.

## Diagnosing the gap

The diagnostic question: **if the want were perfectly delivered, would the underlying problem actually be solved?**

If the answer is "yes" — the want and the need are aligned. This is a simple, well-defined transaction. Usually low-value, often commoditised.

If the answer is "no, the surface problem would recede for a quarter and come back" — the want and the need are misaligned. The buyer is asking for *more of the wrong thing*. This is where high-leverage consulting and positioning lives.

Concrete pattern in B2B marketing:

| What the buyer says they want | What they actually need |
|---|---|
| "More leads" | A different ICP, or better qualification |
| "A redesigned website" | A redefined positioning |
| "Better SEO" | A point of view the market wants to cite |
| "More content" | One piece of content worth citing |
| "Marketing automation" | A reason the customer would respond at all |
| "A faster agency" | An agency that pushes back on the brief |

Each row is a story in miniature: the want is internally consistent with the buyer's current theory of control; the need requires the buyer to revise the theory before they can act on it.

## Why naming the gap is not the same as closing it

A common mistake in challenger-vendor positioning is to *tell* the prospect about the gap up front. "You think you need more leads; you actually need better positioning." This rarely works, because telling someone their theory of control is wrong triggers defence, not curiosity. (See Storr on smart people: *"Smart people are mostly better at finding ways to prove they're right and tend to be no better at detecting their wrongness."*)

The skilled version lets the prospect arrive at the gap themselves. This typically requires:

1. **Engaging with the want at face value** for long enough to earn the right to question it. Run the lead-gen audit they asked for. Then surface the data that makes the want unsustainable in its current form.
2. **Letting an ignition point do the work.** The buyer's old theory has to crack — usually because the data they trusted stops moving, or a peer does the harder thing and wins. The vendor's job is to be present and credible when the crack happens, not to cause it.
3. **Naming the need only after the want has visibly failed.** Then it lands as obvious in retrospect, which is the highest form of consultative work.

## Relationship to jobs-to-be-done

The wants-vs-needs frame is a cousin of jobs-to-be-done, but more dramatic. JTBD asks *what is the customer hiring this product to do?* — a useful, mostly conscious-level question. Wants-vs-needs adds the layer underneath: *which of the customer's two jobs (conscious want vs unconscious need) is the product addressing, and is the customer ready to see the gap?* The same product might be hired for either job by different buyers; the marketing motion that serves a want-buyer is fundamentally different from the one that serves a need-buyer.
