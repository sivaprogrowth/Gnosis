## Abstract

This article draws a sharp distinction between two meanings of the word "mission": a tactical, finite goal (mission with a small *m*) and a high-level, enduring organizational aspiration (mission with a capital *M*). Using the recorded 1962 White House argument between President John F. Kennedy and NASA administrator James Webb as its central case study, the piece shows how Kennedy treated the moon landing as the entirety of NASA's mission, while Webb insisted it was merely one program within a broader mandate of "preeminence in space." The article traces the consequences of Kennedy's framing: once Apollo 11 succeeded in 1969, NASA lost its sense of purpose and entered what historian John Logsdon calls a "four-decade identity crisis." The core lesson for organizational leaders is that a BHAG — however bold — cannot substitute for a durable mission that continuously pulls an organization forward.

## TL;DR

- A goal, even a "big, hairy, audacious" one, is finite and achievable; an organizational mission is an enduring aspiration that remains perpetually ahead.
- NASA's post-Apollo identity crisis illustrates what happens when an institution conflates a single landmark goal with its entire mission.
- Leaders who mistake a BHAG for a mission risk leaving their organizations rudderless once the goal is accomplished.

## Key claims

- There are two distinct types of "mission": a short-term, achievable goal (small *m*) and a long-term organizational aspiration (capital *M*).
- Kennedy framed NASA's mission entirely around beating the Soviets to the moon — a finite Cold War objective — while Webb argued for the broader mission of US "preeminence in space."
- Webb's concern was that a single-goal focus would leave NASA without a sustaining purpose after the moon landing was achieved.
- The two men resolved their dispute by converging: Kennedy broadened his rhetoric to encompass "first in every aspect of space," while Webb prioritised the lunar programme as directed.
- After Apollo 11 (20 July 1969), NASA's budget was cut under President Johnson and planned lunar missions were cancelled under President Nixon, vindicating Webb's fears.
- Historian John Logsdon concludes that "the impact of Apollo on the evolution of the U.S. space program has on balance been negative" because NASA became a one-program agency unable to reframe its mission.
- The article's core prescription: you can achieve a goal and put it behind you, but a mission is always up ahead and just beyond your reach.

## Key passages

> "Everything that we do ought to really be tied into getting onto the moon ahead of the Russians," he said. In this view, NASA's mission was what could now be called a BHAG—an acronym coined 30 years later by Jim Collins and Jerry Porras, authors of *Built to Last*, for a 'big, hairy, audacious goal.'

> "Why can't it be tied to preeminence in space?" replied Webb. For him, the science, the rockets and other technology, the astronauts and engineers, and the fast-emerging aerospace industry needed to reach the moon were part and parcel of a national space capability.

> Webb believed that the agency needed a mission with a large *M*—an organizational objective of "preeminence in space" that it could strive to fulfill over the long term. Achieving this mission required a variety of programs in addition to the lunar project.

> "NASA during the second half of the 1960s became what James Webb had feared, a one-program agency," writes Logsdon in *John F. Kennedy and the Race to the Moon*.

> "With the White House rejection of ambitious post-Apollo space goals," Logsdon continues, "NASA entered a four-decade identity crisis from which it has yet to emerge."

> Don't mistake a goal—no matter how big, hairy, and audacious—for a compelling organizational mission. You can achieve a goal and put it behind you, but a mission is always up ahead and just beyond your reach.
