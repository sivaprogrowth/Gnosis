## Abstract

This article from *The Economist* examines why software developers have adopted AI far more rapidly than any other white-collar profession, and whether that lead can be matched by workers in law, finance, and customer service. It argues that covering the cost of the current data-centre build-out will require AI revenues to reach $2.5 trillion per year by the end of the decade—roughly 17× current levels—making the pace of adoption in non-coding domains a critical economic question. Four structural factors explain coding's outlier status: the abundance of open-source training data, the ease of validating AI-generated code through automated tests, the digital availability of information needed to complete programming tasks, and the inherent willingness of software engineers to experiment with new tools. The article concludes that while law, finance, and customer-service AI are all growing, these structural advantages will be difficult to replicate.

## TL;DR

- Coding is by far the dominant AI use case today, accounting for over half of [[companies/anthropic|Anthropic]] and [[companies/openai|OpenAI]]'s combined annual recurring revenue.
- Four structural factors—training data availability, output testability, digital information access, and engineer culture—give coding a durable edge over other AI applications.
- Reaching the $2.5 trillion annual revenue needed to justify the AI infrastructure build-out requires rapid AI adoption well beyond software development.

## Key claims

- Four-fifths of software developers report using an AI coding tool, per Stack Overflow's survey data.
- The combined annual recurring revenue of four AI-coding startups (Cognition, Cursor, Lovable, Replit) grew from ~$800m in June 2025 to $6bn by mid-2026.
- [[companies/anthropic|Anthropic]] and [[companies/openai|OpenAI]] derive over half their combined annual recurring revenue from coding applications.
- AI has diffused faster in industries with a higher share of software-engineer employment, consistent with bottom-up, engineer-driven adoption.
- Open-source code constitutes almost a fifth of training data for some models, giving coding tools an unmatched data advantage over legal or customer-service AI.
- Automated software testing provides a built-in feedback loop that improves AI coding models and catches errors—a mechanism that is hard to replicate in law or finance.
- AI legal tools (Harvey, Clio, Legora) doubled combined annual recurring revenue in one year to $1bn; AI customer-service startup [[companies/sierra|Sierra]] reached $200m ARR by June 2026.
- Call-centre agents and lawyers face top-down deployment constraints and regulatory guardrails that slow the organic, bottom-up adoption pattern seen among engineers.

## Key passages

> "To justify their colossal outlays on data centres, America's cloud giants, and AI labs such as Anthropic and Open AI, need adoption of the technology to continue spreading rapidly. A back-of-the-envelope calculation by *The Economist* finds that covering the cost of the data-centre build-out will require total annual revenue from the use of AI to reach $2.5trn per year by the end of the decade, up from around $150bn currently."

> "SemiAnalysis, a research firm, reckons that for Anthropic and Open AI coding now accounts for over half their combined annual recurring revenue—a trendy measure of sales that extrapolates from the most recent month."

> "Part of why AI has proved so successful as a programming buddy is that huge amounts of open-source code are published on the internet. It has thus made its way into models, accounting for almost a fifth of all training data in some cases. The same volume of publicly available content simply does not exist for customer complaints or contract negotiations."

> "Code generated by AI is also relatively easy to validate. Software tests are a routine part of engineering: one piece of code checks another. That increases the likelihood that any errors in AI-generated code are caught before they wreak havoc."

> "Being both nerdy and accustomed to change (given that programming languages are updated every few years), they are natural adopters of new technology. Much of the adoption of AI coding has thus happened in a bottom-up way, with individual engineers discovering and experimenting with the tools of their own accord, notes Anish Acharya of Andreessen Horowitz."

> "Call-centre agents, by contrast, typically have neither the incentive nor the freedom to experiment on the job, and tend to wait for technologies to be deployed top-down. Lawyers and finance workers must also tread carefully to avoid falling foul of the many regulations that govern their occupations."
