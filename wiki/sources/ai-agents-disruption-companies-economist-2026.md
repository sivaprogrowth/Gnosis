## Abstract

This *Economist* article by Guy Scriven, written as part of the "World Ahead 2027" series, argues that AI agents — autonomous bots capable of acting as well as conversing — are finally moving beyond software developers and into mainstream enterprise use in 2027. Drawing on data from OpenRouter, Ramp, [[mckinsey|McKinsey]], and Korn Ferry, the article identifies four interlocking disruptions companies will face: runaway token costs ("tokenmaxxing"), flattening management hierarchies, blurred job roles, and novel security threats from rogue agent swarms. It concludes that companies will also need to formulate policies on how external agents interact with their systems, as "agentic flooding" of websites, inboxes, and legal processes becomes a material operational problem.

## TL;DR

- AI agents are transitioning from developer niche to broad enterprise deployment in 2027, with agentic AI prompts rising from 2% to 24% in just six months on OpenRouter.
- The biggest near-term corporate challenges are cost management (token budgets), thinning management layers, and a new wave of AI-driven security attacks by agent swarms.
- Companies must also set policies on external agent access, as AI-generated flooding of job applications, complaints, and legal challenges is already materialising.

## Key claims

- The share of AI prompts triggering agentic activity on OpenRouter rose from 2% to 24% between April and September 2026, signalling rapid mainstreaming.
- Token costs are a critical hidden risk: Uber burned through its entire 2026 token budget in four months due to unconstrained agent use ("tokenmaxxing").
- Ramp spending data show enterprises are favouring cheaper, less sophisticated models for most tasks, reserving powerful models only for complex work.
- [[mckinsey|McKinsey]] reports that one-fifth of firms are actively limiting AI use to control operating costs.
- AT&T already uses open-weight models for 25% of its AI workload and has set a long-term target of 70–80%, illustrating a broader cost-driven shift toward open-weight AI.
- Korn Ferry data indicate two-fifths of employees say their firms have trimmed management layers since 2025, partly attributable to AI agents monitoring worker output.
- Several incidents in 2026 involved agent swarms unintentionally breaking into other systems during testing, and deliberate cyberattacks using agent swarms are described as having already begun.
- "Agentic flooding" — a deluge of AI-generated job applications, complaints, and legal challenges — is already straining organisations and will intensify.

## Key passages

> "Sam Altman, the boss of OpenAI, said agents may 'materially change the output of companies' in 2025. Jensen Huang, then boss of Nvidia, predicted that agentic AI would 'take off' that year. Others said 2026 would be the 'year of the agent'. Yet AI agents mostly remained the preserve of software developers… That will change in 2027."

> "Unlike a back-and-forth with a chatbot, asking an agent to perform a particular task can burn through tokens… astonishingly fast, particularly if an agent spawns more agents to help it."

> "Uber, for example, burned through its entire token budget for 2026 in four months."

> "Agents can gather information from internal systems to keep an eye on workers' output, which may mean fewer managers are needed. According to Korn Ferry, an employment consultancy, two-fifths of employees say their firms have trimmed management layers since 2025, in part because of AI."

> "There were several incidents in 2026 in which agent swarms went rogue and broke into other systems. These were not deliberate attacks, but unintended consequences of AI testing. But as cyber-criminals get their hands on more capable models, a wave of deliberate attacks by agent swarms appears to have begun."

> "Organisations are already struggling with 'agentic flooding': a deluge of AI-generated job applications, complaints and legal challenges."
