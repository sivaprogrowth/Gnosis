## Abstract

This *Economist* article examines how AI-assisted work risks moving beyond useful "cognitive offloading" into full "cognitive surrender" — a term coined by Steven Shaw and Gideon Nave of the Wharton School — whereby people stop thinking critically and simply accept AI outputs, including erroneous ones. Drawing on studies of calculators, GPS navigation, the "Google effect," and new AI-specific experiments, the piece documents a consistent pattern: delegating cognition to technology carries real costs to recall, spatial reasoning, and error-detection. The article asks what managers should do in response, and offers three evidence-backed interventions: hiring for high "need for cognition," using monetary incentives and real-time feedback, and engineering AI-free periods to preserve independent thinking.

## TL;DR

- AI amplifies existing "cognitive offloading" trade-offs to the point of "cognitive surrender," where users uncritically accept even obviously wrong AI outputs.
- People using AI fed wrong answers performed *worse* than a control group relying solely on their own judgment — meaning AI dependence can be net-negative.
- Managers can counter this with targeted hiring, incentive design, real-time feedback, and structured AI-free practice periods.

## Key claims

- "Cognitive surrender" (Shaw & Nave, Wharton) describes a state where AI's broad capability and confident persona leads users to wholesale capitulation of independent judgment, not just delegation of specific tasks.
- In Shaw & Nave's experiment, participants given AI with deliberately incorrect answers performed significantly worse than a control group using no AI at all — demonstrating that uncritical AI reliance can be net-harmful.
- Prior analogue technologies show the same pattern: GPS heavy-users show worse spatial memory (Dahmani & Bohbot), and the "Google effect" shows that expecting to find information online reduces recall of that information.
- A 2019 study by Mark LaCour (University of Louisiana at Lafayette) found that students using calculators programmed with wrong answers rarely questioned even patently absurd results.
- Workers with high "need for cognition" are somewhat — but not entirely — protected against cognitive surrender, suggesting personality-based hiring as a partial mitigation.
- Monetary rewards and in-test correctness feedback caused AI users to override incorrect model outputs more often, though they still underperformed people who relied on their own judgment.
- A study by Stefanos Poulidis (INSEAD) found that chess students with on-demand AI assistance achieved less than half the performance gains of those whose AI access was limited and automatic — suggesting that user-controlled access is worse than structured, rationed access.
- At present, most managers are focused on driving AI adoption rather than guarding against its cognitive side-effects, even though critical thinking remains a valued and necessary capability.

## Key passages

> AI supercharges these trade-offs. Handing specific tasks to models will often make sense: they are much better than humans at many things. But AI's range of capabilities, allied to a convenient conversational interface and a seductively confident persona, raises the prospect less of delegation than of wholesale capitulation. Hence "cognitive surrender", a term coined by Steven Shaw of the Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania in a recent paper written with his colleague, Gideon Nave.

> When the model gave accurate responses, the people using it outperformed a control group of people relying on their own brainpower. When the AI gave the wrong answers, the people using it did much worse than the control group. In other words, people stopped thinking for themselves.

> People with high "need for cognition" (yes, dear reader, that means you) are somewhat, though not entirely, protected against the risk of cognitive surrender, says Mr Shaw.

> The students who had on-demand access achieved less than half the performance gains of those who had no say over when they got help. Offloading is fine. Giving up is another matter.

> The "Google effect" refers to a research finding that people have worse recall of information they expect to be able to find online.
