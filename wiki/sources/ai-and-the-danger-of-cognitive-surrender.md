## Abstract

This *Economist* article examines how AI extends the well-documented phenomenon of "cognitive offloading" — delegating mental tasks to technology — into territory the authors call "cognitive surrender," where workers stop exercising independent judgment altogether. Drawing on research by Steven Shaw and Gideon Nave of the Wharton School, as well as earlier studies on calculators, GPS, and the "Google effect," the piece shows that when AI gives wrong answers, users who rely on it perform significantly worse than those relying on their own brainpower. The article argues that while AI delegation often makes sense, its broad capability range and confident conversational interface create new risks for organisations that value critical thinking. It concludes with practical managerial interventions — hiring for "need for cognition," offering monetary incentives and feedback, and engineering AI-free periods — that can blunt the risk.

## TL;DR

- AI amplifies cognitive offloading into potential "cognitive surrender," where users uncritically accept AI errors and underperform even control groups relying solely on their own judgment.
- People with high "need for cognition" are somewhat more resistant to cognitive surrender, but incentives, feedback loops, and structured AI-free periods can help all workers maintain independent thinking.
- Managers currently focused on AI adoption need to also plan for preserving the critical-thinking skills that AI cannot reliably replace.

## Key claims

- "Cognitive offloading" — delegating mental tasks to technology — has precedents in calculators and GPS, both of which carry cognitive costs alongside their benefits.
- A 2019 study by Mark LaCour found that students using calculators programmed with wrong answers showed very little suspicion of inaccurate results, even when answers were patently absurd.
- Research by Louisa Dahmani and Véronique Bohbot found that greater lifetime GPS use correlates with worse spatial memory; pedestrians navigating by phone also take longer, less efficient routes than map users.
- The "Google effect" documents that people have worse recall of information they expect to find online — a precursor to AI-era cognitive risks.
- Steven Shaw and Gideon Nave coined the term "cognitive surrender" to describe AI users who stop thinking independently; when AI gave wrong answers in their experiments, AI-assisted participants did significantly worse than a control group with no AI access.
- High "need for cognition" provides some, but not complete, protection against cognitive surrender.
- Monetary rewards for accuracy and real-time correctness feedback encouraged AI users to override erroneous model outputs more often, though they still underperformed fully independent thinkers.
- A study by Stefanos Poulidis of INSEAD found that chess students with on-demand AI access achieved less than half the performance gains of those who received AI tips only at pre-set moments — suggesting that limiting access, not just restricting use entirely, improves outcomes.

## Key passages

> AI supercharges these trade-offs. Handing specific tasks to models will often make sense: they are much better than humans at many things. But AI's range of capabilities, allied to a convenient conversational interface and a seductively confident persona, raises the prospect less of delegation than of wholesale capitulation.

> Hence "cognitive surrender", a term coined by Steven Shaw of the Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania in a recent paper written with his colleague, Gideon Nave.

> When the model gave accurate responses, the people using it outperformed a control group of people relying on their own brainpower. When the AI gave the wrong answers, the people using it did much worse than the control group. In other words, people stopped thinking for themselves.

> People with high "need for cognition" (yes, dear reader, that means you) are somewhat, though not entirely, protected against the risk of cognitive surrender, says Mr Shaw.

> The students who had on-demand access achieved less than half the performance gains of those who had no say over when they got help. Offloading is fine. Giving up is another matter.
