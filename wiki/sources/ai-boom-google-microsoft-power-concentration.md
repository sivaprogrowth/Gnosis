## Abstract

Published in the Wall Street Journal on February 11, 2023, Christopher Mims argues that the generative AI boom — exemplified by rival chatbot launches from [[companies/microsoft|Microsoft]] and [[companies/google|Google]] — will not democratise technology but instead entrench the power of the largest tech companies. The core mechanism is trust: because AI "answer engines" must be accepted at face value to be useful, and because minimising hallucinations requires vast compute and human-moderation labour, only companies with the resources of [[companies/google|Google]] and Microsoft can achieve the required reliability at scale. [[companies/openai|OpenAI]], initially an apparent exception as a small startup, illustrates the rule by having been compelled to partner with Microsoft to afford the necessary computing infrastructure. The article concludes that the [[concepts/ai-flywheel-effect|AI flywheel effect]] of data, feedback, and moderation capacity will compound the structural advantages already held by big tech incumbents.

## TL;DR

- Generative AI shifts the user relationship from finding information to accepting answers, demanding far deeper trust in the companies that provide it.
- The compute and human-labour costs of making AI trustworthy are so large that only the biggest tech incumbents can sustain them, concentrating market power further.
- [[companies/openai|OpenAI]]'s dependence on Microsoft funding is the clearest early proof that even leading AI startups cannot escape this gravitational pull.

## Key claims

- Generative AI "answer engines" require users to accept outputs at face value, making trust the central competitive moat rather than raw capability.
- The two resource requirements for trustworthy AI are (1) massive cloud computing infrastructure for training and inference, and (2) large human workforces for continuous testing, tuning, and content moderation.
- Meta's Galactica chatbot was pulled after just three days due to public backlash over inaccurate outputs, illustrating how quickly trust failures can destroy a product.
- [[companies/google|Google]]'s strategy of positioning "Responsible AI" as a brand differentiator (via its 2019 AI Principles) reflects a calculated bet that trustworthiness beats speed-to-market.
- ChatGPT reached 100 million users faster than any service in history (per a UBS report), making aggressive first-mover positioning in AI critically important.
- Content moderation of AI-generated output is structurally similar to content moderation of human-generated output — a task already dominated by large platforms with existing infrastructure.
- Wikipedia is offered as a "social solution" analogy: users tolerate imperfect but largely useful AI outputs in the same way they tolerate Wikipedia's imperfections, yet building that trust still favours well-resourced actors.
- Smaller AI search startups (e.g., Andi, with fewer than 10 employees and $2.5 M raised) can demonstrate proof-of-concept but cannot scale trust at the level required for mainstream adoption.

## Key passages

> "Trust in AI, in other words, will become the new moat that big technology companies will fight to defend. Lose the user's trust often enough, and they might abandon your product."

> "The only reason ChatGPT and other foundational models are so bad at bias and even fundamental facts is they are closed systems, and there is no opportunity for feedback."

> "Generative AI in all its forms will insinuate technology more deeply into the way we live and work than it already is—not just answering our questions but writing our memos and speeches or even producing poetry and art."

> "Creating trust by moderating the content generated by AIs might not prove to be so different from creating trust by moderating the content generated by humans. And that is something the biggest technology companies have already shown is a difficult, time-consuming and resource-intensive task they can take on in a way that few other companies can."

> "It's also clear that being a successful first-mover in this space will require the kinds of resources that only the biggest tech companies can muster."
