## Abstract

This *Economist* "Free Exchange" column argues that AI — and tools like ChatGPT in particular — exhibit the hallmarks of a **general-purpose technology (GPT)**: wide adoption, inherent improvement potential, and innovational complementarities. Drawing on the economic history of steam, electrification, and computing, the article makes the case that even transformative technologies take decades to manifest in productivity statistics, partly due to fine-tuning lags, capital constraints, and the time needed to accumulate [[intangible-capital|intangible capital]]. It also surveys AI's likely impact on workers, noting that history's record on technological unemployment is more reassuring than alarming, though disruption to specific occupations and skill groups is real. The piece concludes that, as with prior industrial revolutions, the challenge will be to translate radical economic change into broad-based prosperity.

## TL;DR

- AI fits the classic definition of a **general-purpose technology**, but history shows such technologies take decades — sometimes nearly a century — to show up in productivity data.
- A "productivity J-curve" may already be operating: measured productivity growth can *fall* before it rises as firms invest in learning and redesigning processes around new tech.
- History offers mixed comfort on jobs: mass technological unemployment has never materialised, but disruption to specific occupations and income groups has been severe.

## Key claims

- Timothy Bresnahan and Manuel Trajtenberg's 1995 paper defines a general-purpose technology as one used in many industries, capable of continued improvement, and generating innovational complementarities — criteria AI currently meets.
- Steam power took roughly 60–90 years after Watt's 1769 patent to peak its contribution to productivity growth in Britain, per Nicholas Crafts of the University of Sussex.
- American productivity growth *slowed* from 1888 to 1907 despite electrification being technically complete before 1880, illustrating the gap between invention and economic impact.
- Robert Solow's observation that computers appeared "everywhere but in the productivity statistics" persisted until the mid-1990s, nearly three decades after the first silicon integrated circuits.
- Erik Brynjolfsson, Daniel Rock, and Chad Syverson propose a "productivity J-curve": measured output may decline in the years or decades after a new technology appears, as resources are diverted to learning and process redesign — and they suggest AI-related intangible-capital investment may already be doing this.
- Robert Allen of NYU Abu Dhabi argues that the slow productivity uptake in industrialising Britain reflected a capital shortage that was gradually resolved as profits were reinvested.
- Early industrialisation increased demand for unskilled labour while crushing craftsmen's earnings, driving some into the Luddite machine-smashing movement — a warning that benefits are not automatically shared.
- The assembly-line analogy (Henry Ford's cost cuts → demand surge → worker gains) suggests AI productivity gains in sectors like medicine could expand demand rather than simply displace workers.

## Key passages

> "It might well stand for general-purpose technology: an earth-shaking sort of innovation which stands to boost productivity across a wide-range of industries and occupations, in the manner of steam engines, electricity and computing."

> "In Britain the contribution of steam to productivity growth peaked post-1850, nearly a century after Watt's patent, according to Nicholas Crafts of the University of Sussex."

> "Erik Brynjolfsson of Stanford University, Daniel Rock of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and Chad Syverson of the University of Chicago suggest a disruptive new technology may be associated with a 'productivity J-curve'. Measured productivity growth may actually decline in the years or decades after a new technology appears, as firms and workers divert time and resources to studying the tech and designing business processes around it."

> "Despite epochal technological and economic change, fears of mass technological unemployment have never before been realised. Tech can and does take a toll on individual occupations, however, in ways that can prove socially disruptive."

> "A technology capable of handling almost any task the typical person can do would bring humanity into uncharted economic territory. Yet even in such a scenario, the past holds some lessons."
