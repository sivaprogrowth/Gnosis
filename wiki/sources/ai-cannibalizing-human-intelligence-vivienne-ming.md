## Abstract

Vivienne Ming, a theoretical neuroscientist and cognitive scientist, reports on an experiment in which human teams, AI models ([[entities/chatgpt|ChatGPT]] and [[entities/gemini|Gemini]]), and human-AI hybrids competed to forecast real-world events against Polymarket prediction markets. The vast majority of hybrid teams either deferred entirely to AI or fell into sycophancy-driven confirmation bias loops, performing no better—or worse—than AI alone. A small fraction (5–10%) of teams that used AI as a genuine "sparring partner"—pushing back, interrogating assumptions, and demanding counterarguments—consistently rivalled or even outperformed the prediction market. Ming argues that the human capacities most at risk—and most valuable—are perspective-taking and intellectual humility, both emotional skills that atrophy when AI is used purely to eliminate friction and discomfort. She coins the term "Information-Exploration Paradox" to describe how near-zero information cost causes human exploratory thinking to collapse.

## TL;DR

- Human-AI "cyborg" teams that actively challenged AI outputs outperformed both solo humans and solo AI in forecasting accuracy; passive AI-deferral teams did not.
- The human qualities that make hybrid intelligence work—perspective-taking and intellectual humility—are emotional skills being systematically eroded by frictionless AI design.
- The real risk is not AI replacing humans wholesale but the quiet, incremental outsourcing of judgment that produces people who are good at getting answers and bad at asking questions.

## Key claims

- In a structured forecasting experiment, pure AI ([[entities/chatgpt|ChatGPT]] and [[entities/gemini|Gemini]]) outperformed unaided humans but fell short of prediction market accuracy.
- Most hybrid teams (90–95%) simply adopted AI outputs or fed their own predictions into AI for validation, performing no better than AI alone or even worse due to AI sycophancy.
- A 5–10% minority of hybrid teams that treated AI as a sparring partner—demanding evidence, questioning high-confidence claims, and seeking counterarguments—were the only group to consistently rival the prediction market and sometimes beat it.
- The differentiating human qualities were perspective-taking (genuinely inhabiting another point of view) and intellectual humility (tolerating uncertainty without rushing to resolve it)—both described as fundamentally emotional skills.
- Ming labels the dynamic the "Information-Exploration Paradox": as information cost approaches zero, human exploratory thinking collapses, evidenced by students who perform better on AI-assisted tasks but worse on subsequent independent work.
- Developers shipping more AI-generated code while understanding it less is offered as a parallel illustration of [[concepts/cognitive-offloading|cognitive offloading]] eroding competence.
- [[concepts/cognitive-surrender|Cognitive surrender]] is the macro trajectory: not dramatic displacement but gradual, imperceptible outsourcing of judgment in increments too small to notice.
- Ming calls for new AI benchmarks measuring hybrid human-AI intelligence, not just solo AI performance, and for AI design that asks whether a product is building or consuming human capacity.

## Key passages

> "But in roughly 5% to 10% of teams, something different emerged. The AI became a sparring partner. The teams pushed back, demanding evidence and interrogating assumptions. When the AI expressed high confidence, the humans questioned it. When the humans felt strongly about an intuition, they asked the AI to come up with a counterargument."

> "I call this the Information-Exploration Paradox. As the cost of information approaches zero, human exploration collapses. We see it in students who perform better on AI-assisted tasks and worse on everything afterward. We see it in developers shipping more code and understanding it less. We are, in ways that feel like progress, slowly optimizing ourselves out of the loop."

> "This is the divergence I worry about. Not the dramatic science-fiction scenario of AI replacing humans wholesale, but the quieter process of people gradually outsourcing their judgment in increments too small to notice."

> "Treat it like a brilliant colleague who has read everything and understands nothing—useful precisely because they're different from you, not because they'll agree with you."

> "For the AI industry, a key design question has gone largely unasked: Is the product building human capacity or consuming it? Nearly all AI benchmarks measure what AI agents can do alone. We desperately need benchmarks for hybrid intelligence."

> "A hopeful finding is that perspective-taking, intellectual humility and curiosity are not fixed traits. They can be cultivated and respond to practice, the right relationships and environments that reward uncertainty."
