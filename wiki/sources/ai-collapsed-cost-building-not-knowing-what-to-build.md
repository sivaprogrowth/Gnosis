## Abstract

In this thread shared by [[andreessen-horowitz]], [[companies/andreessen-horowitz|a16z]] partner Josh Elman argues that AI has dramatically reduced the cost of building software but has left the cost of *judgment* — knowing what to build and why — entirely unchanged. Drawing on a career arc from RealNetworks to LinkedIn, Elman reframes the core artifact of product management away from the traditional spec toward a *story* that is immediately understandable and repeatably shareable. He traces how the classic waterfall product loop (idea → spec → scope → build) has been rearranged by cheap AI prototyping into a new loop (build → play → design → ship), where working prototypes replace up-front what-if debates. The piece argues that taste, judgment, and deep user understanding are now *more* important, not less, as the mechanical barriers to building disappear.

## TL;DR

- AI has collapsed the cost of making software, but figuring out *what* to build still requires the same human judgment as ever.
- The product development loop has inverted: build a quick AI prototype first, then play, design, and ship — rather than spec before you build.
- The core artifact of a product manager is not a spec but a *story* — a repeatable narrative about why a product will matter in users' lives.

## Key claims

- The cost of building software has fallen by an order of magnitude thanks to AI, but the cost of judgment has not changed at all.
- The traditional product loop — idea, spec, costing, scoping, then building — was invented primarily to protect scarce engineering time from bad decisions; that scarcity has largely disappeared.
- The new product loop is: build quickly with AI to feel how the idea works, play with it, *then* design (both visually and in engineering terms), then ship and learn.
- Prototypes beat "what-ifs" every time; having something running lets teams know what a thing *is* before committing to full design and engineering work.
- The true artifact of a product manager is a *story* — not a spec — because a story must be immediately understandable and repeatable without the PM in the room.
- A spec describes a system and its requirements; a story describes the people who will use the product and why it will matter in their lives.
- Elman's one-sentence definition of PM work remains durable: "A product manager helps their team (and company) ship the right product to their users."
- AI is also changing what users *expect* a product to be — particularly in consumer, where natural-language description and background agents may replace learned interfaces.

## Key passages

> AI has collapsed the cost of building products, but not the cost of knowing what to build.

> There's no question that the cost of *making stuff* has collapsed. It's no longer all that difficult to scope something out and try it; that gives you enormous flexibility. But the cost of *judgment* hasn't changed at all. Figuring out what to build is more important now than ever.

> The spec describes a system, what it must do, and what boxes must be checked off before it is done. *That is not the art of product management.* Product management is about telling the story of people who are going to use the product, and why it will matter in their lives. It has to be immediately understandable, by whoever you're talking to. And it has to be *repeatable* — people need to be able to pass it around faithfully, without you being in the room.

> These are all rituals that we invented in order to protect engineering time from bad decisions. Because you only got six or eight turns around that loop a year.

> First, take the idea and you build it quickly with AI, just to see how it works and how it feels. You get to play with it, and figure out how it feels and fits into the overall picture. Prototypes beat "what-ifs", every time.
