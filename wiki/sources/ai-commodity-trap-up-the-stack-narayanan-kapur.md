## Abstract

Narayanan and Kapur argue that frontier AI model inference is structurally predisposed to become a commodity business — exhibiting near-identical models, similar capital costs, minimal switching costs, and free price competition — making it nearly impossible for labs to recoup the estimated $4–8 trillion projected AI infrastructure investment through token sales alone. Drawing on six historical infrastructure analogues (railroads, electricity, telecom, cloud, semiconductors, aviation) and the [[concepts/build-vs-buy|Bertrand paradox]], the authors contend that the only durable exit from this trap is to migrate "up the stack" into vertically integrated enterprise products, AI-native SaaS, bespoke deployments, and eventually "digital worker" agents that accumulate deep organisational lock-in. They warn that this transition — already underway at [[companies/openai|OpenAI]] and [[companies/anthropic|Anthropic]] — raises serious concerns about enterprise switching costs, competitive foreclosure, and the concentration of economic and political power in a small number of AI incumbents.

## TL;DR

- Selling raw model inference is a structural commodity trap; competition will push token prices toward marginal cost, making the model layer alone insufficient to recoup multi-trillion-dollar AI infrastructure investments.
- The labs' most viable path to durable profitability is moving "up the stack" — into products, AI-native SaaS, bespoke enterprise deployments, and digital-worker agents — borrowing moat-building strategies from enterprise software.
- This "up the stack" migration solves the financial problem for labs but risks harmful enterprise lock-in, reduced competition, and concentrated market power that regulators should address early.

## Key claims

- Historical infrastructure industries (railroads, electricity, telecom, aviation) consistently failed to capture the value they created; value accrued instead to applications built on top, and the same dynamic threatens AI labs selling raw inference.
- Enterprise software escapes the commodity trap via three structural properties infrastructure lacks: zero marginal cost of reproduction, deep switching costs, and non-ephemeral value that amortises fixed costs over decades.
- The Bertrand paradox applies with unusual force to frontier model inference: models are largely undifferentiated, capital costs are similar across labs, there is no geographic fragmentation, open-source competition prevents tacit collusion, and API routing tools reduce switching friction to near zero.
- Recouping $4–8 T of infrastructure investment at a 5% net margin over five years would require $16–32 T in annual revenue — the upper bound being roughly a quarter of current world GDP — making the model-inference-only business model mathematically implausible.
- The two historical partial exceptions — cloud computing and TSMC — escaped the commodity trap by either acquiring software-like lock-in properties or achieving near-monopoly fabrication leadership; these are the only proven templates.
- Labs are already climbing the stack: [[companies/openai|OpenAI]]'s ChatGPT (Company Knowledge) and [[companies/anthropic|Anthropic]]'s Claude Code and Claude Tag ("digital worker") represent escalating layers of embedding, ecosystem, commercial, behavioral, and outcome-based moats.
- Behavioral moats — skill erosion, personality attachment (e.g., the #Keep4o backlash), and credence-good dynamics — are novel forms of lock-in with no clean analogue in earlier enterprise software, with acute societal implications beyond value capture.
- Outcome-based pricing (charging a share of value delivered rather than per token) is the logical endpoint of moving up the stack, but requires deep visibility into customer workflows that labs do not yet possess.

## Key passages

> "The labs' most likely path to durable profitability runs not through the foundation layers (chips, datacenters, models) that have thus far accounted for the bulk of investments, but higher up the stack, through a mix of vertical integration, embedded enterprise deployments, and the deliberate construction of switching costs and other 'moats'."

> "We must distinguish between *value creation* and *value capture*. We expect the value *created* to be unfathomably large. The central — perhaps even existential — question for the labs is whether they will be able to *capture* even a small percentage of that value."

> "A digital worker that is embedded with every team in a company quickly becomes a store of tacit knowledge for the whole organization and becomes essential to workflows and business processes across different teams. Unless portability is explicitly built in, it becomes a digital employee that effectively cannot be fired, creating a level of lock-in that exceeds that of enterprise software at its worst."

> "Behavioral moats also include novel and poorly understood forms of AI lock-in that have no clean analog in earlier software. A notable manifestation is relational attachment to a model's tone or 'personality,' as vividly demonstrated by the #Keep4o backlash, when OpenAI was forced to reinstate GPT-4o in response to user demands."

> "Infrastructure providers rarely capture the value they create. Across railroads, electricity, telecom, and airlines, the firms that built capacity were eventually competed, regulated, or commoditized into thin margins."

> "History suggests that the time to establish interoperability standards, portability requirements, and switching-cost transparency is early, before lock-in compounds and intervention becomes structurally more difficult."
