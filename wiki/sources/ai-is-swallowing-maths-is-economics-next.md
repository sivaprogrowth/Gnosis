## Abstract

Following AI's success in solving hundreds of open problems in mathematics, the economics profession is grappling with whether AI will similarly transform — and potentially erode — the human advantage in economic research. The article surveys several empirical studies showing that AI agents can already conduct policy evaluations and reproduce empirical papers, but with substantially higher error rates than human authors, particularly around novel ideas and contextual judgment. While AI is becoming a de facto co-author — cutting the cost of writing, analysis, and modelling — economists argue that human judgment remains indispensable for framing research questions, extrapolating from evidence, and catching the kinds of flaws that AI produces by brute-force "p-hacking." The piece concludes that AI will progressively remove drudgery from the discipline but is unlikely to fully replace the integrative, multi-domain reasoning Keynes described as the hallmark of a master economist.

## TL;DR

- AI agents can already run autonomous economic policy evaluations, but current error rates are far higher than in human-authored papers (84% severe-error rate vs. ~10% for top-journal human papers).
- AI outperforms humans in crowded, literature-heavy fields but struggles with novel research framing, contextual judgment, and reliable identification of conceptual flaws.
- Leading economists see AI as a "co-author" that cuts research costs, but argue human judgment over models, evidence, and research frames remains essential for now.

## Key claims

- By July 2026, roughly one in four working papers filed with the [[nber|National Bureau of Economic Research]] contained AI-assisted writing, per an analysis by Paul Goldsmith-Pinkham of Yale School of Management.
- The *Journal of Political Economy* will raise its submission fee to as much as $500 per paper in 2027 to deter AI-generated "slop" flooding top journals.
- David Yanagizawa-Drott's Autonomous Policy Evaluation (APE) programme uses AI agents to replicate the workflow of human economists using publicly available data, but an internal check of 1,000 APE papers found 842 contained at least one severe error.
- A joint exercise between *The Economist* and research-verification firm Refine found that among 100 papers accepted to the top five economics journals in 2026, one in ten contained at least one grave error — a substantially lower rate than for APE papers.
- Alexis Akira Toda's experiment found AI lowers the cost of checking for candidate flaws in economic theory papers but "does not yet reliably substitute for the human judgment that locates the flaw in the first place."
- Michele Zampa found AI performs better than humans in crowded research fields with large existing literatures, but humans maintain consistent performance regardless of literature size.
- Nic Fishman and Gabriel Sekeres calculate that guarding against AI p-hacking would require ~7,000 robustness checks per paper, versus ~50 for a human author — rendering verification practically infeasible under current methods.
- Benjamin Kohler and colleagues showed that when AI reproduced 48 social-science papers, discrepancies were split roughly equally between AI and human error, but performance tilted against humans as the AI model improved.

## Key passages

> "the figure format, some of the analysis, the more polished text" — Stefanie Stantcheva, on observable changes in papers submitted to the *Quarterly Journal of Economics*

> "We need to think about ai as a co-author," says Dani Rodrik of Harvard, though it will be a while before economics can dispense with human co-authors altogether. "There is still a large amount of uncertainty about the right models, how to extrapolate from the evidence and what the right research frame is."

> Nic Fishman of Harvard and Gabriel Sekeres of Cornell University calculate that the number of needed robustness checks would rise from 50 for a human author to an impractical 7,000 for agentic ones.

> A master-economist, he noted, "must be mathematician, historian, statesman, philosopher…He must understand symbols and speak in words. He must contemplate the particular in terms of the general, and touch abstract and concrete in the same flight of thought."

> The discrepancies between the original papers and the ai versions were due in roughly equal measure to problems with the ai and human work. But the better the ai model, the more the balance tilted against humans.

> ai did better in crowded fields with lots of existing research to digest. Humans performed at the same level regardless of the size of the literature.
