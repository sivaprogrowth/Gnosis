## Abstract

This Mercatus Center research paper examines whether the post-2024 proliferation of widely accessible generative AI tools has coincided with disproportionate growth in independent work and solo business formation in AI-exposed sectors and occupations. Drawing on US Census Bureau Business Formation Statistics (BFS) and Current Population Survey (CPS) microdata, the study finds that nonemployer-type business applications rose 26.8 percent in AI-exposed sectors from Q1 2024 to Q1 2026 while employer-type applications in those same sectors fell 6.4 percent, suggesting the growth is specifically in solo formation. The paper grounds these findings in a Coasean transaction-cost framework, arguing that generative AI reduces the need for complementary labour inputs, improves output measurability, and thereby lowers the minimum efficient scale for professional services — shifting the firm–market boundary toward independent work. Results are explicitly descriptive and not causal, but the pattern is coherent across two independent data sources and concentrated precisely in the AI-exposed occupations and industries where the mechanism predicts it should be.

## TL;DR

- Solo-type business formation and solo self-employment rose sharply in AI-exposed sectors and occupations after early 2024 while comparison groups (construction, wholesale trade) remained flat or declined.
- The growth is specifically in filings with no markers of employer intent — indicating "unbundling" of knowledge work from firms, not merely more entrepreneurship broadly.
- The paper's policy punchline is institutional mismatch: unemployment insurance and employer-sponsored benefits are built for W-2 employment, not for a labour market reorganising around solo knowledge work.

## Key claims

- From Q1 2024 to Q1 2026, nonemployer-type applications in AI-exposed sectors (Professional Services, Information, Education, Finance & Insurance) rose 26.8 percent; the construction-and-wholesale comparison group was essentially unchanged at −0.4 percent.
- In the same window, employer-type applications in AI-exposed sectors *fell* 6.4 percent even as total applications rose 18.0 percent — the entire gain came from solo-type filings.
- An event-study panel (2022–2025, seven sectors) finds no statistically significant pre-treatment difference in 2022, a positive-but-insignificant 2024 coefficient, and a positive, statistically significant 2025 coefficient implying ~16 percent more growth in AI-exposed sectors than the comparison group.
- CPS data show solo self-employment in AI-exposed industries rose 7.9 percent from the 2022–2023 baseline to 2025, while the comparison group declined 2.1 percent.
- At the occupation level, solo self-employment in the 10 most AI-exposed occupations (per the Felten-Raj-Seamans AIOE index) rose 20 percent from the 2022–2023 baseline to 2025; the 10 least-exposed occupations were essentially unchanged.
- Among management analysts specifically, the solo self-employment share rose from ~5.6 percent in 2022–2023 to over 7.0 percent in Q1 2026 — near its highest level in a decade — while overall occupation employment grew, not shrank.
- The paper argues generative AI functions as a transaction-cost shock analogous to (but distinct from) gig-platform effects: it reduces the need for complementary labour inputs, lowers output-specification costs, and shrinks the firm's bundling advantage for knowledge workers.
- The paper proposes portable, worker-owned benefit accounts (health, retirement, income-disruption) as the primary policy response, paired with a pooled insurance backstop for lower-income workers.

## Key passages

> The result is not necessarily unemployment. It may instead be a migration of some work away from firms.

> The sharpest result is not simply that applications rose in AI-exposed sectors. It is that the rise came entirely from applications that do not display employer intent.

> AI may be understood as a related transaction-cost shock, though one operating through a different mechanism… generative AI may reduce several transaction costs that historically favored firm-based employment—including the need for complementary labor inputs, relationship-specific skill investments, and the difficulty of specifying and measuring knowledge work outputs.

> The occupation is not shrinking; it is reorganizing… Solo self-employment grew more than twice as fast as overall employment in the occupation from 2022 to early 2026, reversing a decade-long decline in the share working independently.

> The first labor-market effects of AI may not appear primarily as mass unemployment. They may instead emerge through the growth of independent work and solo self-employment.

> The EIN business applications are already coming in. The question is whether labor policy will catch up.
