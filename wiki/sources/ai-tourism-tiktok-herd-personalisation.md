## Abstract

This *Economist* culture piece examines the tension between social-media-driven travel homogenisation — epitomised by [[companies/tiktok|TikTok]]-famous queues at the same handful of restaurants and landmarks — and the potential for AI-powered travel recommendation to push tourists toward more personalised, off-the-beaten-path experiences. Using Rome as an illustrative case, the article contrasts the convergent recommendations of legacy platforms (Lonely Planet, Tripadvisor) and viral social media with the divergent, preference-sensitive outputs that AI chatbots could theoretically produce. The central argument is that, contrary to fears that AI will amplify the "TikTok herd" effect by surfacing the same top results for everyone, well-designed AI assistants may instead act as a counterforce by tailoring suggestions to individual tastes. The piece situates this debate within broader anxieties about [[concepts/ai-slop|AI slop]] and [[concepts/cognitive-offloading|cognitive offloading]] in everyday decision-making.

## TL;DR

- Social media platforms like [[companies/tiktok|TikTok]] and aggregators like Tripadvisor have intensified tourism herding by funnelling crowds toward the same viral spots.
- AI travel assistants may break this pattern by generating personalised, idiosyncratic recommendations rather than popularity-ranked lists.
- The outcome depends on whether AI systems are designed (and used) to reflect individual preferences or simply to surface consensus "best" options.

## Key claims

- Guidebook series such as Lonely Planet and review platforms such as Tripadvisor have long concentrated tourist footfall on a small number of highly-rated venues.
- [[companies/tiktok|TikTok]] has accelerated this concentration further, making queues at "TikTok-famous" spots a visible and self-reinforcing phenomenon.
- There is a widespread fear that AI recommendation tools will replicate and amplify this herding effect at even greater scale.
- The article argues the opposite is plausible: AI chatbots, by engaging with individual context and preference, are structurally better suited to personalised recommendation than ranked-list platforms.
- The contrast is framed as convergent recommendation (popularity-ranked aggregators) vs. divergent recommendation (preference-sensitive AI).
- The quality of AI travel guidance depends heavily on how users prompt the system and how the system is trained to balance popularity signals against individual fit.
- Luxury travel agents are cited as a parallel case where human contextual judgment still outperforms AI, suggesting AI's travel advantage is strongest in the mid-market.
