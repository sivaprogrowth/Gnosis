## Abstract

South Korea is experiencing a dramatic AI-driven windfall, concentrated in its semiconductor sector — particularly SK Hynix and Samsung Electronics — which supply the high-bandwidth memory chips underpinning AI model infrastructure. The bonanza is minting a new "silicon-collar" worker class with bonuses exceeding $400,000, while retail investors and workers outside the chip industry have largely missed out. The KOSPI stock index has doubled in a year but also suffered extreme volatility, becoming the world's most volatile market. President Lee Jae Myung's government is debating how to steward the surge in tax revenues through mechanisms such as a "Future Response Fund" and expanded sovereign-wealth investment, while critics argue over whether redistributive levies on chipmakers are appropriate given the private-sector nature of the firms.

## TL;DR

- South Korea's AI semiconductor boom is generating historic corporate profits and tax revenues, but the wealth is narrowly concentrated in the chip industry.
- The government is exploring sovereign-wealth funds, a "Future Response Fund," and possible citizen dividends to distribute windfall gains broadly.
- The Korean experience is an early case study for how nations might manage AI-driven economic concentration before it plays out elsewhere.

## Key claims

- SK Hynix and Samsung Electronics reported operating profit growth of 557% and 1,800% year-on-year respectively in Q2 2026, driven by AI chip demand.
- South Korea's chipmakers are described as supplying "picks and shovels" for the AI gold rush — infrastructure providers rather than AI model developers.
- A new "silicon-collar" worker class has emerged: chip-industry employees earning bonuses that can exceed $400,000 in a country where average annual salary is under $40,000.
- Corporate-tax receipts from Samsung and SK Hynix alone could match the government's total expected corporate-tax intake for the year; overall tax revenue is projected at 500trn won (~17% of GDP) in 2026.
- The government announced a "Future Response Fund" to deploy excess tax revenues into future industries, regional development, education, and support for young people.
- Officials also plan to inject at least 20trn won ($14bn) into the Korea Investment Corporation sovereign-wealth fund for AI, data centres, and infrastructure.
- Critics of redistribution argue chipmakers' profits differ fundamentally from state-owned resource revenues, and that new levies risk discouraging the continuous capital investment chipmaking requires.
- The KOSPI index's doubling in a year followed by a 22% drop in July made it the world's most volatile major stock market, burning many retail investors.

## Key passages

> "This raises a new policy challenge for us: how should we approach such extraordinary excess profits from a socioeconomic perspective and from the standpoint of national policy?"

> "South Korean officials speak of producing 'picks and shovels' for an AI gold rush. While the gold miners may or may not hit paydirt, the pickmakers (or, rather, the chipmakers) are already flush."

> "Such payouts are unheard of in a country where the average annual salary is less than $40,000. A new class of workers has been minted: neither white- nor blue-collar, but 'silicon-collar'."

> "Chipmakers' success was made possible by 'long-term public investment by the state and support from taxpayers and the public', and should be returned in part to the public."
