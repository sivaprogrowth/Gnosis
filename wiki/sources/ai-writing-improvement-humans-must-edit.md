## Abstract

This *Economist* leader argues that large language models (LLMs) are rapidly closing the gap with human prose — an internal study comparing 14 variants of ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, and Grok across 55,940 sentences and 1.2 million words finds that formerly telltale AI stylistic quirks are fading with each model update. While the flood of [[concepts/ai-slop|AI slop]] (LLMs have tripled new e-book output on Amazon to 300,000 per month) degrades the information environment, the piece is ultimately prescriptive: as AI writing improves, the human skill that appreciates in value is not writing but *editing*. The article offers three concrete editorial principles — commission well, pay attention to detail, and know your audience — drawn from *The Economist*'s own editorial practice.

## TL;DR

- LLMs are measurably converging on human prose style, erasing formerly reliable detection signals like em-dashes and words such as "leveraging."
- The human skill that becomes more valuable as AI writing improves is **editing**, not writing itself.
- Three editorial principles apply when working with AI output: commission precisely, scrutinise detail, and tune tone to audience.

## Key claims

- *The Economist* tested 14 LLM variants (ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok) released since 2024 against its own corpus of 55,940 sentences and 1.2 million words, finding AI writing increasingly indistinguishable from human writing.
- Former stylistic tells — heavy em-dash use, words like "leveraging" — are no longer reliable markers in recent model outputs.
- LLMs have tripled the monthly volume of new e-books published on Amazon to 300,000; one estimate credits them with drafting more than a third of all new websites.
- Bot-detection tools such as Pangram remain effective enough that AI-assisted job applications and column submissions can still be identified.
- AI prose exhibits persistent patterns: preference for Latinate vocabulary over Anglo-Saxon words, overuse of the "rule of three" rhetorical device, clichéd metaphors (e.g. "Achilles' heel"), and a sycophantic, often off-key tone.
- The editorial function — intermediary between writer and reader — is the locus of human comparative advantage in an AI-writing world.
- [[concepts/ai-sycophancy|AI sycophancy]] is flagged as a practical editorial hazard: LLMs default to affirmation and exclamation marks regardless of communicative context.

## Key passages

> "To them, writer's block is a 500-sheet pack of paper: any user of an LLM can produce as many words in a day as Kafka did in his life."

> "We compared our corpus across 55,940 sentences and 1.2m words, and checked our results against other newspapers and hit fiction books. We found that some of AI's former tricks—using lots of em-dashes, for instance, or words like 'leveraging'—are no longer leveraged much in its writing."

> "If LLMs keep improving, in a few years they could be as good as professional wordsmiths. In the meantime, though, their baggy prose puts a premium on editing skills."

> "AI writers will make mistakes and offer phrases that sound good but mean nothing. They like to lavish their prose with Latinate words... remind them that Anglo-Saxon words are often punchier."

> "LLMs are sycophants and their smarmy tone is often off-key... Most people are short of time, but a verbose AI pins you to the wall like a bore at a party."

> "'Write drunk, edit sober,' goes the old writers' maxim. If that's right, when the AI-human team gets down to work, the AIs will have all the fun."
