## Abstract

This *Economist* article examines why America is experiencing a record-breaking entrepreneurship boom, with new-business applications averaging 466,000 per month since early 2024 — nearly double pre-pandemic levels. It identifies three interlocking drivers: a tight macroeconomic labour market that pushes jobseekers toward self-employment, shifting consumption patterns (ageing demographics, e-commerce niche expansion) that reward small operators, and — most importantly — AI tools that dramatically lower the activation energy for starting a business. The piece also grapples with whether this boom creates jobs or simply produces a wave of [[concepts/solo-self-employment|solo self-employed]] "solopreneur" ventures, a question on which policymakers and economists disagree.

## TL;DR

- New-business applications in the US hit record highs in 2024–2026, driven by labour-market friction, e-commerce tailwinds, and AI-enabled business formation.
- AI is the single biggest structural change: 60% of founders used AI to launch in 2025 (up from 30% in 2023), and economists argue it is "filling the capability gaps that once made hiring necessary."
- The boom may be producing more [[concepts/solo-self-employment|solopreneur]] ventures than traditional job-creating small businesses, with "high-propensity" applications falling from 38% to 30% of the total.

## Key claims

- Since early 2024, an average of 466,000 new-business applications have been filed per month in the US; June 2026 saw 531,000, nearly double the 2019 monthly average.
- Full-time self-employment reached 16.8 million in 2025 — its highest level this century — according to the Small Business & Entrepreneurship Council.
- The industries with the biggest surge in applications are professional services (+25% from 2021–2025), retail (+18%), and health care & social assistance (+18%), not AI startups.
- A tight hiring environment and declining youth job-market confidence (75% saying it was a good time to find a job in 2022, falling to 43% in 2025) are pushing people toward entrepreneurship.
- Research by Ryan Decker (Federal Reserve Board) and John Haltiwanger (University of Maryland) found a strong correlation between high quit-rates and new-business applications, concentrated in pandemic-era suburban migration zones.
- More than half of [[companies/stripe|Stripe]]-supported Shopify sales now come from "niche" categories outside the top 100, enabling micro-entrepreneurs to monetise specialist interests.
- The share of founders using AI to launch doubled from 30% to 60% between 2023 and 2025, per a Gusto survey; JPMorgan Chase found new cohorts adopting AI earlier and at higher rates than prior cohorts.
- [[companies/stripe|Stripe]] economists argued AI is ushering in "the age of the solopreneur" by eliminating capability gaps that previously required hiring; SBA head Kelly Loeffler disputes this, predicting AI-powered small businesses will eventually hire at similar or higher rates.

## Key passages

> "Since the beginning of 2024 an average of 466,000 new-business applications have been filed each month in America, according to data from the Census Bureau. This June saw 531,000 applications, nearly double the average monthly figure in 2019."

> "'It's like chemical reactions that have an activation potential,' says Miquel Llobet, who launched an AI-powered commercial-insurance broker last year. AI lowers the bar for those reactions to occur."

> "'AI is filling the capability gaps that once made hiring necessary', ushering in 'the age of the solopreneur', argued a team of economists at Stripe, a payments platform, in a recent blog post."

> "Aaron Terrazas, an economist at Gusto, reckons that much of the value generated by AI could end up accruing not to corporate giants, but to Etsy-sellers and small-town accountants. That would reverse a century-long shift toward working for The Man."

> "The share of Americans aged 15-34 who say it is a good time to find a job fell from 75% in 2022 to 43% last year, according to Gallup."

> "More than half of sales supported by Shopify, a provider of e-commerce tools, now come from 'niche' categories outside the 100 most common; entrepreneurs can mint money selling trading cards and custom pill cases, avoiding crowded categories."
