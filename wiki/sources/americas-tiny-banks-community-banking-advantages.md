## Abstract

This *Economist* article examines the distinctive role of American community banks — generally defined as institutions with less than $10bn in assets — in providing credit to small businesses, farms, immigrant communities, and rural areas that larger banks underserve. Drawing on two case studies, Canandaigua National Bank & Trust in upstate New York and Abacus Bank in Manhattan's Chinatown, the piece argues that community banks' competitive advantage rests on relationship-based, on-the-ground underwriting that larger banks cannot replicate at scale. Despite comprising 97% of the total number of US banks but less than 14% of assets, community banks account for 36% of all small-business loans and 31% of farm-sector debt. The article also contextualises the historically large number of American banks, traces their decline from 30,000 in 1921 to roughly 4,100 today, and flags commercial real-estate concentration — rather than deposit flight — as the sector's principal near-term risk.

## TL;DR

- Community banks punch far above their asset-share weight in small-business and agricultural lending by substituting local knowledge and relationship underwriting for algorithmic credit assessment.
- Their large numbers are an artefact of early American anti-federalist suspicion of centralised banking, not economic inefficiency.
- Failure of the sector would not cause systemic risk directly, but research shows small-business lending in an acquired bank's territory falls after a larger-bank takeover.

## Key claims

- Community banks (assets < $10bn) represent ~97% of US bank count but under 14% of assets and deposits.
- They account for 36% of all small-business loans and 31% of farm-sector debt despite holding less than one-sixth of total US banking assets.
- Relationship-based underwriting — including site visits and in-person lending committees — allows community banks to extend credit on the basis of borrower character and business vision, not just quantitative metrics.
- America's high bank count is a historical artefact rooted in anti-federalist politics; interstate branching was not permitted until 1994.
- The number of US banks has fallen from over 30,000 in 1921 to ~4,100 at end-2022, driven by the S&L crisis, the 2007–09 financial crisis, and mergers.
- Community banks' revenue model is more interest-dependent and less fee/investment-banking-dependent than large banks, producing lower earnings volatility but greater sensitivity to falling rates.
- The largest near-term risk for community banks is their outsize concentration in commercial real-estate loans, not deposit outflows.
- Research indicates that when a large bank acquires a community bank, small-business lending in the target's service area declines.
