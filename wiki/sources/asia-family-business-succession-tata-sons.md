## Abstract

The article examines the leadership upheaval at India's Tata Sons — triggered by the surprise resignation of non-family chairman Natarajan Chandrasekaran — as a lens for the broader corporate governance challenges facing Asia's family-founded conglomerates. Despite Tata Sons being widely regarded as a model of professional management, Chandrasekaran's exit reveals persistent family influence through Tata Trusts, the charity vehicle that holds a two-thirds stake in Tata Sons and whose new head, Noel Tata, is seen as driving the change. The article situates Tata's troubles within a pan-Asian pattern: heavy inheritance taxes in South Korea, property-sector stress in Hong Kong, and aging patriarchs across the region mean that decades of accumulated family wealth are now being transferred, raising the potential for internal conflict. The piece underscores the public stakes of these private disputes, given that family conglomerates are major employers, drivers of national industrial policy, and, in Tata's case, key nodes in global supply chains such as Apple's iPhone assembly in India.

## TL;DR

- Natarajan Chandrasekaran's resignation as Tata Sons chairman exposes the enduring grip of founding families over ostensibly professionally managed Asian conglomerates.
- A structural tension between Tata Sons' board and Tata Trusts (which holds ~67% of Tata Sons) means family interests can override professional management at any time.
- Across Asia — South Korea, China, Hong Kong, Indonesia — the simultaneous transfer of post-WWII family wealth is creating a wave of succession conflicts with national economic consequences.

## Key claims

- Chandrasekaran was the first Tata Sons chairman with no family ties and was widely expected to be reappointed to a third term before resigning in February 2026.
- Chandrasekaran reportedly lost the backing of at least one unnamed board director despite having support from two key Tata Trusts charities, suggesting a rift with Noel Tata.
- Noel Tata, who assumed leadership of Tata Trusts in 2024 after Ratan Tata's death, is seen by business experts as asserting himself in ways that increased friction within the group.
- Tata Sons faces compounding operational crises: heavy losses at Air India, the aftermath of a fatal plane crash (260 deaths), a cyberattack that shuttered Jaguar Land Rover production for weeks, and AI-driven headwinds at [[companies/tata-consultancy-services]].
- India's central bank is pushing Tata Sons to go public due to its designation as a systemically important lender — a move Noel Tata opposes because it could reduce Tata Trusts' control and philanthropic flexibility.
- Academic research (Morten Bennedsen, University of Copenhagen) frames Asia's succession wave as a structural phenomenon: most family wealth built after WWII is now transfer-ready, and larger wealth transfers correlate with greater conflict potential.
- The Reliance conglomerate precedent — a public, bitter split between Mukesh and Anil Ambani after founder Dhirubhai Ambani's 2002 death — established how high the stakes of Asian family succession can be.
- Professional CEOs in family-founded firms face an inherent dual mandate: satisfy the board while managing the founding family as a parallel institution, a balance Chandrasekaran's predecessor Cyrus Mistry failed to maintain and was ousted for in 2016.

## Key passages

> "The more wealth that has to be transferred, the more potential for conflict." — Morten Bennedsen, professor at the University of Copenhagen

> "Noel Tata has been in some sense pushing for change, asserting himself." — Kavil Ramachandran, professor at the Indian School of Business in Hyderabad

> "It isn't during this kind of crisis that we change the captain." — Kavil Ramachandran

> A successful outside manager must manage family members' expectations and understand "that there is an institution called the family." — Morten Bennedsen

> "Those kinds of risks could be unpalatable to many of your investors. It might potentially stop you from doing what may actually be a good thing for the group and the country." — Mukund Rajan, on the risks of listing Tata Sons

> Chandrasekaran, who was appointed in 2017, "played the game well with Ratan Tata. He kept him in the loop." — Kavil Ramachandran
