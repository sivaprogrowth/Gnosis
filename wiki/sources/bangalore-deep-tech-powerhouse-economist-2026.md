## Abstract

This *Economist* article surveys Bangalore's emergence as a hub for "deep tech" — a broad term covering space, defence, and semiconductor sectors grounded in advanced engineering or scientific breakthroughs. It profiles leading startups such as Pixxel (hyperspectral Earth-observation satellites), Skyroot (reusable rockets, now India's first space unicorn), and Sarvam (a government-backed indigenous AI model), while tracing how India's shift from hands-off IT-era governance to active state involvement has catalysed the sector. The piece argues that although government deregulation, procurement, and a new $11bn research fund have accelerated growth, coordination failures, capital constraints, export-control anxieties, and Bangalore's notoriously poor urban infrastructure pose significant risks to the city's ambitions as a global technology powerhouse.

## TL;DR

- Bangalore now hosts 1,300+ deep-tech companies spanning space, defence, and chips, with funding growing nearly twice as fast as for other Indian startups.
- Government deregulation (especially the 2020 opening of the space sector) and an $11bn high-risk research fund have been key accelerants, but coordination among India's many technology missions remains weak.
- Urban dysfunction — traffic, unreliable utilities, broken roads — and capital-repatriation concerns for foreign investors are the most tangible threats to Bangalore's rise.

## Key claims

- Pixxel has raised $200m, operates six hyperspectral-imaging satellites, holds contracts with India's air force and a US spy-satellite agency, and plans to quadruple its constellation within a year.
- Skyroot became India's first space unicorn (valued at $1.1bn) in May 2026, shortly before completing its first satellite launch, and is developing reusable rockets.
- India went from one space startup a decade ago to more than 400 today, with many attracting global attention and capital.
- Sarvam, India's government-backed indigenous AI model effort, is valued at over $1.5bn after a $234m funding round in June 2026.
- The Modi government opened the space sector in 2020, created the IN-SPACe agency to champion private enterprise, and is disbursing $11bn for high-risk research through venture-capital funds.
- Indian deep-tech startups face a growth-capital shortage, exacerbated by a weak rupee and foreign-investor concerns about repatriating returns.
- National-security concerns — including potential export controls and scrutiny of foreign equity in startups working on sensitive technologies — could constrain the sector's international expansion.
- Bangalore's urban governance is described as a "disaster," with chronic traffic congestion, unreliable utilities, and deteriorating infrastructure threatening its status as a global tech hub.

## Key passages

> "For decades Bangalore has powered India's software and IT industry, as the home to giants that include [[infosys]] and Wipro. In the 2010s it produced dozens of companies focused on India's huge consumer market… Now the buzz in India's startup capital is 'deep tech'."

> "India's first tech boom happened largely despite the government, bad schools and shoddy infrastructure… This time is different. India's bureaucratic government has become highly involved in what it calls strategic sectors."

> "A decade ago India had only one space startup. Now there are more than 400, and many are attracting global attention."

> "A pot of $11bn for high-risk research is being disbursed through venture-capital funds; in October it made its first awards."

> "Some officials fret at how quickly things have taken off. They worry that they lack the oversight tools for companies developing sensitive technologies."
