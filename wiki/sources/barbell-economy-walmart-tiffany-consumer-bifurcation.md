## Abstract

This *Economist* article argues that America's post-pandemic consumer slowdown has not hit all businesses equally: a "barbell" consumption pattern has emerged in which budget and discount retailers flourish as middle-income shoppers trade down, while luxury goods sellers remain buoyant thanks to wealthy households whose savings and asset prices have held up. Persistent inflation disproportionately eroded the purchasing power of lower- and middle-income households, widening the gap between consumer segments. Businesses caught in the middle of the price spectrum — the "missing middle" — face the greatest headwinds. In response, companies are repositioning via premiumisation, budget-tier launches, or shedding mid-range sub-brands.

## TL;DR

- A barbell spending pattern has emerged: discount/budget and luxury businesses are thriving, while mid-market firms are squeezed from both sides.
- Inflation hit lower-income households hardest, with the bottom quintile experiencing an inflation rate one-fifth higher than the top quintile in 2022.
- Companies are responding by acquiring luxury brands, launching budget tiers, or divesting mid-range assets.

## Key claims

- Inflation for the bottom income quintile was approximately one-fifth higher than for the top quintile in 2022, according to [[companies/goldman-sachs|Goldman Sachs]].
- Excess pandemic savings have fallen from ~$2.5trn (mid-2021) to ~$1.5trn, with the remaining bulk held by high-income households.
- The S&P 500 is up ~26% since January 2020 and house prices ~38%, fattening the wallets of asset-owning affluent consumers.
- Discount retailer Burlington saw ~13.2% year-on-year sales growth in Q1 2023, versus a ~4.2% decline for mid-market Macy's.
- [[companies/amazon|Walmart]]-rival LVMH reported 8% year-on-year US sales growth in Q1 2023; Hermès reported no US slowdown in the same period.
- The US luxury goods market grew 8.7% in 2022, well above inflation, per Euromonitor.
- Luxury car sales hit a record 19.6% of the total US market in January 2023 (Kelley Blue Book data).
- Companies are repositioning: L'Oréal acquired Aesop for $2.5bn (premiumisation); Walmart sold Bonobos for $75m (shedding mid-market); Netflix and Disney launched ad-supported tiers (budget expansion).

## Key passages

> Hard economic times have, as in the past, pushed consumers of middling means to trade down to budget-friendly stores and products, boosting the performance of those businesses. Wealthy households, however, remain flush with cash, leaving businesses that cater to the affluent surprisingly buoyant. That has raised awkward questions for firms that offer their customers neither frugality nor luxury, but something in between.

> Over the course of 2022 the inflation rate for households in the bottom income quintile was one-fifth higher than that for the top quintile, according to Goldman Sachs, a bank, offsetting faster wage growth among low-earners.

> The resilience of the luxury business has been helped by a shift in focus since the financial crisis from the merely rich to the positively loaded, notes Claudia D'Arpizio of Bain, a consultancy... Whereas aspirational buyers may in good times splash out on a pair of Gucci sneakers, those at the tippy-top of the income distribution are reliable patrons even when the economy looks shaky. That has made luxury a less cyclical business than it once was.

> With consumer spending shifting to the two extremes of the price spectrum, some companies have already begun to reposition themselves. One strategy is to beef up pricier ranges... Other businesses are reducing exposure to the shaky middle... A third strategy is to invest in offerings for the budget-conscious.

> The new logic of consumption suggests that the pedlars of the most essential fare can expect to do well as the economy sours. But so can sellers of the exceedingly discretionary.
