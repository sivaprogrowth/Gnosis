# The Science of Storytelling

## Abstract

Will Storr's synthesis of neuroscience, evolutionary psychology, and narrative craft into a unified account of why story works on humans. The central claim is that the brain runs a **[[controlled-hallucination]]** of reality, and story works by hijacking the model-building machinery the brain uses to construct that hallucination. From this foundation Storr derives a set of consequences: curiosity is an **[[information-gap-curiosity|information gap]]**, character is a **[[theory-of-control]]**, status play is **[[connect-and-dominate|connect-and-dominate]]**, and the gap between **[[wants-vs-needs|what a protagonist wants and what they need]]** is the single most reliable engine of plot. The book's headline framework, the **[[sacred-flaw-approach]]**, builds story from the protagonist's foundational flawed belief outward rather than from a plot inward. ## Key claims

- **The brain models reality, and story exploits the model-building machinery.** "The world we experience as 'out there' is actually a reconstruction of reality that is built inside our heads." Stories are accepted as real because they're processed by the same hallucination engine that constructs waking life. See [[controlled-hallucination]].
- **Curiosity is the perception of an incomplete information set.** Loewenstein's four routes: pose a puzzle; present a sequence with anticipated but unknown resolution; surprise with information that suggests a hidden whole; remind the subject of something they once knew but forgot. See [[information-gap-curiosity]].
- **Specific sensory detail builds the hallucination; abstraction collapses it.** "A cabbagey brown sock" defeats "a smelly sock" because the brain models concrete sensory pairs. Active voice ("Jane kissed Dad") outperforms passive because word order controls model-assembly order.
- **Character = theory of control.** Every protagonist has a learned theory of how the world works that lets them get what they want. Drama begins at the **ignition point** — the moment unexpected change cracks the theory. See [[theory-of-control]].
- **The sacred flaw is built from the protagonist's deepest mistaken belief about reality.** Storr's character-first method: identify the flawed core belief, derive everything (values, behaviors, desires, plot) from it. See [[sacred-flaw-approach]].
- **All status play is "connect and dominate."** Humans simultaneously want to get *along* with the tribe (connect) and get *ahead* of it (dominate). Heroism is altruistic punishment — costly signaling that defends tribal norms. Villainy is status-seeking that diverges from the tribe. See [[connect-and-dominate]].
- **Wants vs needs is the engine of multidimensional plot.** "The conscious and unconscious desires of a multidimensional protagonist contradict each other. What he believes he wants is the antithesis of what he actually but unwittingly needs." See [[wants-vs-needs]].
- **Story is tribal propaganda *and* the cure for tribal propaganda.** ~80% of hunter-gatherer stories contained behavioral lessons. Story enforces in-group morality, but is also the only mechanism we have for genuinely inhabiting another mind.
- **Cause-and-effect is the brain's native grammar.** Plots that violate cause-effect become confusing because they're not speaking the brain's language. Every scene is a cause that opens an information gap about its potential effects.
- **Humiliation = "annihilation of the self."** Removal of any ability to claim status is the most toxic psychological state and underlies disproportionate violence (spree shootings, honor killings). The reverse — watching deserving villains lose status — is correspondingly rapturous to audiences.

## Highlights — locations 129–650 (Part 1: Creating a World)

> "It's people, not events, that we're naturally interested in. It's the plight of specific, flawed and fascinating individuals that makes us cheer, weep and ram our heads into the sofa cushions." (loc. 129)

> "The 'Sacred Flaw Approach' is a character-first process, an attempt to create a story that mimics the various ways a brain creates a life, and which therefore feels true and fresh, and comes pre-loaded with conflict." (loc. 150)

> "Brains, concluded the researchers, seem to become spontaneously curious when presented with an 'information set' they realise is incomplete." (loc. 251)

> "Loewenstein breaks down four ways of involuntarily inducing curiosity in humans: (1) the 'posing of a question or presentation of a puzzle'; (2) 'exposure to a sequence of events with an anticipated but unknown resolution'…" (loc. 266)

> "The world we experience as 'out there' is actually a reconstruction of reality that is built inside our heads. It's an act of creation by the storytelling brain." (loc. 289)

> "We do know that actual reality is radically different than the model of it that we experience in our heads. For instance, there's no sound out there." (loc. 345)

> "We start modelling words as soon as we start reading them. We don't wait until we get to the end of the sentence. This means the order in which writers place their words matters." (loc. 384)

> "Active sentence construction – Jane kissed her Dad – is more effective than passive – Dad was kissed by Jane. Witnessing this in real life, Jane's initial movement would draw our attention…" (loc. 390)

> "All it takes is deployment of specific detail, with the sensory information ('a cabbagey') paired to visual information ('brown sock')." (loc. 415)

> "Brain scans illustrate the second, more powerful, use of metaphor. When participants in one study read the words 'he had a rough day', their neural regions involved in feeling textures became more active." (loc. 597)

> "Every scene in a compelling story is a cause that triggers our childlike curiosity about its potential effects." (loc. 693)

## Highlights — locations 650–1280 (Part 2: The Flawed Self)

> "We're insatiably inquisitive… Being a domesticated species, we're most interested of all in the cause and effect of other humans." (loc. 746)

> "What arouses our curiosity about them, and provides them with a dramatic battle to fight, is not their achievements or their winning smile. It's their flaws." (loc. 754)

> "Identifying and accepting our flaws, and then changing who we are, means breaking down the very structure of our reality before rebuilding it in a new and improved form. This is not easy. It's painful." (loc. 786)

> "Locked inside the black vault of our skulls, stuck forever in the solitude of our own hallucinated universe, story is a portal, a hallucination within the hallucination, the closest we'll ever really come to escaping the self." (loc. 855)

> "When designing a character, it's often useful to think of them in terms of their theory of control. How have they learned to control the world?" (loc. 857)

> "It is from character that flow goals, plans and actions." — Keith Oatley (loc. 889)

> "Storytellers can show the personality of their characters in almost everything they do: it's in their thoughts, dialogue, social behaviours, memories, desires and sadnesses." (loc. 914)

> "For the Greeks, the primary agent of control was the individual. For the Chinese, it was the group. For the Greeks, reality was made up of individual pieces and parts. For the Chinese, it was a field of relationships." (loc. 1024)

> "Smart people are mostly better at finding ways to 'prove' they're right and tend to be no better at detecting their wrongness." (loc. 1087)

> "Our brains are hero-makers that emit seductive lies. They want to make us feel like the plucky, brave protagonist in the story of our own lives." (loc. 1165)

> "A character's conviction in their rightness and superiority is precisely what gives them their terrible power. Great drama often forms itself around a clash of competing hero-maker narratives." (loc. 1232)

## Highlights — locations 1280–2200 (Part 3: The Dramatic Question)

> "Who is this person? This is the question all stories ask." (loc. 1315)

> "The terrible and fascinating truth about the human condition is that none of us really know the answer to the dramatic question as it pertains to ourselves." (loc. 1363)

> "The conscious and unconscious desires of a multidimensional protagonist contradict each other. What he believes he wants is the antithesis of what he actually but unwittingly needs." (loc. 1604)

> "We're wired to find selfless behaviour heroic and selfish deeds evil. Selflessness is thought to be the universal basis of all human morality." (loc. 1713)

> "Humans are driven to connect and dominate." (loc. 1764)

> "Getting ahead means gaining status, the craving for which is a human universal." (loc. 1767)

> "Heroes in archetypal stories are selfless costly signallers." (loc. 1753)

> "Psychologists define humiliation as the removal of any ability to claim status. Severe humiliation has been described as 'an annihilation of the self'. It's thought to be a uniquely toxic state…" (loc. 1882)

> "A recent study of eighteen hunter-gatherer tribes found almost eighty per cent of their stories contained lessons in how they should behave in their dealings with other people." (loc. 1925)

> "We willingly allow highly simplistic narratives to deceive us, gleefully accepting as truth any tale that casts us as the moral hero and the other as the two-dimensional villain." (loc. 2015)

## Highlights — locations 2200–2690 (Part 4: Plots, Endings, Meaning)

> "Our goals give our lives order, momentum and logic. They provide our hallucination of reality with a centre of narrative gravity." (loc. 2239)

> "When we push ourselves towards a tough yet meaningful goal, we thrive. Our reward systems spike not when we achieve what we're after but when we're in pursuit of it. It's the pursuit that makes a life and the pursuit that makes a plot." (loc. 2298)

> "A character in a drama who isn't reacting, making decisions, choosing and trying somehow to impose control on the chaos isn't truly a protagonist." (loc. 2308)

> "The job of the plot is to plot against the protagonist." (loc. 2313)

> "Act I: This is me, and it's not working… Act II: Is there another way?… Act III: There is another way… Act IV: But can I handle it?… Act V: Who am I really?" — John Yorke's five-act distillation (loc. 2360)

> "A gripping plot is one that keeps asking the dramatic question. It uses its story event to repeatedly change and gradually break the protagonist's model of who they are and how the world works before finally rebuilding it." (loc. 2406)

> "Story, then, is both tribal propaganda and the cure for tribal propaganda." (loc. 2558)

> "Follow the sacredness. Find out what people believe to be sacred, and when you look around there you will find rampant irrationality." — Jonathan Haidt, quoted (loc. 2677)
