## Abstract

This New York Times wellness article synthesises cognitive-science research to argue that deliberate, structured breaks from focused work are not a productivity loss but a prerequisite for sustained performance and creativity. Drawing on researchers Gloria Mark, Marta Sabariego, and Srini Pillay, as well as author Johann Hari, it explains how the brain's task-related network and default mode network alternate in ways that make continuous focus neurologically unsustainable. A 2022 systematic review in PLoS ONE is cited as evidence that even sub-10-minute breaks reduce mental fatigue and increase creative output. The article offers practical guidance on break timing, type of activity, phone use, napping, and nutrition.

## TL;DR

- Sustained focus depletes cognitive resources; short, intentional breaks measurably reduce mental fatigue and boost creativity, especially on tasks requiring divergent thinking.
- The brain's task-related network and default mode network are largely mutually exclusive — the default mode (daydreaming/mind-wandering) is essential for problem-solving and cannot be activated while multitasking.
- Effective breaks involve physical activity or low-demand "little mind" play (puzzles, knitting), not switching to email or social media on a phone.

## Key claims

- A 2022 systematic review in *PLoS ONE* found breaks of 10 minutes or fewer reduced mental fatigue and increased "vigor" (willingness to persist); longer breaks produced larger performance boosts, with the greatest gains on creative tasks.
- Gloria Mark's longitudinal research found knowledge workers' average time on a single screen tab fell from 75 seconds in 2012 to 44 seconds by 2020, suggesting accelerating attention fragmentation.
- Multitasking — rapidly toggling between applications — increases mistakes, reduces creativity, and causes fatigue, according to Johann Hari.
- Dopamine mediates focused attention: rewarding or challenging tasks produce dopamine that sustains focus, while menial tasks do not, making breaks more frequently necessary during low-stimulus work.
- The default mode network can retrieve memories and associations inaccessible to the logical/task-focused brain, explaining why creative insights often arrive during idle moments (e.g., the shower effect).
- A 20-minute walk in nature (studied in collaboration with Microsoft Research) improved workers' "divergent attention" — yielding more creative ideas than continuing to work.
- A 2013 study found 15-minute midday exercise breaks reduced worker stress and enhanced workplace social interaction ("booster breaks").
- Even a 5–15-minute nap can bring cognitive clarity, but a full ~90-minute rest reaching REM sleep is associated with increased creativity; a light snack (e.g., an apple) can also restore glucose to brain cells without triggering parasympathetic-driven sleepiness.

## Key passages

> "We can't expect to lift weights nonstop all day, and we can't expect to use sustained focus and attention for extended periods of time, either."
> — Gloria Mark

> "The default mode network can actually retrieve details from the nooks and crannies in your brain's memories that the logical brain cannot retrieve, which is why sometimes people say they have their best ideas in the shower."
> — Srini Pillay

> "We've devalued the idea of what a break is so that it's, 'I took a break from looking at my emails on my computer so I could take a walk and look at my emails on my phone.'"
> — Johann Hari

> "If you see something upsetting on Twitter, that can interrupt your task at hand. That's going to stick with you as you move on to something else."
> — Gloria Mark

> "I love this idea. The little mind allows your big mind to kind of refresh and replenish."
> — Gloria Mark, on Maya Angelou's "big mind / little mind" framework
