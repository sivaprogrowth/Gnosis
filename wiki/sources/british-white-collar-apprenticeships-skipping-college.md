## Abstract

A growing cohort of British high-school graduates are forgoing university — including offers from Oxford and Cambridge — in favour of white-collar apprenticeships at law firms, banks, consultancies, and the NHS. The shift is driven by the high cost of degrees, limited practical experience gained at university, and anxiety that AI will eliminate entry-level white-collar roles before graduates can establish themselves. Firms such as PricewaterhouseCoopers, Freshfields, Barclays, and Grant Thornton are expanding apprenticeship intakes, arguing that work-based training produces more employable, practically skilled candidates. The article positions the U.K.'s experience as a potential model for the U.S., where similar pressures on degree value are building but structured white-collar apprenticeship pathways remain scarce.

## TL;DR

- Top UK graduates are declining university places for employer-funded white-collar apprenticeships in law, finance, and consulting, citing AI disruption and student-debt risk.
- Major firms including PwC, Barclays, Freshfields, and Grant Thornton are scaling apprenticeship programmes to rival or match their graduate intakes.
- The trend offers a template for the U.S., where white-collar apprenticeships are still nascent despite $150 million in federal investment over the past decade.

## Key claims

- Rumaysah Khan declined an Oxford place to apprentice at law firm Eversheds Sutherland; her employer funded her law-school training and she earned a degree in April while being paid a reported £55,000–£65,000 salary range ($70,000–$80,000).
- Samuel Rankin turned down four university offers to join Barclays as a people transformation analyst via training provider Multiverse, completing a technology degree and now earning ~$50,000 as a project manager.
- Mary Amanuel declined five top UK universities to train as a data analyst with the NHS, where she contributed to machine-learning models for early cancer detection.
- At Grant Thornton, apprenticeship intakes have grown from 10% of UK trainees in 2010 to parity with graduate hires today.
- PwC now offers over 1,000 apprenticeship positions annually across consulting, tax, technology, and accounting.
- Apprentices cited AI disruption as a key reason: hands-on, firm-embedded roles are seen as harder to automate than entry-level graduate roles.
- U.S. federal investment in apprenticeships totals $150 million over the past decade, with $100 million directed at cybersecurity, AI, and healthcare — but most programmes remain blue-collar.
