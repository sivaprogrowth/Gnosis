## Abstract

This *Economist* article examines whether the United States has the institutional capacity to retrain workers displaced by AI before disruption outpaces the safety net. Drawing on Goldman Sachs projections of up to 10 million job losses over the next decade—concentrated in entry-level white-collar and administrative roles—the piece surveys America's fragmented, underfunded retraining infrastructure and finds it critically unprepared. It benchmarks the current system against the historical response to manufacturing job losses (Trade Adjustment Assistance), apprenticeship programmes in Germany, and emerging private initiatives such as RAISE US (co-founded by former Commerce Secretary Gina Raimondo). The article argues that a serious response must simultaneously help young workers enter the workforce without four-year degrees, help incumbent workers adapt to evolving job requirements, and provide meaningful pathways for displaced workers into entirely new occupations—something no current programme achieves at scale.

## TL;DR

- America's retraining system was built for brief unemployment spells, not repeated career transitions driven by AI, leaving tens of millions of workers structurally exposed.
- Federal spending on displaced-worker training is negligible—$170m for ~39,000 people in 2023—while AI firms plan to spend thousands of times more on compute than on workforce programmes.
- Promising models exist (sectoral training, apprenticeships, employer-led skilling), but none yet operate at the scale the coming disruption demands.

## Key claims

- Goldman Sachs estimates roughly 10 million U.S. jobs could be displaced by AI over the next decade, with entry-level white-collar and routine office work most exposed.
- Nearly one in five U.S. workers already expects AI to eliminate their job within five years, shaping political sentiment even before mass layoffs materialise.
- Trade Adjustment Assistance (TAA), the main historical precedent, covered only ~160,000 workers per year during a period when manufacturing shed ~500,000 jobs annually; TAA closed to new applicants in 2022.
- The principal federal training programme for displaced workers spent just $170m on approximately 39,000 people in 2023—far short of projected need.
- Randomised trials of sectoral training programmes in health care, IT support and manufacturing found lasting earnings gains of 11–40%.
- The U.S. has only ~800,000 registered apprentices—barely half Germany's figure despite having four times the population—and they are concentrated in construction and trades rather than white-collar sectors.
- [[companies/anthropic|Anthropic]] and the OpenAI Foundation have committed $200m and $250m respectively to workforce-navigation programmes, but OpenAI alone plans to spend ~3,000 times that sum on computing power through 2030.
- [[companies/salesforce|Salesforce]] offers an internal skill-mapping and training-reimbursement platform as an early model of employer-led retraining, but such platforms remain rare across industry.

## Key passages

> "Around 10m jobs could be displaced over the next decade, according to Goldman Sachs, a bank. Entry-level white-collar jobs and routine office work are especially exposed, putting both new and current workers at risk."

> "Our whole system is predicated on funding based on attendance, not outcomes." — Gina Raimondo, former U.S. Commerce Secretary

> "There is a cost to people's egos. It is not realistic to take someone with a 20-year career in a white-collar setting and ask, 'Would you like to become a welder?'" — Lee Lilley, North Carolina's commerce secretary

> "If we don't get this right, we won't lead the world in AI. There will be regulatory backlash, which is bad for the companies." — Gina Raimondo

> "Anthropic and the Open AI Foundation have committed $200m and $250m, respectively, to research and programmes such as RAISE US to help workers and economies navigate AI disruption. By way of comparison, Open AI plans to spend 3,000 times that sum on computing power through 2030."
