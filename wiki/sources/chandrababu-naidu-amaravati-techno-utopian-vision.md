## Abstract

This article profiles Chandrababu Naidu, the 76-year-old chief minister of Andhra Pradesh, India, and his sweeping ambition to build Amaravati — the largest greenfield capital city in Indian history — and reposition his agrarian, bifurcated state as a hub for advanced manufacturing, data centres, and quantum computing. Naidu, who previously orchestrated Hyderabad's rise as a tech centre in the late 1990s, is now attempting to replicate that success on a far more difficult canvas: a state stripped of its former capital and economic engine after Telangana's 2014 creation. The piece assesses both the genuine momentum he has generated — including a landmark [[companies/google]] AI data-centre and [[companies/adani-group]] joint venture — and the serious risks of financial overextension, political fragility, and the structural difficulty of bending economic geography to political will. Experts and precedents from other greenfield city projects worldwide cast doubt on whether Amaravati can fulfil its "Quantum Valley" ambitions on the timeline Naidu envisions.

## TL;DR

- Chandrababu Naidu is building Amaravati as India's biggest-ever greenfield city, pitching Andhra Pradesh as a destination for advanced manufacturing, data centres, and quantum computing.
- The project has attracted major anchors including a 1 GW [[companies/google]] AI data centre and IBM, but rests on shaky state finances, fragile coalition politics, and the speculative economics of land-price appreciation.
- Scholars of greenfield cities warn that such projects routinely underdeliver, and the quantum computing bet in particular is years from commercialisation.

## Key claims

- Naidu credits his early courting of Bill Gates with catalysing Hyderabad's tech boom, framing investor signalling as a core tool of statecraft.
- Andhra Pradesh was left without a capital or economic engine after the 2014 bifurcation that created Telangana and gave Hyderabad to the new state.
- The Amaravati master plan covers 33,000 acres, is Singapore-inspired, and involves 25,000 workers operating around the clock across 95 active construction sites.
- Financing for Amaravati relies primarily on $1.6 bn in long-term loans from the [[companies/world-bank]] and the Asian Development Bank, to be repaid through land sales contingent on rising land prices.
- A [[companies/google]] 1 GW AI data-centre project in Visakhapatnam — described as the largest such scheme in India — was secured partly through Naidu's alliance with Gautam Adani and, by extension, Prime Minister Narendra Modi.
- Naidu's coalition leverage stems from the [[companies/bharatiya-janata-party]] lacking an outright parliamentary majority nationally, making his regional party's support critical — a balance that could shift after any election.
- Scholars who study greenfield cities globally warn that hundreds of such projects have ended as ghost cities, and that even capable leaders cannot easily override economic geography.
- Naidu's quantum computing strategy carries a technology-choice risk: IBM's particular quantum approach could result in lock-in if a rival paradigm prevails.

## Key passages

> "In 1998 Microsoft snubbed Bangalore to establish its Indian base in Hyderabad, then the state's capital. Today the city has 10,000-plus Microsoft employees and a big airport, and has doubled in population. More than as an investor, Mr Naidu saw Mr Gates as a signal that would propel Hyderabad's rise."

> "A dizzying ambition was born. As chief minister of the rump state, Mr Naidu planned a shining new capital called Amaravati, the biggest greenfield city in India's history, and among the biggest under way anywhere."

> "'He's trying to punch above his weight,' says Parakala Prabhakar, a political economist. The bulk of the financing for the city is $1.6bn in long-term loans from the World Bank and the Asian Development Bank, which will be paid back by selling off land. The maths relies on land prices soaring, which in turn depends on the city attracting foreign investors, at a time when India as a whole is struggling to do this."

> "'These projects always look like utopias in expensive 3D renderings,' says Sarah Moser of McGill University, who has compiled a database of 300 greenfield cities worldwide. Many end up half-empty, some as ghost cities."

> "Amaravati's fortunes rest on a delicate political balance. If Mr Naidu loses power, his opponents would have different priorities. For now he has the clout to push it forward because the BJP holds power nationally but is short of a parliamentary majority."

> "Even as investment pours into AI, many experts think that quantum computers will have a complementary role, partly by performing some tasks more efficiently than large language models. Mr Naidu wants Amaravati to manufacture components. But the technology is years from commercialisation."
