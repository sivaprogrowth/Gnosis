## Abstract

This *Economist* article examines China's standing in the generative-AI race against America, arguing that while China leads on AI publication volume and computer-vision benchmarks, it lags two to three years behind America in foundation-model building due to three compounding shortfalls: limited internet-scale training data in Chinese, US export controls restricting access to advanced chips, and dwindling flows of AI talent. The article then weighs the workarounds available to China — open-source model weights, chip intermediaries, state data releases, and cross-lingual transfer learning — and concludes that the modelling gap may eventually close. Even so, America's deeper structural advantage lies not in model quality alone but in its superior ability to diffuse cutting-edge innovation throughout the broader economy, an advantage reinforced by China's regulatory censorship burden, state-enterprise inertia, and a chilled private investment climate following Xi Jinping's tech crackdown.

## TL;DR

- China is roughly 2–3 years behind America in foundation-model capability, held back by data scarcity, US chip export controls, and talent attrition.
- The gap may narrow via open-source models, chip workarounds, and cross-lingual transfer — but it won't necessarily translate into an economic or military edge.
- America's decisive long-run advantage is technology diffusion across the economy, not model leadership alone.

## Key claims

- China surpassed America in share of highly cited AI papers in 2019 and accounts for 26% of global AI conference publications vs. America's 17% (2021), yet still lags in foundation models.
- Only 1.5% of websites are in Mandarin versus 56% in English, and China's mobile super-app "walled gardens" (WeChat, Weibo) make large volumes of Chinese content inaccessible for model training.
- More than half of 26 major Chinese AI models analysed by the Centre for the Governance of AI depended on [[companies/nvidia|Nvidia]] chips, exposing deep hardware dependency.
- [[companies/tsmc|TSMC]]-equivalent mass-production capability at SMIC is estimated to lag three to four years behind, limiting China's domestic chip alternative.
- Open-source model weights (e.g., Meta's LLaMA and Stanford's Alpaca, costing under $600 to fine-tune) could substantially lower the barrier for Chinese labs and partially offset US export controls.
- China has form as a "fast follower" — absorbing international research advances rapidly with flush state resources — making an unbridgeable US lead in model capability unlikely.
- Private AI investment in China reached only $13.5bn in 2022, less than one-third of US levels, with the gap widening in early 2023 after Xi's tech crackdown.
- Technology diffusion — not model quality — decided the US-Soviet tech race, and America's edge in cloud computing, business software, and sensor deployment makes a similar dynamic likely in AI.

## Key passages

> "This leads those in the know to conclude that China is two or three years behind America in foundation-model building."

> "American model-builders benefit from the fact that 56% of all websites are in English, whereas just 1.5% are in Mandarin or China's other languages… The Chinese interact with the internet primarily through mobile super-apps like WeChat and Weibo. These are 'walled gardens', so much of their content is not indexed on search engines."

> "Alpaca, a model built by researchers at Stanford University using the weights from LLaMA, a foundation model created by Meta, was made for less than $600, compared with sums on the order of $100m for training something like GPT-4."

> "Matt Sheehan of the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace… says that China has form in being a 'fast follower'—its labs have absorbed advances from abroad and then rapidly incorporated them into their own models, often with flush state resources. A prominent Silicon Valley venture capitalist is more blunt, calling open-source models a gift to the Communist Party."

> "It was, after all, more efficient diffusion of technology that helped America open up a technological lead over the Soviet Union, which in the 1950s was producing twice as many science PhDs as its democratic adversary."

> "China's private sector may find it hard to take full advantage of generative AI, especially if the Communist Party imposes heavy regulations to prevent chatbots from saying something its censors do not like."
