## Abstract

Written by Natalia Quintero, head of Every Consulting, this article diagnoses why most companies fail to capture financial value from AI despite widespread tool adoption. Drawing on training engagements with thousands of employees at firms including the New York Times, Ripple, Headway, and Thumbtack, as well as investment firms managing over $100 billion in assets, the author argues that the bottleneck has shifted from model capability to organisational capability. The piece introduces a five-step implementation loop — executive fluency, AI champions, painful workflow selection, building to 95% reliability, and scaling wins — framed as a 60-day plan. It situates this guidance within three historical waves of enterprise AI adoption (license, prompt, and implementation) and references McKinsey data showing that only 6% of surveyed organisations qualify as AI high performers.

## TL;DR

- Enterprise AI adoption has entered a third "implementation wave" where prompt libraries are being replaced by skills libraries, agents, evals, and owned workflows — but most organisations haven't kept up.
- The bottleneck for AI value is no longer model capability but organisational capability; leaders who haven't personally used AI lack the practical understanding needed to guide adoption.
- A five-step loop — executive fluency → AI champions → one painful workflow → 95% reliability → scale — is presented as a repeatable playbook for closing the gap.

## Key claims

- Only 6% of the ~2,000 organisations surveyed by [[companies/google|McKinsey]] meet the criteria for AI high performers (significant value + >5% EBIT impact), yet those organisations are nearly 3× as likely to have fundamentally redesigned their workflows.
- Enterprise AI adoption has moved through three distinct waves: the **license wave** (buying access and waiting), the **prompt wave** (prompt libraries and custom GPTs), and the current **implementation wave** (skills libraries, agents, evals, named workflow owners).
- Executive AI fluency is a prerequisite for effective company-wide implementation; leaders who haven't used the tools directly cannot accurately assess practical opportunities or challenges.
- An automation that works 80% of the time is characterised as "a demo"; genuine production automation requires gold-standard examples, structured evals, human review gates, and a named owner — targeting 90–95% reliability.
- AI champions should be operators (not strategists) given protected time of at least two days per month, a clear mandate, and the goal of moving workflows from "works in a demo" to "works in production."
- Workflow selection should start with something frequent, data-rich, and narrow enough to test in a week, chosen by champions who know where the most tedious work lives.
- One visible organisational win creates pull for further adoption; show-and-tells and adjacent team training are the key scaling mechanisms.
