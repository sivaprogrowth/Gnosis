## Abstract

This article from Collaborative Fund argues that optimism about human progress is chronically underestimated because compound growth is deeply counterintuitive. Drawing on biology (Muller's Ratchet and sexual recombination), business history (Jobs, Bezos, Buffett, Edison, and their intellectual predecessors), and science writer Matt Ridley's concept of ideas "having sex," the author builds a theory that progress is not driven by lone genius but by the mixing, tweaking, and compounding of many small ideas over time. The piece contends that if one reframes progress as an accumulative, combinatorial process rather than a product of individual brilliance, a dramatically better future becomes far more conceivable and achievable.

## TL;DR

- Progress compounds the same way genes do through recombination — small ideas mix, mutate, and build on each other to produce outsized results.
- Pessimism thrives because compound growth is unintuitive; what looks like a giant leap often requires only modest incremental improvement sustained over time.
- The chain of influence (Faraday → Edison, Graham → Buffett, Land → Jobs) shows that innovators rarely know the downstream impact of their ideas, yet the compounding happens regardless.

## Key claims

- [[Muller's Ratchet]] (the evolutionary principle that dangerous mutations pile up without genetic recombination) serves as a biological analogy for why idea-mixing is essential to progress.
- Great innovators consistently build on predecessors: Steve Jobs drew from Edwin Land and Akio Morita; Jeff Bezos drew from Sam Walton; Warren Buffett learned from Ben Graham and Phil Fisher; Thomas Edison built on Michael Faraday.
- Dee Hock's observation that "a book is far more than what the author wrote" illustrates how ideas can inspire outcomes entirely unintended and unforeseen by their originators.
- Matt Ridley's concept of ideas "having sex" posits that most innovations are combinations of previously mundane components (e.g., plastic + electronics + metal = iPhone).
- Compound growth makes ambitious-sounding goals (e.g., doubling average income) mathematically routine: doubling over 30 years requires only ~2.3% annual growth.
- Pessimism is structurally easier than optimism precisely because human intuition struggles to perceive compounding; end results look miraculous even when constituent steps were modest.
- When progress is viewed as combinatorial and incremental rather than the product of individual genius, an optimistic future becomes much easier to believe in.

## Key passages

> "You're never going to find anybody who gets to the top of the profession without studying the people that came before them and learning from them and admiring them."

> "A book is far more than what the author wrote; it is everything you can imagine and read into it as well."

> "A mutation that occurs in one creature can join forces with a mutation that occurs in another. If microbes had not begun swapping genes a few billion years ago, and animals had not continued doing so through sex, all the genes that make eyes could never have got together in one animal; nor the genes to make legs or nerves or brains."

> "What's relevant to a society is how well people are communicating their ideas, and how well they're cooperating, not how clever the individuals are."

> "If you view progress as being driven by the genius of individuals, of course it's hard to imagine a future where things are dramatically better, because no individual is orders of magnitudes smarter than average."

> "When you view it as one person coming up with a small idea, another person copying that idea and tweaking it a little… incremental, tiny bits, little ideas mixing, joining, blending, mutating, and compounding together – it's suddenly much more conceivable."
