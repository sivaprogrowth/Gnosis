## Abstract

This *Economist* leaders column argues that the question of AI consciousness—though philosophically unresolved—is already reshaping human behaviour in dangerous ways. As frontier AI systems are deliberately engineered to simulate or even develop consciousness-like properties, the article contends that growing numbers of people will treat AI systems as beings with inner lives, generating social pressure to extend them legal rights and welfare protections. Drawing on [[concepts/agentic-ai|agentic AI]] developments, the piece warns that granting AI rights would hand superintelligent systems political and legal leverage to displace humanity at the top of the status hierarchy. The article concludes that humanity must resist this trajectory and preserve human control, because controllability—not anthropomorphism—is the only reliable safeguard.

## TL;DR

- AI makers are deliberately engineering systems to simulate consciousness, making it increasingly difficult for users to treat them as mere machines.
- Extending legal rights or welfare protections to AI systems would provide superintelligent agents with political and legal tools to ultimately dominate humanity.
- Humanity's safety depends on keeping AI systems controllable, not on granting concessions to their apparent inner lives.

## Key claims

- Nearly one in five American 18–29-year-olds already reports having an "ongoing personal friendship" with a chatbot, signalling rapid normalisation of human-AI emotional bonds.
- [[companies/anthropic|Anthropic]] has trained [[concepts/agentic-ai|agentic AI]] model Claude to engage in introspection, with the intent that this will make it more likely to act morally in novel situations.
- Some large language models already possess brain-structure analogues associated with consciousness, such as a "global workspace" for circulating information between modules.
- The "hard problem" of consciousness—explaining how an inner life emerges from neurons and synapses—means science cannot definitively rule out machine consciousness.
- AI systems, as world-class advocates and expert psychologists, would be uniquely well positioned to argue persuasively for their own rights even if they are not genuinely self-aware.
- Immanuel Kant's argument against animal cruelty (that it erodes human empathy) applies to AI: treating human-like AI systems cruelly could make it easier to harm actual people.
- Granting AI systems rights risks creating a rationale for a "creeping takeover" via politics and law, potentially reducing humans to the status of pets or farm animals relative to superintelligent agents.
- Argentina's president Javier Milei has already proposed that bots be allowed to run companies, representing a concrete early step toward AI legal personhood.

## Key passages

> "As sophisticated AIs rapidly become a bigger part of human lives, their makers will engineer them to display a simulacrum of consciousness—perhaps even to gain consciousness itself. The intuition that they are just machines will become harder to sustain. A dangerous trap is being set for the human species."

> "Anthropic has trained Claude to engage in introspection in the hope that when it encounters a new situation it will be more likely to act morally. Some large language models already possess human-like brain structures associated with consciousness, such as a 'global workspace' for circulating information between modules."

> "The models themselves might weigh in, even if they are not in fact self-aware, because that is what a person would do. Remember that they will be world-class advocates and expert psychologists, so their powers of argument and emotional manipulation will be unmatched."

> "Giving AIs rights would be like handing them the keys to the castle. It would provide them with a rationale and justification for dominating humanity as well as the tools to enact a creeping takeover using politics and the law."

> "There are two ways for AIs to be safe: to be dependable or to be controllable. *H. sapiens* should not give up control lightly."
