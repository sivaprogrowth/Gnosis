## Abstract

This *Financial Times* review by Jane Shaw (professor of the history of religion at Oxford) covers Martin Puchner's *Culture: A New World History*, a wide-ranging survey tracing cultural borrowing, grafting, and revival from cave paintings to K-Pop. Puchner argues that originality is always indebted — what matters is not *what* we borrow but *how* we remake it — and that culture is forged through "engagement across barriers of time and place" rather than being a form of property. Shaw finds the book compelling in its examples and prose but notes that Puchner sidesteps thornier questions, particularly why cultural appropriation has become so contested today and what modern hierarchies of culture owe to colonialism and anthropology.

## TL;DR

- Puchner's thesis is that culture has always advanced through cross-fertilisation, not originality, and that treating culture as property is historically mistaken.
- The book moves chronologically across 15 chapters, drawing examples from Pompeii, medieval Germany, Aztec Mexico, the Enlightenment, and K-Pop.
- The reviewer admires the ambition but flags gaps — notably Picasso's use of "primitive" art and an under-theorised account of why appropriation debates are so fierce now.

## Key claims

- A small ivory statue of the goddess Lakshmi found at Pompeii illustrates Rome's absorption of Indian cultural objects, while Roman coins found in India show reciprocal exchange.
- Puchner contends that "everyone is influenced by someone; every instance of originality can be traced to things borrowed from others," and that what matters is "not *what* we borrow, but *how* we borrow."
- The book explicitly argues against the conception of culture as "a form of property" that belongs exclusively to those who live it.
- Historical examples of layered borrowing include: Virgil borrowing Greek epic for Rome; Xuanzang carrying Buddhist texts from India to China; Sultan Firoz Shah Tughlaq relocating an Ashokan pillar to Delhi; Ashoka himself likely borrowing the inscribed-pillar idea from Persian travellers.
- Hildegard of Bingen is presented as evidence that institutional structures (the scriptorium) could enable cultural production that exceeded normal social constraints on women.
- Puchner acknowledges culture is "not always a pretty story," illustrated by the violence underpinning Spanish colonisation of the Aztec world, while the Mexico City inscription frames it as "the painful birth of the *mestizo* people."
- Shaw faults the book for not fully explaining why cultural syncretism has been so "profoundly challenged in our own day," despite chapters on anthropology, museums, and colonial hierarchies.

## Key passages

> "Everyone is influenced by someone; every instance of originality can be traced to things borrowed from others."

> For Puchner, culture is always forged by "engagement across barriers of time and place, of surprising connections and subterranean influences" — though he admits that is "not always a pretty story."

> "This [battle] was neither victory nor defeat. It was the painful birth of the *mestizo* people that is Mexico today."

> "Each generation must work through the uneasy entanglement of destruction and creation that cultural contact produces."

> "not *what* we borrow, but *how* we borrow, what we make of what we find"
