## Abstract

Written by founder and executive coach Casey Rosengren on Every, this article argues that great leaders must explicitly articulate a worldview—a structured belief system answering fundamental philosophical questions—rather than leaving it implicit in their organization's culture. Drawing on the academic framework developed by religious studies scholar Ann Taves (and originally theorized by Immanuel Kant), Rosengren maps six philosophical domains—ontology, cosmology, epistemology, axiology, praxeology, and eschatology—onto practical leadership questions. The article uses Rosengren's own travel company, Hacker Paradise, as a running case study to show where implicit beliefs went unarticulated and what was lost as a result. The central claim is that leaders who intentionally share meaning make it easier for everyone in an organization to find meaning for themselves, functioning like the lead bird in a "V" formation.

## TL;DR

- Leaders almost always have an implicit worldview embedded in their organization; making it explicit is a high-leverage act for creating shared meaning and purpose.
- The six philosophical domains—ontology, cosmology, epistemology, axiology, praxeology, and eschatology—provide a ready-made scaffold for articulating that worldview.
- Communicating a worldview works best through story rather than lists; the goal is shared meaning-making, not imposing belief.

## Key claims

- A worldview is a belief system that answers "Big Questions" across six philosophical domains: ontology, cosmology, epistemology, axiology, praxeology, and eschatology.
- Most organizations operate on an *implicit* worldview ("way of life"); failing to make it explicit causes members to either project their own meanings or find none at all.
- As organizations scale and new members lose direct founder contact, the meaning vacuum created by an unarticulated worldview becomes progressively more damaging.
- Epistemological balance is a core leadership paradox: founders must hold a strong enough vision to motivate others while remaining self-aware enough to not be captured by their own hype (citing WeWork as a cautionary example).
- Eschatology—connecting organizational work to mortality and "matters of ultimate concern" (Paul Tillich)—is described as a "force multiplier" for meaning-making.
- CrossFit's explicit framing around aging and physical independence is offered as a real-world example of organizational eschatology done well.
- Max Depree's dictum that "the first responsibility of a leader is to define reality" anchors the article's conclusion that worldview articulation is a core leadership duty.
- Stories are more effective than belief-lists for transmitting a worldview; this mirrors how most religious traditions convey teaching through myth and narrative rather than commandments alone.

## Key passages

> "Much like the bird at the front of the 'V' makes flying easier for the rest of the flock, leaders who intentionally articulate and share meaning make it easier for everyone else in an organization to find meaning for themselves."

> "If leaders fail to do this work, members will either project their own meanings onto the organization or will fail to mark it as meaningful altogether. As an organization grows and new members no longer get facetime with the founder, this problem only gets worse."

> "This is the great paradox of the founder—needing to thread the needle between having a strong enough vision to motivate others and being self-aware enough to not get lost in one's own hype."

> "Honoring mortality is a force multiplier to meaning-making, as it connects your organization's story to what philosopher Paul Tillich called 'matters of ultimate concern'—things that pertain to the ultimate drama of life and death."

> "Max Depree, former CEO of furniture company Herman Miller, once wrote that 'the first responsibility of a leader is to define reality.'"

> "While religions do typically contain lists of beliefs … the bulk of the teaching in most traditions is wrapped up in myths and stories."
