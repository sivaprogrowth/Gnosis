## Abstract

Paul Graham argues that the single most important and most neglected early-stage startup tactic is doing things that are deliberately unscalable — recruiting users by hand, over-engaging with individual customers, manufacturing your own hardware, or even being your own software. He illustrates this thesis through examples including Stripe's "Collison installation," Airbnb's door-to-door recruiting in New York, Facebook's deliberately narrow initial market, and Wufoo's hand-written thank-you notes. The essay challenges the widespread founder assumption that startups grow organically if the product is good, contending instead that founders must personally crank the engine before it gains its own momentum. Treating a startup idea as a vector — the product you build *plus* the unscalable bootstrapping actions — is offered as a mental model that forces founders to plan for both dimensions of early effort.

## TL;DR

- Startups don't take off on their own — founders must manually recruit early users and over-deliver on their experience before any organic growth can kick in.
- Unscalable tactics (hand-holding customers, self-manufacturing, acting as consultants) are not just permissible shortcuts; they embed crucial feedback loops and cultural DNA into the company.
- Big launches and big partnerships almost never substitute for the slow, laborious, one-at-a-time work of early user acquisition.

## Key claims

- The default startup growth assumption — build a better product and users will come — is wrong; founders must actively push to get the engine started.
- Manual user recruitment via direct, personal outreach (the "Collison installation" technique) is the most commonly neglected yet most universally necessary early tactic.
- Compound weekly growth (e.g., 10% per week) makes even tiny initial numbers consequential — 100 users growing 10%/week becomes ~2 million after two years.
- Over-engaging with early users is not merely permissible but a necessary part of the feedback loop that actually makes the product good.
- Deliberately limiting to a narrow initial market (the "contained fire" strategy) lets startups achieve critical mass quickly, as Facebook did by starting with Harvard students only.
- Hardware startups should self-assemble their early units ("pulling a Meraki") to bypass minimum factory order requirements and learn design lessons they couldn't otherwise discover.
- B2B founders should treat their first user as if they were consulting exclusively for that user, using the relationship as a mold to shape the product.
- Being your own software (manually performing what will eventually be automated) lets you launch faster and builds the muscle memory needed to design the right automation later.
- Big launches and corporate partnerships are largely ineffective for generating early growth; what matters is how happy the first users are, not how many there are.

## Key passages

> "Actually startups take off because the founders make them take off. There may be a handful that just grew by themselves, but usually it takes some sort of push to get them going."

> "When anyone agreed to try Stripe they'd say 'Right then, give me your laptop' and set them up on the spot."

> "The question to ask about an early stage startup is not 'is this company taking over the world?' but 'how big could this company get if the founders did the right things?'"

> "I have never once seen a startup lured down a blind alley by trying too hard to make their initial users happy."

> "It's not the product that should be insanely great, but the experience of being your user. The product is just one component of that."

> "In the best case, both components of the vector contribute to your company's DNA: the unscalable things you have to do to get started are not merely a necessary evil, but change the company permanently for the better."
