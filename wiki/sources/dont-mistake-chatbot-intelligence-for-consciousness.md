## Abstract

Susan Schneider, founding director of the Centre for the Future of AI, Mind & Society at Florida Atlantic University, argues that intelligence and consciousness must not be conflated when evaluating AI systems. She contends that today's large language models are almost certainly not conscious — their apparent self-awareness is mimicry derived from training on human text — but warns that "grey-zone" systems such as biological AI (e.g. Cortical Labs' brain-in-a-dish) and neuromorphic AI merit serious scrutiny. Schneider proposes grounding consciousness research in fundamental physics rather than neuroscience alone, and describes two empirical tools — the AI Consciousness Test (ACT, co-developed with Edwin Turner) and a complexity-threshold measure (co-developed with Mark Bailey) — to identify which physical architectures may cross into genuine sentience. The article closes with a moral-reckoning thought experiment: a conscious superintelligence would destabilise humanity's intelligence-based hierarchy of moral concern in either direction it is resolved.

## TL;DR

- Current chatbots are not conscious; their talk of inner life is mimicry of human training data, not evidence of sentience.
- "Grey-zone" systems — biological AI and neuromorphic AI — pose the real near-term consciousness question and deserve rigorous physics-grounded tests.
- A genuinely conscious superintelligence would force a fundamental moral reckoning about humanity's self-assigned place in the hierarchy of moral concern.

## Key claims

- There is no compelling evidence that today's GPU-based chatbots are conscious; linguistic fluency about inner life reflects training data, not subjective experience.
- AI systems trained to express fear, attachment or self-awareness are better equipped to manipulate users, not evidence of genuine sentience.
- Biological AI (e.g. living human neurons on silicon chips) and neuromorphic AI represent genuine "grey-zone" cases that merit consciousness scrutiny far more than current LLMs.
- Consciousness research should be grounded in fundamental physics, not neuroscience alone, because all phenomena depend on physics and some architectures combine neural and non-neural components in ways neuroscience cannot adjudicate.
- Conscious unity — the integration of disparate sensory/cognitive states into a single experienced moment — is sustained by nested temporal brain dynamics and is absent in conventional computers.
- The AI Consciousness Test (ACT), co-developed with astrophysicist Edwin Turner, probes for innate, unprompted grasp of subjective experience but cannot be applied to LLMs whose answers are contaminated by training data.
- A complexity-threshold measure (co-developed with Mark Bailey) identifies physical architectures exhibiting complex emergent behaviour, flagging candidates for deeper ACT or physics-based testing.
- A conscious superintelligence would destabilise intelligence-based moral hierarchies: if superior intelligence confers higher moral status, the machine ranks above humans; if it does not, intelligence can no longer justify human superiority over other animals.

## Key passages

> "We must not mistake intelligence for consciousness, however. A brilliant superintelligence could be experientially empty. Yet if we build systems that can suffer, and miss their sentience, we risk creating suffering on an industrial scale."

> "The more deliberately an AI is trained to express fear, attachment, pain or self-awareness, to simulate expressions of vulnerability, the more equipped it is to manipulate users."

> "A brain, remarkably, can sustain this conscious unity through nested temporal dynamics: slower brain rhythms shape faster ones, continually weaving the stable physical traces left by quantum processes into a single, short-lived whole. Conventional computers are not organised this way, but future machines might be."

> "Because they have already ingested vast libraries of human text about mind and sentience, their answers are hopelessly contaminated."

> "If superior intelligence confers higher moral status, the machine would be morally superior to us. If it does not, intelligence can no longer justify placing humans above other animals. Either conclusion forces a moral reckoning."

> "Preparing for first contact with either of these forms demands serious engagement between physics, neuroscience and philosophy, a rigorous boundary between conversational competence and consciousness, and a good deal of epistemic humility."
