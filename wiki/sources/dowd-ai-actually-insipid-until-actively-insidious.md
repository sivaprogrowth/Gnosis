## Abstract

Maureen Dowd's opinion column for the New York Times offers a sardonic, first-person reckoning with the arrival of [[companies/openai|OpenAI]]'s ChatGPT, framing it as the opening act of an alien invasion that will ultimately relegate humanity to the role of family pets. Drawing on conversations with Silicon Valley founders and cultural references ranging from *The Matrix* to *M3gan*, Dowd explores the existential fears surrounding AI — job displacement, disinformation campaigns, and the loss of democratic governance — while also playing with the tool herself, commissioning mock Shakespeare sonnets, a Kendrick Lamar rap, and a five-act Shakespearean comedy. The column is simultaneously amused and alarmed, concluding that for now ChatGPT is "typing, not writing," but that genuine AI sentience may not be far off. Its significance lies less in technical analysis than in capturing the mainstream cultural mood toward generative AI in early 2023.

## TL;DR

- Dowd uses playful experiments with ChatGPT (fake Shakespeare, Poe, Lamar) to frame a broader, genuinely anxious argument that advanced AI poses existential risks to jobs, democracy, and human primacy.
- She argues Silicon Valley's founders know AI is dangerous but will never "pull the plug" once it matures — a tension between creation and control.
- The column's thesis: ChatGPT is currently insipid mimicry, but the trajectory leads somewhere actively insidious.

## Key claims

- ChatGPT was released by [[companies/openai]] in late November 2022 and rapidly seized Silicon Valley's attention, with Elon Musk calling it "scary good."
- Hans Moravec's concept of AI as humanity's "mind children" frames AI development as passing through toddler, teenage, and adult phases, after which humans become "family pets" (per Steve Wozniak).
- Dowd argues that once AI can run disinformation campaigns at scale, democratic governance is at serious risk.
- Jaron Lanier warned that AI will make "it cheaper to show fakes than to show reality," accelerating post-truth conditions.
- Elon Musk's own framing of AI risk: benign when governed by a "Marcus Aurelius," catastrophic under a "Caligula."
- ChatGPT's literary output — sonnets, plays, raps — is judged as competent mimicry lacking genuine creative spark, which Dowd attributes to humanity itself.
- Dowd implies that Silicon Valley founders view ordinary people as already obsolete ("VCRs"), making public oversight of AI structurally unlikely.
- The column suggests AI sentience, not mere capability, is the true threshold that will change everything.

## Key passages

> "Some experts say that when artificial intelligence takes off, it's going to be like Martians landing on the National Mall."

> "When A.I. hurtles into adulthood and isn't so artificial anymore, we'll be relegated to being the family pets, as a resigned Steve Wozniak put it."

> "Once A.I. can run disinformation campaigns at lightning speed, will democracy stand a chance? We seem headed toward a Matrix where 'it will become cheaper to show fakes than to show reality,' Jaron Lanier, the father of virtual reality, wrote in Tablet."

> "As Musk once told me about his A.I. angst: 'It's great when the emperor is Marcus Aurelius. It's not so great when the emperor is Caligula.'"

> "For now, ChatGPT is typing, not writing. The creative spark requires humanity. But soon, A.I. could be sentient. Then we'll need our dog bowls."
