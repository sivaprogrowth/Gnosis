## Abstract

This *Economist* essay situates the rise of large language models (LLMs) and generative AI within a long arc of civilisational disruption, arguing that the technology need not be existentially dangerous to be profoundly world-changing. Drawing on three historical analogues — the web browser, the printing press, and Freudian psychoanalysis — the piece explores how LLMs may transform not only computing infrastructure and economic models, but also humanity's relationship to knowledge and to itself. It engages seriously with competing views on whether LLMs exhibit genuine understanding or merely sophisticated pattern-matching, and concludes that the uncanny quality of AI-generated pseudocognition may reshape self-conception even as most people adapt pragmatically. The essay urges attention to both the disinformation threat posed by infinite AI-generated content and the deeper philosophical questions raised about the nature of mind, creativity, and moral responsibility for AI "offspring."

## TL;DR

- LLMs are more plausibly compared to transformative predecessor technologies (browser, printing press, psychoanalysis) than to extinction-level threats.
- The hallucination problem and infinite disinformation supply represent clear and present dangers, even if human extinction does not.
- AI's most profound impact may be philosophical: forcing a re-examination of what is distinctively human about cognition, creativity, and inner life.

## Key claims

- The existential-risk framing around current generative AI (GPT-4-era LLMs) is disproportionate; the technology produces prose, poetry, images and code but lacks the attributes needed to "control civilisation."
- The browser analogy illuminates how LLMs may dissolve the distinction between user interface and software application, enabling natural-language prompts to generate executable code on demand.
- The printing-press analogy highlights a step-change in the cost of creating and distributing information, with LLMs representing a further order-of-magnitude drop that also enables interactive, conversational access to entire corpora.
- LLMs' tendency to "hallucinate" plausible falsehoods is a structural property arising from training on coherence rather than truth, and will fuel an effectively infinite supply of disinformation (per Renée DiResta of the Stanford Internet Observatory).
- The psychoanalytic analogy suggests that just as Freud argued humans are "not masters in their own house," LLMs could further decentre human self-conception — rendering a whole wing of mind as automatable "smart home."
- Competing expert positions (Emily Bender's "stochastic parrots" vs. Blaise Agüera y Arcas's emergent-meaning view) remain unresolved, and the models' inner workings are not yet theoretically understood even by their creators (per Stephen Wolfram).
- Posthumous AI avatars — chatbots trained on a deceased person's work and voice — are already technically feasible, as demonstrated by the Laurie Anderson / Lou Reed assistant built by the Australian Institute for Machine Learning.
- Practical ethics frameworks drawn from psychotherapy (surfacing non-conscious biases, managing hallucination-generating contradictions, assigning parental responsibility to creators) may be more immediately useful than extinction-focused AI-safety discourse.

## Key passages

> "A technology need not be world-ending to be world-changing."

> "The supply of disinformation, Renée DiResta of the Stanford Internet Observatory has warned, 'will soon be infinite'."

> "There's no 'ultimate theoretical reason' why anything like this should work." — Stephen Wolfram, on LLMs' inner workings.

> "These models are just representations of the distributions of words in texts that can be used to produce more words." — Emily Bender, University of Washington.

> "LLMs could be argued to take the idea further still. At least one wing of Freud's house becomes an unoccupied 'smart home'; the lights go on and off automatically, the smart thermostat opens windows and lowers blinds, the roomba roombas around. No master needed at all."

> "If some chatbots do become, to some extent, their user's inner voice, then that voice will persist after death, should others wish to converse with it."
