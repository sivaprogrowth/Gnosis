## Abstract

This *Economist* article examines the franchise model as a durable path to wealth in America, tracing its spread from the 1950s to the present day when nearly 850,000 franchise establishments employ some 9 million people and generate roughly 3% of GDP. Through case studies of operators like Greg Flynn—reportedly the world's first billionaire franchisee—and Bert Albertse of Jetset Pilates, the piece explains why franchisors and franchisees both benefit from the arrangement: franchisees gain brand security and proven systems while franchisors gain motivated, capital-bearing entrepreneurs. The article also addresses criticisms, particularly around labour practices, citing Brian Callaci's argument that franchisors' control suppresses worker wages, but notes the empirical evidence for broad harm is mixed. It concludes that renewed interest among younger Americans, partly driven by AI's threat to white-collar jobs, may swell the ranks of franchisees further.

## TL;DR

- The franchise model quietly accounts for ~3% of US GDP, has created more millionaires than almost any other business structure, and is now attracting a new generation spooked by AI's threat to white-collar work.
- Franchisees outperform corporate-owned units because motivated owner-operators with local knowledge work harder than salaried managers—though failure risk varies sharply by brand maturity.
- Labour-economics criticism centres on no-poach clauses (now largely eliminated) and joint-employer liability, but the wage penalty at franchised outlets is empirically small.

## Key claims

- There are close to 850,000 franchise establishments in the US, run by ~250,000 owners, employing ~9 million people and generating ~3% of GDP; one in six businesses with at least one employee is part of a franchise.
- Greg Flynn, who started with eight Applebee's restaurants in 1994, now runs more than 3,000 outlets across seven brands and is reportedly worth over $1bn—likely the first franchisee to reach that milestone.
- McDonald's franchisees own 95% of its ~14,000 US outlets, and the chain has "plausibly created more millionaires than any firm in history."
- Approximately two-thirds of American motels are owned by Indians, many descended from Gujarati immigrants who bought Super 8 and Travelodge franchises in the 1980s.
- Franchise Disclosure Documents (FDDs), required by US law, reveal that ongoing royalties typically run 5–7% of sales for food-service and 10–12% for beauty and fitness, with total opening costs often exceeding $1m for restaurants.
- Francine LaFontaine (University of Michigan) finds that franchise survival rates versus independent businesses differ only in the first year or two; afterwards, survival likelihood is roughly equal.
- Alan Krueger's 2018 paper documented widespread no-poach clauses in franchise agreements and argued they suppressed wages; Brian Callaci's 2024 study found eliminating them raised affected workers' wages by 4–6%.
- The "joint-employer rule"—making franchisors jointly liable for franchisees' workers—was reintroduced in a narrow form ("direct and immediate control") under the Trump administration in February 2026.
