## Abstract

This *Economist* leader argues that while AI-driven mass unemployment has not yet materialised in labour-market data, the pace of AI improvement is rapid enough that governments would be rash to dismiss the risk. It warns that even a modest disruption to white-collar employment could trigger severe political backlash—analogous to the "China shock" that helped propel populist politics—and that concentrations of AI-generated rents among capital owners could eventually render most human labour economically unviable. Rather than slowing technological progress, the article advocates a suite of proactive policy responses: rent-capturing tax reforms (on excess corporate profits, land, and natural resources), inheritance taxes, wage insurance, active labour-market policies, and potentially partial public ownership of AI firms to make the social upside of the technology transparent before rentier power becomes entrenched.

## TL;DR

- AI-driven job destruction is not yet visible in aggregate data, but its speed of improvement makes pre-emptive policy action necessary.
- Governments should capture AI-generated rents via tax reform and wage insurance rather than throttling the technology.
- Waiting for conclusive evidence before building a safety-net will leave it too late to act.

## Key claims

- Seven in ten Americans believe AI will make it harder to find work; roughly one-third fear for their own jobs specifically.
- [[companies/anthropic|Anthropic]]'s annualised recurring revenue is on track to reach $50 bn by end of June 2026, illustrating the explosive commercial scale of frontier AI.
- Goldman Sachs projects that data centres will account for 8.5% of America's peak power demand in 2027, up from 4.1% in 2025, meaning AI infrastructure spending will itself reduce the real purchasing power of wages.
- The "China shock" cost roughly 2 million American jobs between 1999 and 2011—a small figure by labour-market standards—yet was sufficient to reshape politics and produce the highest US tariffs since the 1930s; a comparable or smaller AI disruption among politically influential white-collar workers could produce an even larger backlash.
- Slowing AI adoption is dismissed as unwise: suppressing automation would forfeit gains against disease, climate change, and poverty.
- Preferred policy instruments include levies on above-normal returns on capital, land-value taxes, natural-resource rents, inheritance taxes, public wage insurance, and Denmark-style active labour-market policies.
- Partial nationalisation of AI firms—or citizens' dividend schemes funded by AI-sector tax revenues—is flagged as a radical but potentially necessary measure to make the social upside of AI visible and legitimate.
- Rent-capture measures must be enacted early, before the political power of AI rentiers makes reform impossible.

## Key passages

> "The top AI models are awesome. They can tackle much more complex coding tasks than people were predicting a year ago. The number of AI agents has exploded. Spending on AI by businesses is up dramatically."

> "Eventually humans could, like horses in the age of the car, become uneconomical. Income may go mostly or entirely to owners of capital, who then go on to spend it on things that are made by AI and robots using natural resources that they monopolise."

> "The white-collar employees threatened by AI have more political and social clout than factory workers hurt by Chinese competition. Even a small number of lay-offs could provoke a backlash against the technology; furious opposition to new data centres is a hint of what may be to come."

> "Clever tax reforms, such as levies on corporate profits that are above a normal return on capital, on land and on natural resources, could capture these rents. The case for inheritance taxes to prevent the entrenchment of a capital-owning elite looks even stronger than before."

> "Concentrations of rent must be confronted early, before the power of rentiers is too great. The jobs apocalypse is not yet here. But if governments wait for conclusive evidence before creating a safety-net, it will be too late."
