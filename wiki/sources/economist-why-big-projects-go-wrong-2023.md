## Abstract

This *Economist* Bartleby column reviews *How Big Things Get Done* by Bent Flyvbjerg and Dan Gardner, using Britain's HS2 high-speed rail project as a framing example of chronic megaproject failure. Drawing on Flyvbjerg's database of over 16,000 projects, the piece argues that only 8.5% of projects meet initial cost and time estimates, and a mere 0.5% hit all three targets of cost, time, and benefits. The authors identify two root causes — psychological optimism bias and deliberate "strategic misrepresentation" of budgets — and prescribe rigorous upfront planning and standardisation of components as the most reliable antidotes. The column concludes that lessons from infrastructure megaprojects apply broadly to managers in any domain.

## TL;DR

- Fewer than 1 in 10 large projects meet their cost and time targets; barely 1 in 200 also delivers the promised benefits.
- Over-optimism and deliberate budget lowballing ("strategic misrepresentation") are the twin engines of megaproject failure.
- Rigorous planning before execution and standardising replicable components are the most evidence-backed remedies.

## Key claims

- Flyvbjerg's database of 16,000+ projects shows only 8.5% meet initial cost and time estimates, and only 0.5% hit cost, time, and benefits targets.
- Two distinct biases drive overruns: psychological over-optimism (intuition over data) and political "strategic misrepresentation" (deliberate lowballing to secure approval).
- The sunk-cost fallacy means projects are rarely cancelled once started, compounding initial errors.
- Rushed planning — rather than rushed execution — is the primary structural failure mode; more time spent planning reduces exposure to unexpected events during implementation.
- Pixar's film development process and Frank Gehry's architectural modelling for the Guggenheim Bilbao are cited as exemplars of rigorous pre-production planning.
- Projects built from standardised, replicable components (solar, wind) dramatically outperform bespoke one-off projects (nuclear plants, Olympics) in delivering on schedule and budget.
- China built the world's largest high-speed rail network in under a decade by using standardised designs and manufacturing processes for tracks, viaducts, and trains.
- Modular nuclear reactors and Paris 2024's use of existing venues are offered as examples of applying the standardisation principle to traditionally bespoke sectors.

## Key passages

> "By his reckoning, only 8.5% of projects meet their initial estimates on cost and time, and a piddling 0.5% achieve what they set out to do on cost, time and benefits."

> "This is when budgets are deliberately lowballed in order to get things going, on the premise that nothing would ever get built if politicians went around being accurate."

> "The sunk-cost fallacy, whereby people hesitate to halt projects because money spent will seem to have been wasted, means that the plug is seldom pulled once work is under way."

> "By keeping to a minimum the window when the project is actually being implemented, thorough planning reduces the probability of unexpected events derailing things."

> "Mr Flyvbjerg's database shows that solar-power and windpower installations stand the best chance of not going wrong, in part because standard components can be snapped together into arrays and turbines. At the other end of the risk scale lie gigantic one-off efforts like nuclear-power stations and the Olympic games."

> "If you plan rigorously and standardise where possible, you are less likely to dig yourself into a hole."
