## Abstract

Writing for *The Economist*'s "By Invitation" section, Sam Freedman argues that the shift to algorithmic social media and chatbots as the primary news source for young people represents a fundamental challenge for schools worldwide. More than half of 18- to 24-year-olds globally now cite social media and chatbots as their main news channel, and research shows that algorithmic feeds actively promote polarising content and create divergent information environments between demographic groups. Freedman, who chairs the Current Affairs Education Commission for The Economist Educational Foundation, draws on polling and focus groups in Britain to show that teachers lack both the resources and the training to help students contextualise this firehose of unfiltered content. He argues that schools must urgently build media literacy and the capacity for constructive disagreement into curricula — a need that England's forthcoming 2028 curriculum reform begins to address but does not fully resolve.

## TL;DR

- Algorithmic news feeds have made young people passive recipients of vast, unfiltered, often polarising content rather than active news-seekers, creating new challenges for educators.
- Teachers lack training and confidence to facilitate conversations about controversial current affairs, and social media's incentive structures actively reinforce polarisation.
- Curriculum reform (including compulsory citizenship in England from 2028) and teacher training in managing sensitive discussions are necessary but insufficient responses.

## Key claims

- Over 50% of 18- to 24-year-olds globally now say social media and chatbots are their main news source; for UK 12–15-year-olds, Ofcom puts the figure at 78%.
- A randomised study published in *Nature* found that X's algorithmic "for you" feed made users 5.5% more likely to view investigations into Donald Trump as unacceptable, compared with a chronological feed.
- Algorithmically curated feeds create information divides within peer groups: in one UK school, 11-year-old girls had been exposed to a contentious US campus rape case that the boys in the same class had never heard of.
- Young people's feeds are disproportionately skewed toward US-generated content, meaning a typical British teenager may be more conversant with American political memes than with domestic politics.
- A second study of X found that highly negative posts about political opponents significantly outperform positive posts about political allies, incentivising polarising language.
- Teachers cite lack of resources and lack of confidence around sensitive topics (e.g. Israel-Gaza, race riots) as the main barriers to current-affairs teaching.
- England's new curriculum, due from September 2028, will make citizenship a compulsory primary subject, but Freedman argues this alone is insufficient without cross-subject integration and dedicated teacher training.
- Proposed social-media bans for under-16s (UK ban forthcoming) may worsen information quality for some young people by pushing consumption onto less-moderated channels such as WhatsApp.

## Key passages

> "More than half of the world's 18- to 24-year-olds now say that social media and chatbots are their main way to access news."

> "A recent study, published in Nature, randomised American users of Elon Musk's X so that half saw the algorithmic 'for you' feed and half a chronological one of people they followed. The first group were 5.5% likelier to view investigations into Donald Trump as unacceptable."

> "The bigger issue is that children are seeing a lot more news than they used to and it is unfiltered. Videos on all sorts of topics flash by in a muddle without context or explanation."

> "Another study of X found that highly negative posts about political opponents significantly outperform positive ones about allies. Increasingly, people say they are less likely to be friends with people who hold a different view."

> "Learning how to manage these kinds of conversation, which increasingly occur spontaneously in lessons, is not a formal part of teacher-training courses."

> "Either way there will be no return to the old model. Teenagers aren't suddenly going to start reading physical newspapers or watching the evening news. A core purpose of education is to give young people the knowledge they need to be active citizens."
