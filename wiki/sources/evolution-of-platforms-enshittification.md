## Abstract

This opinion column by Jaspreet Bindra examines the systemic degradation of dominant online platforms — Google, [[companies/facebook|Facebook]], [[companies/meta|Meta]]/Instagram, Twitter, and [[companies/amazon|Amazon]] — through the economic lens of "enshittification," a term coined by writer-activist Cory Doctorow. The article argues that all successful networks follow a predictable three-stage arc: first serving users well, then abusing users to benefit business clients, and finally abusing both to extract maximum value for themselves. Two structural forces — network effects and switching costs — enable and sustain this trajectory, while Doctorow's concept of "weaponized" switching costs explains why users cannot easily exit. The piece proposes interoperability as the primary antidote, drawing on Web3 experiments and real-world analogies to competitive markets.

## TL;DR

- Dominant platforms inevitably degrade user experience through a three-stage process Cory Doctorow calls "enshittification."
- Network effects and deliberately weaponized switching costs trap users and business clients, enabling monopolistic abuse.
- Interoperability — the ability to move your data, social graph, and content across platforms — is proposed as the structural fix.

## Key claims

- [[companies/google|Google]]'s search experience has deteriorated from a clean interface to one dominated by sponsored links and ads, illustrative of broad platform decay.
- Cory Doctorow's "enshittification" framework describes a three-stage platform lifecycle: good to users → abuse users to serve business clients → abuse business clients to reclaim all value.
- Network effects (users go where their friends/buyers already are) and switching costs (rebuilding social graphs, losing locked-in content) are the two structural pillars that give platforms monopoly power.
- Switching costs are actively "weaponized" by social networks, creating deliberate friction to prevent user exit — illustrated by Doctorow's fictional story "Unauthorized Bread."
- Platforms typically subsidize users and suppliers during growth phases, then monetize that captive base at the expense of experience once critical mass is reached.
- Economists propose interoperability — cross-platform messaging, portable social graphs, transferable digital goods — as the antidote to enshittification.
- Web3 and decentralized platforms offered an early model of portability (e.g., taking virtual assets between games), though this remains nascent.
- Competitive real-world markets (coffee shops, taxis) naturally prevent enshittification because switching is easy; the same principle should apply to digital platforms.

## Key passages

> "Here is how platforms die: First, they are good to their users; then they abuse their users to make things better for their business customers; finally, they abuse those business customers to claw back all the value for themselves." — Cory Doctorow

> "The Eric Schmidts and Sheryl Sandbergs then take over the business reins from founders and ruthlessly go after appeasing business customers—either advertisers or sellers on the platform."

> "Doctorow talks about how these switching costs are 'weaponized', with social networks deliberately creating frustrating hoops that you have to jump through to move away."

> "Successful networks take huge losses while subsidizing their growing user base and even suppliers. When they have a critical mass, they turn to monetizing it at the cost of their user experience."

> "Coffee shops, restaurants or local taxis do not serve you badly because they know you can go somewhere else with your money and appetite. The same should happen in social networks—before they descend into a putrid monopolistic morass."
