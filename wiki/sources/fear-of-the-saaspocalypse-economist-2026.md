## Abstract

The Economist surveys the mounting threat that AI poses to the Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) industry, which suffered a brutal stock sell-off in early 2026 and has only partially recovered. The article identifies four overlapping attack vectors — large AI labs, AI-native vertical startups, DIY enterprise software development, and the incumbents' own self-disruptive AI pivots — dubbing them the "four SaaSquatches of the apocalypse." Core SaaS giants such as Salesforce, ServiceNow, and Workday face a structural threat to their per-seat recurring-revenue model as [[concepts/agentic-ai|AI agents]] replace human workers and consume resources at variable cost. While some SaaS sectors (cyber-security, data management) are booming from AI tailwinds, the article argues that the greatest danger for legacy vendors is cannibalisation: their own AI products erode revenue from traditional licences faster than new AI-driven revenue can replace it.

## TL;DR

- AI is attacking SaaS incumbents from four directions simultaneously: large labs ([[companies/anthropic|Anthropic]], [[companies/openai|OpenAI]]), AI-native startups, enterprise DIY builds, and the incumbents' own AI tools.
- The per-seat recurring-revenue model underpinning Salesforce, ServiceNow, and Workday is structurally threatened because AI agents don't occupy "seats" and carry rising marginal costs.
- Cannibalisation — where a vendor's new AI products hollow out its own legacy licence revenue — is the core financial risk the SaaSocalypse scenario describes.

## Key claims

- The "SaaSpocalypse" fear has driven an average 34% share-price decline YTD for Salesforce, ServiceNow, and Workday, even as cyber-security SaaS stocks (CrowdStrike, Fortinet, Palo Alto Networks) are up ~52%.
- AI labs are positioned to move *horizontally* across SaaS verticals, stitching together siloed applications through plug-ins and a single chat interface — potentially reducing SaaS apps to "plumbing."
- [[companies/anthropic|Anthropic]]'s [[entities/claude-code|Claude Code]] and [[companies/openai|OpenAI]]'s Codex are the most visible early coding-layer threats from the major labs.
- AI-native vertical startups such as Harvey ($11bn valuation, legal AI) are attacking the domain-specific strongholds of SaaS incumbents like Thomson Reuters.
- Enterprises are increasingly adopting a "build versus buy" stance: Kirkland & Ellis announced a $500m plan to build proprietary AI tools rather than license third-party software.
- Unlike traditional software, AI agents impose rising marginal costs on suppliers as usage grows, breaking the near-zero marginal cost economics that made SaaS highly profitable.
- Hybrid consumption-based pricing (seat fees + token fees) is emerging as the transition model, but analyst Tal Liani of Bank of America argues it cannot yet offset lost legacy licence revenue.
- Snowflake's stock jumped 36% in a single day on AI-powered data-querying demand, illustrating that AI creates winners as well as losers within SaaS.

## Key passages

> "The threat to SaaS incumbents is coming from four directions: large AI labs; AI-native startups; DIY software development; and the industry's own disruptive efforts to reinvent itself. Call them the four SaaSquatches of the apocalypse."

> "AI labs, by contrast, threaten to move horizontally, producing agents that operate at a level above the SaaS products, stitching together various programs through plug-ins and a single chat-based interface. Some SaaS applications that were once considered snazzy business tools risk becoming more like plumbing."

> "The SaaS industry built its success on persuading customers that it is cheaper and easier to buy standardised software programs than to develop custom ones in-house... Now, however, enterprises are embracing a 'build versus buy' spirit."

> "Unlike traditional software, AI agents cost a supplier more as their customers increase their usage. Another problem is that, by replacing human workers, these new tools eat into the revenue that comes from their legacy products. AI agents 'don't need seats'."

> "The risk is cannibalisation... Not long ago, software was said to be 'eating the world'. Now the danger is that it eats itself."
