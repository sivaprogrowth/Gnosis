## Abstract

Originally published by *The Economist* and republished in *Livemint*, this article curates five essential books for investors ranging from complete novices to seasoned professionals. It argues that long-term prosperity is best achieved through disciplined saving, low-cost investing, and exposure to broad economic growth — not through stock-picking, speculation, or chasing high returns. The piece frames each book around a distinct investor pitfall: overpaying for stocks, trading too often, paying high fees, falling for fraud, and misreading historical returns. Together, the five titles form a coherent curriculum on what to do — and, just as importantly, what to avoid — as an investor.

## TL;DR

- Long-term wealth is built through low-cost, diversified investing aligned with broad economic growth, not stock-picking or speculation.
- The five recommended books address the core dangers investors face: overvaluation, high costs, fraud, survivorship bias, and low future expected returns.
- Historical American equity outperformance is not a reliable guide to the future; global data shows savings can be wiped out by hyperinflation or political seizure.

## Key claims

- [[The Intelligent Investor]] by Benjamin Graham establishes that price paid for a stock is paramount, and that fashionable growth industries are often poor investments.
- [[The Clash of the Cultures]] by John Bogle argues that most investors should own the entire market through low-cost index trackers rather than chasing hot stocks or funds.
- John Bogle founded [[Vanguard]], a mutually-owned asset manager, embodying his belief that minimising costs is as important as maximising returns — "the tyranny of compounding costs."
- [[Lying for Money]] by Dan Davies identifies financial fraud as a third major pitfall, noting it thrives in unregulated markets (e.g. cryptocurrencies) and preys on investors who don't scrutinise implausibly high returns.
- [[Triumph of the Optimists]] by Dimson, Marsh, and Staunton demonstrates that 20th-century U.S. equity outperformance creates a survivorship-bias distortion; elsewhere, investors have been wiped out by hyperinflation or revolution.
- [[Investing Amid Low Expected Returns]] by Antti Ilmanen argues that the low bond and equity yields prevailing at time of publication would structurally reduce future investment returns, a thesis apparently confirmed by the brutal 2022 market.
- Investors who start saving in unfavourable eras (1920s America, 1980s Japan) or under hostile governments can suffer even with sound strategies — luck and timing play a real role.

## Key passages

> "It is a mistake for novice investors to assume that they can find instant success in achieving high returns when professionals, armed with extensive research and sophisticated technology, struggle to do so."

> "Obvious prospects for physical growth in a business do not translate into obvious prospects for investors" whereas "a sufficiently low price can turn a security of mediocre quality into a sound investment opportunity." — Benjamin Graham

> "Investors need to understand not only the magic of compounding long-term returns, but the tyranny of compounding costs." — John Bogle

> "If it seems too good to be true, it probably is." — summarising the thesis of *Lying for Money*

> "America's great success tends to skew investor impressions. Elsewhere, investors have seen their savings wiped out by hyperinflation or revolutionary governments. Just because the optimists were right in the 20th century doesn't mean they will always be proved right in the 21st."
