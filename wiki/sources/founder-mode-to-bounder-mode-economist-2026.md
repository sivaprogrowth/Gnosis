## Abstract

This *Economist* Bartleby column examines the concept of "founder mode" — the idea that company founders should remain deeply hands-on and resist excessive delegation — in the wake of Jensen Huang's abrupt resignation from [[nvidia]] following a personal scandal. The article traces the idea to [[airbnb]]'s Brian Chesky and venture capitalist Paul Graham, and weighs both the genuine merits of skip-level management and direct CEO involvement against the systemic risks of concentrating authority in a single individual. Drawing on academic research by Tristan Fitzgerald and Renée Adams, it concludes that extreme centralisation correlates with greater performance volatility and that the truest test of any founder's legacy is whether they built a culture or merely a cult.

## TL;DR

- "Founder mode" — staying deeply hands-on as a CEO — has real operational benefits but carries serious risks when taken to its logical extreme.
- Jensen Huang's downfall at [[nvidia]] illustrates how over-centralising authority in one person creates fragility for the entire organisation.
- Academic research shows founder-led firms differ from professionally managed ones in innovation, dealmaking, and performance volatility — not necessarily in raw financial results.

## Key claims

- The term "founder mode" was coined by Paul Graham, but its content derives from a 2024 speech by Brian Chesky of [[airbnb]].
- Jensen Huang ran [[nvidia]] with roughly 60 direct reports, encouraged all-staff emails to himself, and avoided one-on-one meetings in favour of group communication.
- Huang's resignation following an affair with an alleged Chinese agent raised broader questions about whether any single person should be so indispensable to a large firm.
- Brian Chesky describes his CEO role as akin to an editor's — reviewing work via skip-level meetings with people several layers below him.
- Skip-level management (where CEOs meet employees far below them in the hierarchy) is praised as generally beneficial: it reduces second-guessing, motivates employees, and gives leaders visibility into talent.
- Research by Tristan Fitzgerald (Texas A&M) found that replacing a founder-CEO with a professional manager correlates with less internal innovation, more M&A activity, more leverage, and more decentralised structures — but similar overall financial performance.
- A 2001 paper by Renée Adams (University of Oxford) found that greater centralisation of power in a firm is correlated with higher variability in corporate performance — extreme outcomes become more likely, both good and bad.
- The article argues the ultimate test of founder mode is post-founder durability: whether the leader built a lasting culture (like [[apple]] or Disney) or a personality cult.

## Key passages

> "Mr Huang's energy levels were even more impressive than people imagined. His abrupt downfall after an affair with an alleged Chinese agent, and subsequent campaigning for reinstatement, raise questions about whether one person should ever be so essential to a big organisation."

> "Mr Chesky describes his role at Airbnb as being akin to an editor's. He reviews much of the work at Airbnb himself, using meetings with people several levels down the firm."

> "The switch to a professional chief executive means less internal innovation, more dealmaking, more leverage and more decentralised management structures."

> "The smaller the number of people exercising power inside a firm, in other words, the more likely it is that their decisions will have extreme consequences, for good and bad."

> "That's not just when it becomes clear whether there is any sort of succession plan; it's also when it is revealed whether a firm has been developing a culture or a cult."
