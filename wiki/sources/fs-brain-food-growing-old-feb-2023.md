## Abstract

This edition of Shane Parrish's *FS Brain Food* newsletter curates a handful of aphorisms and quotations on themes of resilience, mortality, and practical wisdom. It draws on Henry Miller's sardonic view of human nature, Frederick Buechner's call to treat every moment as sacred, Bill Ackman's conviction that success is determined by how one handles failure, and Bertrand Russell's late-life essay on the two psychological dangers of old age. Together the pieces argue that wise living requires orienting toward the future, avoiding avoidable stupidity, and accepting life's hardship without either nostalgic retreat or a desperate clinging to youth.

## TL;DR

- Resilience over brilliance: how you respond to failure is a stronger predictor of success than any single talent or strategy.
- Chess-master thinking — ask "what is the threat?" before seeking a winning move — is a template for avoiding catastrophic error in any domain.
- Bertrand Russell identifies two traps in old age: absorption in the past and clinging to youth; both are remedied by forward-directed, contemplative engagement with the world.

## Key claims

- Henry Miller holds that stupidity, unlike evil, is essentially unkillable — and that humans uniquely dress ignoble acts in the language of high moral purpose.
- Frederick Buechner argues that every moment — including boredom and pain — carries hidden grace and should be attended to with full sensory presence.
- Bill Ackman contends that success is not a straight-line trajectory and that persistence through failure, not avoidance of it, is the defining variable.
- Shane Parrish's "Tiny Thought" applies chess-master heuristics to general decision-making: defensive threat-assessment precedes offensive strategy.
- The principle "avoid stupidity before seeking brilliance" is presented as a meta-strategy superior to pure optimisation.
- Bertrand Russell warns that dwelling in memories or regrets actively accelerates cognitive and emotional decline — forgetting the vividness of youth helps preserve it.
- Russell draws an analogy to animal behaviour: non-human parents disengage from offspring once they are self-sufficient, while humans struggle with this transition to their own and their children's detriment.

## Key passages

> "One can fight evil but against stupidity one is helpless. … The ironic, the tragic thing is that we often behave in ignoble fashion from what we consider the highest motives."
> — Henry Miller

> "Listen to your life. See it for the fathomless mystery it is. In the boredom and pain of it, no less than in the excitement and gladness: touch, taste, smell your way to the holy and hidden heart of it, because in the last analysis all moments are key moments, and life itself is grace."
> — Frederick Buechner, *Now and Then: A Memoir of Vocation*

> "I've always had the view that how successful you are is really a function of how you deal with failure. If you deal with failure well and you persist, you have a high probability of being successful."
> — Bill Ackman

> "Psychologically there are two dangers to be guarded against in old age. One of these is undue absorption in the past. … The other thing to be avoided is clinging to youth in the hope of sucking vigour from its vitality."
> — Bertrand Russell

> "To win, you have to avoid losing. … Avoid stupidity before seeking brilliance."
> — Shane Parrish
