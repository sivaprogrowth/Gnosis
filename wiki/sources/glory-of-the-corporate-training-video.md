## Abstract

This *Economist* Bartleby column offers a satirical anatomy of the corporate training video, dissecting three recurring pathologies: production values frozen in a previous era, subject matter so elementary it insults adult intelligence, and pedagogical techniques borrowed wholesale from primary school. The author draws on personal experience completing an online course on workplace slips, trips and falls to illustrate how condescending design — cartoons, gold stars, confetti certificates — paradoxically produces childish behaviour in adult learners, including the urge to cheat. The piece ultimately argues that the credential-granting machinery of corporate training creates the illusion of learning while delivering almost none of it. The column matters as a concise critique of how compliance-driven training optimises for liability protection rather than genuine skill transfer.

## TL;DR

- Corporate training videos are typically designed for legal compliance and shelf-life, not actual learning.
- Treating employees like dim children produces childish responses — including cheating — rather than skill acquisition.
- The gold-star, certificate-and-confetti reward loop yields credentials without retained knowledge.

## Key claims

- Many corporate training videos are either genuinely decades old or deliberately produced to look timeless, prioritising shelf-life over relevance.
- Training is frequently commissioned on subjects (e.g., not slipping on wet floors) that require no specialised instruction for a competent adult.
- The dominant pedagogical model of corporate e-learning mirrors primary school: cartoons, multiple-choice quizzes, badges, gold stars, and downloadable certificates.
- Gamified reward mechanisms (confetti, certificates) produce genuine emotional engagement even in jaded, near-retirement employees — an unintended testament to their power.
- The same reward structure also incentivises gaming the system: skipping video content and clicking randomly through quizzes to obtain a passing credential.
- Cyber-security training modules (covering phishing, smishing, and quishing) are cited as a prime example where the certificate is obtained without meaningful knowledge transfer.
- The author concludes that corporate training certificates are structurally analogous to school credentials obtained by rote or cheating — a credential without absorption.

## Key passages

> "To watch some corporate videos is to be treated like a particularly dim child—or, more accurately, like someone a particularly dim child would consider to be a moron."

> "Treat an audience as if they are children and their behaviour will revert to childish norms. You might be approaching retirement but you still get a tiny thrill from getting a gold star."

> "You start playing a cyber-security training video on avoiding phishing, smishing and quishing, and pay it attention only when it requires you to press the 'next' button."

> "You click things randomly until you pass with distinction, and are awarded a phishing, smishing and quishing certificate."

> "It's just like school: you have a credential but have absorbed almost nothing."
