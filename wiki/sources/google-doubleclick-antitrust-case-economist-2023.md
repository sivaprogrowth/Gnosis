## Abstract

The Economist's *Free Exchange* column examines the U.S. Department of Justice's 2023 antitrust complaint against [[companies/google|Google]] for monopolising digital advertising exchanges, which centres on [[companies/google|Google]]'s 2008 acquisition of DoubleClick. The article questions the prevailing regulatory consensus that the Federal Trade Commission made a mistake in allowing the DoubleClick merger, arguing that the integrated ad-tech stack may simply be more efficient under a single operator and that network externalities naturally favour consolidation in multi-sided markets. It raises the possibility that the FTC's perceived laxity — rather than being a straightforward failure — may have reflected genuine uncertainty about market boundaries, a difficulty the DOJ case still struggles with. Former FTC commissioner William Kovacic suggests an internal administrative trial at the time might have been a better path than litigation. The piece concludes that the upcoming court battle, while potentially landmark, rests on contested assumptions about where monopoly power actually resides.

## TL;DR

- The DOJ's antitrust case against Google's ad-tech business attempts to undo a 15-year-old merger (DoubleClick, 2008), but the article argues it is far from clear the merger was actually a mistake.
- The "choke point" of monopoly power in Google's ad-tech stack is ambiguous and shifts throughout the DOJ's own complaint, weakening its analogy to cleaner historical cases like the Microsoft browser case.
- Vertical integration in multi-sided ad-tech markets may reflect efficiency and network externalities rather than anticompetitive foreclosure.

## Key claims

- The DOJ's January 2023 complaint (155 pages) alleges [[companies/google|Google]] used strong-arm tactics to lock up the ad-tech business, billing it as the biggest antitrust challenge to tech since the DOJ vs. Microsoft in the late 1990s.
- The case centres on the 2008 DoubleClick acquisition; regulators now treat FTC approval of that merger as an article of faith in antitrust under-enforcement.
- Unlike the Microsoft/Windows case — where the choke point (the operating system) was clear — the locus of monopoly power in the DOJ's Google case "shape-shifts" between the demand side, supply side, and the exchange itself.
- [[companies/google|Google]]'s end-to-end presence in the ad-tech stack (publisher ad server → exchange → demand-side platforms) may produce efficiency gains and smoother data flows rather than anticompetitive harm.
- Ad-tech exhibits network externalities typical of multi-sided platforms, where single-enterprise consolidation is common (analogous to payment card networks serving both merchants and cardholders).
- The European Commission — described as "no friend to American tech" — conducted an in-depth investigation and still allowed the DoubleClick merger.
- William Kovacic (FTC commissioner at the time) argues the agency could have pursued an internal administrative trial instead of probable losing litigation, potentially enabling remedies short of blocking the merger.
- The narrative of "antitrust under-enforcement" that has driven today's aggressive merger scrutiny may itself rest on a misreading of the DoubleClick decision.

## Key passages

> "The charge against Google is more complex, or at least the story is one that is harder to tell. The locus of monopoly, in the doj's telling, seems to shift. First it lies with Google's power on the demand side of digital advertising, through its adjacent strength in search ads. At other times, it is the company's hold on the supply side, bolstered when it bought DoubleClick. At still other times, the locus of market power is the exchange."

> "The profitability of the ad-tech stack might reflect the fact it is more efficient under a single roof. The integration of publisher ad server, exchange and demand-side platforms is likely to make for a smoother flow of data, better matches between buyers and sellers and a more streamlined experience."

> "Trustbusters like to define markets narrowly. The smaller the market, the larger the leading firms loom in it."

> "Instead of suing in court to block the merger and (probably) losing, the agency could have pursued an internal-administrative trial. This would have afforded officials an opportunity to learn about tech and update their practices, says Mr Kovacic. It might have allowed for remedies, short of unwinding the merger, to put Google on watch."

> "The charge of 'antitrust under-enforcement', which has fuelled today's over-active merger control, might not have taken hold."
