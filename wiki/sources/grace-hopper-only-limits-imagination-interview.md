## Abstract

This article is a transcript of an interview conducted with Rear Admiral Grace M. Hopper by *Chips Ahoy*, a U.S. Navy computing publication, near the end of her nearly 40-year uniformed career. Hopper ranges across topics including the indispensability of computers to naval operations, the imminent decline of mainframes in favour of networked microcomputer systems, the early promise of artificial intelligence expert systems (notably MYCIN), and the capacity of the next generation — already learning BASIC in third grade — to unlock computing's full potential. The interview is notable for Hopper's forward-looking conviction that the only real limit on what computers can do is human imagination, and for her famous aphorism: "It's easier to ask forgiveness than it is to get permission."

## TL;DR

- Hopper predicted the death of mainframes and the rise of distributed, networked microcomputer systems decades before it became conventional wisdom.
- She identified early AI expert systems (MYCIN) as a model for tactical decision-support and argued that computers amplify — rather than replace — human expertise.
- She insisted that computing's ceiling is set exclusively by human imagination, not by the technology itself.

## Key claims

- Hopper argued that the U.S. Navy could not function without its data-processing computers, framing them as an existential dependency rather than a convenience.
- She predicted mainframes would disappear entirely, replaced by arrays of microcomputers running workloads in parallel — anticipating distributed computing architectures.
- Hopper contended that organisational restructuring, not computers, is responsible for workforce displacement, pushing back on the narrative that machines eliminate jobs.
- She identified weather forecasting and water resource management as two critical domains demanding far more powerful, specialised computing systems than existed at the time.
- Hopper described [[concepts/agentic-ai|expert-system-style AI]] through the MYCIN example — a rule-based diagnostic system — as "the very first of the artificial intelligence expert routines" and a model for military tactical AI.
- She foresaw universal quick access to information once a new generation of computer-native children entered the workforce.
- Hopper argued that personalised financial and insurance services, telephone routing, and other complex modern services were already impossible without computers — making reliance on them unavoidable rather than excessive.
- She coined (or popularised) the dictum "It's easier to ask forgiveness than it is to get permission," applying it even to bureaucratic publishing logistics.

## Key passages

> "They won't know where anybody is or where the ships are. They won't know where anchorages are. They won't know where anything is. They won't know what they have or what they are committed to. They can't run the Navy without our computers."

> "The computer will probably do it better. The computer's decisions will come from those very people. Computers don't do anything. People have to tell them what to do."

> "MYCIN is an outstanding example of the very first of the artificial intelligence expert routines... You collect all the information from all the tactical experts, and the computer tells you what you have. You have the knowledge of the best people at your fingertips."

> "The big mainframes are going to disappear. In fact, I intend to scuttle them... We'll build systems of computers. It will be a whole bunch of micros, and they'll all call each other up and talk."

> "They'll only be limited if our imaginations are limited. It's all up to us. Remember, there were people who said the airplane couldn't fly."

> "It's easier to ask forgiveness than it is to get permission."
