## Abstract

This *Wall Street Journal* review by Marc Levinson covers *How Big Things Get Done*, a book by economic geographer Bent Flyvbjerg (with journalist Dan Gardner) that draws on a database of over 16,000 public and private projects to diagnose why megaprojects so routinely fail. Flyvbjerg finds that only 1 in 200 projects is completed on time, on budget, and delivering promised benefits, with major building projects averaging a 62% cost overrun. The book's central prescription is "think slow, act fast": invest heavily in upfront planning to stress-test assumptions, then execute rapidly to minimise exposure to low-probability disruptions (black swans). Levinson argues the book is essential reading for legislators, executives, and anyone responsible for large capital commitments.

## TL;DR

- Megaprojects fail overwhelmingly due to rushed planning and prolonged execution, not bad luck alone.
- Flyvbjerg's remedy is "think slow, act fast": exhaustive front-end planning followed by swift, modular execution.
- The U.S. infrastructure cost crisis is a solvable problem—Madrid Metro's repeatable-design approach proves it.

## Key claims

- Only 1 in 200 projects in Flyvbjerg's 16,000-project database was completed on time, on budget, and delivered its promised benefits.
- Major building projects have an average cost overrun of 62%; 18% of IT projects have cost overruns above 50%.
- The biggest risk during execution is low-probability black-swan events (pandemics, inflation spikes, natural disasters), so minimising execution duration reduces exposure.
- Managers and politicians share a "bias for action" that leads them to short-circuit planning, which paradoxically increases both cost and time.
- Flyvbjerg recommends "reference-class forecasting": benchmarking a proposed project against the historical performance of similar projects to strip away "uniqueness bias."
- Pixar's iterative storyboarding process—seven or eight full cycles before committing to costly animation—is held up as a model of thorough pre-execution planning.
- The Madrid Metro built 76 stations and 81 miles of subway in eight years by using a single repeatable station design and multiple boring machines running short tunnel stretches simultaneously.
- U.S. infrastructure costs have reached extreme levels (e.g., ~$1.2 billion per mile for San Francisco's Central Subway), which Flyvbjerg frames as a policy failure, not an inevitability.

## Key passages

> "Most big projects are not merely at risk of not delivering as promised. They are at risk of going *disastrously* wrong."

> "Imposing tight deadlines for completion may end up adding costs and time, because the easiest way to craft a tighter schedule is to short-circuit the planning process."

> "The biggest source of risk, he says, is low-probability events—black swans—that occur after the start of construction or implementation… The way to mitigate the risk, he says, is to 'think slow, act fast.'"

> "He recommends a procedure he calls 'reference-class forecasting': Planners should determine what category of projects is similar to their own and then consider how such projects have fared in practice."

> "The best way to bring the costs of large projects under control, Mr. Flyvbjerg says, is to break them into small steps that can be repeated."

> "While countries around the world are building massive transit and rail systems at a fraction of the U.S. cost, many American officials share the fatalism of a former head of New York's Metropolitan Transportation Authority, who opined: 'The cost of construction is what the cost of construction is.'"
