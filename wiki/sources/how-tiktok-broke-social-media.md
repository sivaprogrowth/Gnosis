## Abstract

This *Economist* article argues that [[companies/tiktok|TikTok]], regardless of its political fate in the United States, has permanently restructured the social-media industry by shifting it from social-graph-based news feeds to algorithmically driven short-form video. Every major platform — including [[companies/facebook|Meta's Facebook and Instagram]], YouTube, Snapchat, Pinterest, Netflix, and Spotify — has responded by cloning TikTok's format. The central problem the article surfaces is that short-form video is structurally less monetisable than the old news-feed model: TikTok earns only $0.31 per user-hour versus roughly $1.55 for Instagram, and even mature video platforms like YouTube cannot close that gap. The implications extend beyond platforms to advertisers, who will find social-media budgets buying less reach and response than before.

## TL;DR

- [[companies/tiktok|TikTok]] has forced every major social platform to pivot to algorithmically curated short-form video within six years of entering the US market.
- Short-form video monetises at a fraction of the rate of traditional news feeds, threatening the revenue models of [[companies/facebook|Meta]], Snap, and the entire social-ad ecosystem.
- Even if TikTok is banned, the structural shift it caused — weaker ad targeting, lower ad loads, and a passive viewing mindset — will persist across all its imitators.

## Key claims

- TikTok entered the US in 2017 and has amassed over 100 million American users, with 18–24-year-olds spending roughly one hour per day on the app — five times more than on [[companies/facebook|Facebook]].
- Americans now spend 40 out of their 64 daily social-media minutes watching video clips, up from 28 minutes just three years prior (per Bernstein estimates).
- TikTok monetises its US audience at $0.31 per user-hour — about one-third the rate of Facebook and one-fifth the rate of Instagram — earning ~$67 per US user annually versus Instagram's ~$200.
- [[companies/facebook|Meta]] CEO Mark Zuckerberg acknowledged that Reels' monetisation efficiency is "much less than Feed" and that the company "actually loses money" when users shift from Feed to Reels.
- YouTube, after 18 years, still earns less than half as much per user-hour as Facebook or Instagram; in China, short-video ads monetised at only ~15% the rate of e-commerce ads.
- Short-form video carries a structurally lower ad load (three ads in five minutes vs. dozens on a scrollable image feed) and induces a more passive mindset, reducing click-through rates and advertiser willingness to pay.
- TikTok's algorithm-only targeting is weaker than the decade-plus of rich personal-profile data held by [[companies/facebook|Meta]], giving Meta's Reels a structural targeting advantage over TikTok itself.
- Generative AI may eventually close the production gap for small-business video advertisers, but until it does, roughly 60% of Meta's ~10 million advertisers have yet to adopt Reels ads.

## Key passages

> "In less than six years TikTok has weaned the world off old-fashioned social-networking and got it addicted to algorithmically selected short-form video. Users love it. The trouble for social apps is that the new model makes less money than the old one, and may always do so."

> "TikTok monetises its American audience at a rate of just $0.31 per hour, a third the rate of Facebook and a fifth the rate of Instagram."

> "Mark Zuckerberg, Meta's chief executive, told investors last month that 'Currently, the monetisation efficiency of Reels is much less than Feed, so the more that Reels grows…it takes some time away from Feed and we actually lose money.'"

> "YouTube, which has been around for 18 years, makes less than half as much money per user-hour as Facebook or Instagram, estimates Bernstein. In China, where short-form video took off a few years before it did in the West, short-video ads last year monetised at only about 15% the rate of ads on local e-commerce apps."

> "The upshot is that many advertisers still treat short-form video as a place for loosely targeted so-called brand advertising, to raise general awareness of their product, rather than the hyper-personalised (and more valuable) direct-response ads that old-school social networks specialise in."

> "'All advertising is about what the next-best alternative is,' says Brian Wieser of Madison and Wall, an advertising consultancy. Most advertisers allocate a budget to spend on ads on a particular platform, he says, and 'the budget is the budget', regardless of how far it goes."
