## Abstract

In this essay adapted from a talk at the Oxford Union, Paul Graham argues that becoming a billionaire through a startup is mathematically straightforward — not morally suspect — once you understand exponential growth. He demonstrates with simple compound-growth calculations that a startup growing at 15% per month for five years, or at 93% per month for fewer than ten months, can produce billion-dollar outcomes from a modest base, with no cheating required. The piece contends that the best startup ideas come not from deliberate idea-hunting but from founders building things they personally want, since young founders reliably predict future demand. Graham concludes that the fundamental driver of startup wealth is empathy with users — making something people love enough to tell their friends — rather than exploitation.

## TL;DR

- Becoming a billionaire via a startup is a function of two numbers — growth rate and duration — neither of which requires dishonesty.
- The best startup ideas are found by building things you and your friends personally want, not by consciously searching for "startup ideas."
- Exponential growth makes billion-dollar outcomes seem impossible to observers who don't understand the math, fuelling mistaken beliefs about how wealth is earned.

## Key claims

- A startup growing at 93% per month needs only ~9.5 months to grow 500× from a $2 million base, reaching billionaire territory.
- Even at a conservative 15% monthly growth rate, a startup grows ~4,384× over five years, sufficient to make founders billionaires at typical ownership stakes.
- The two variables that determine a startup's ultimate size — growth rate and duration — are both achievable without unethical behaviour; growth rate comes from user love, duration comes from market size.
- Politicians who assume billionaires must have cheated are failing to understand the mathematics of exponential growth, not making a sound moral observation.
- Young founders should build for themselves because their own needs are leading indicators of broader future demand, making personal intuition a better signal than market research.
- The best startup ideas often sound absurd at first, which is precisely why they remain undiscovered; consciously filtering for "good ideas" causes founders to eliminate the most promising outliers.
- [[companies/y-combinator|Y Combinator]] funded [[companies/airbnb|Airbnb]] despite believing the idea was bad, investing purely on founder quality — and Justin.TV, later rebranded Twitch, is cited as another example of a preposterous-sounding idea that succeeded.
- The key to exponential growth is deep user empathy: understanding what users really want well enough to make something they love and share with friends.

## Key passages

> "She wasn't exploiting anyone. Exactly the opposite in fact. The reason her startup was growing so fast was that she and her cofounder had been working their asses off to make their users happy, and as a result the users had been telling their friends. And that gets you exponential growth."

> "They don't understand the math of exponential growth, so when they see people becoming what seems to them impossibly rich, they assume they must have cheated."

> "The way to get the very best startup ideas is not to look for startup ideas. If you're consciously looking for startup ideas, it will make you too conservative. You'll lop off the outliers."

> "You're young, and usually young founders should make something that they themselves want. You don't have enough experience yet to know what other people need. But at the same time your own needs are uniquely valuable, because your needs predict future demand."

> "There are other ways to get rich than by starting startups. Some of those do require you to exploit people. But startups are the most common way to become really rich, and if you want to start a successful startup, the key is not exploitation but empathy."

> "Initially they're not even meant to be companies. They're just something people built because they thought it would be cool. That's how Apple and Google and Facebook all started."
