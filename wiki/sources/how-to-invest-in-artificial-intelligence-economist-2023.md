## Abstract

Published in *The Economist*'s Buttonwood column, this article examines where the financial value of the generative-AI wave will ultimately land — in private AI-focused startups backed by venture capital, or in already-dominant listed technology incumbents. Drawing on [[clayton-christensen|Clayton Christensen]]'s theory of disruptive innovation, the piece argues that "disruptive" technology does not always produce disruptive business outcomes: in previous machine-learning advances, almost all value accrued to existing giants such as [[companies/microsoft|Microsoft]] and [[companies/nvidia|Nvidia]] rather than to startups. The author concludes that, as things stand, the most likely beneficiaries of generative AI are the mega-cap tech firms already spending heavily on the technology — making a broad index fund tracking listed tech potentially more rewarding than bets on private AI startups.

## TL;DR

- Generative AI may follow the pattern of earlier machine-learning waves, where incumbents — not startups — captured most of the value.
- [[clayton-christensen|Clayton Christensen]]'s disruptive-innovation framework helps explain when upstarts win, but recent tech history favours existing giants.
- Investors may be better served by broad listed-tech index funds than by venture-capital bets on private AI firms.

## Key claims

- Son Masayoshi and [[companies/softbank-vision-fund|SoftBank Vision Fund]] are positioning for AI investments despite losses from the higher-interest-rate environment that hurt their previous VC bets.
- The core investor dilemma is whether generative-AI value will accrue to VC-backed startups or to existing technology giants — a question that remains unresolved as of mid-2023.
- [[companies/amazon|Amazon]], [[companies/meta|Meta]], and [[companies/alibaba|Alibaba]] illustrate that the last era of platform innovation (the internet) created enormous value for early movers — but those early movers are now incumbents.
- Christensen's disruptive-innovation theory predicts smaller firms gain traction in low-end or entirely new markets while incumbents focus on existing customers — but this pattern does not always hold.
- Elad Gil, a venture capitalist, has observed that value from previous machine-learning advances accrued almost entirely to incumbents — producing no listed "Amazon or Google" of AI.
- Existing tech giants are spending enormous sums on AI, positioning them well if the technology proves transformative.
- A broad index fund tracking existing listed tech firms may outperform equivalent investments in private, strictly AI-focused startups.
- The excitement around generative AI has centred on deploying the technology within existing businesses rather than opening brand-new markets — a signal that incumbents, not startups, are likely to win.

## Key passages

> "Nobody knows yet if it is better to have the best chatbot or plenty of customers; having a head start in a whizzy new tech is not the same as being able to make money from it."

> "Elad Gil, a venture capitalist, has noted that the value of previous advances in machine learning, the broader category of which generative ai is a part, have accrued almost entirely to incumbents. The early internet startups have benefited, as have Microsoft and chip firms like nvidia and Micron. The earlier stages of machine learning produced no listed firms that might be considered the Amazon or Google of their niche."

> "Christensen's insights make clear that revolutionary innovation does not always end up being revolutionary in mere business terms."

> "It is possible that an investment in a broad index fund tracking existing listed tech firms will end up outperforming the equivalent investment in private, strictly ai-focused startups."

> "As things stand, it looks more likely that the market value of the technology will end up as a new string to the bow of already giant tech firms."
