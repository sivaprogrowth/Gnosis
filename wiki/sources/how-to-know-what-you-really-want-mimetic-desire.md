## Abstract

Written by Luke Burgis, author of *Wanting: The Power of Mimetic Desire in Everyday Life* (2021), this guide draws on the social theory of [[rene-girard|René Girard]] to explain why human desires are fundamentally imitative rather than self-generated. Burgis argues that desire is a social process — we unconsciously mimic what others around us want — and that this "mimetic" mechanism drives unnecessary rivalry, status anxiety, and career dissatisfaction. The guide offers a practical framework for identifying the "models of desire" that shape one's wants, mapping the social systems that trap people in unfulfilling paths, and cultivating what Burgis calls an "anti-mimetic" life grounded in deeper, more personally authentic values. The piece connects Girard's academic theory to everyday domains including relationships, social media, career development, possessions, and lifestyle choices.

## TL;DR

- Human desires are largely mimetic — we imitate what others want rather than originating desires from within ourselves, a mechanism identified by French theorist [[rene-girard|René Girard]].
- Breaking free requires identifying your "models of desire" (internal vs. external), mapping the social systems entrapping you, and actively authoring your own desires rather than defaulting to mimetic ones.
- An "anti-mimetic" life is not the absence of influence, but the cultivation of thick, deeply-rooted desires and virtues that resist the volatility of social imitation.

## Key claims

- **Desire differs from need**: unlike physiological needs, desire is an intellectual appetite for things *perceived* as good, and it is by definition always for something one feels one lacks — making it inherently unsatisfying.
- **Desire is mimetic**: following [[rene-girard|René Girard]], Burgis claims we do not originate our desires; we copy them from "models" — people and culture whose wants we imitate, often without awareness.
- **Models are internal or external**: internal models (people you can interact with) create volatile, reflexive desire dynamics; external models (celebrities, historical figures, fictional characters) are one-way influences and therefore less rivalrous.
- **Social media occupies a dangerous grey zone**: platforms like Twitter/X straddle internal and external mediation, making desires maximally contagious while creating the illusion of accessibility to remote figures.
- **Systems of desire are as constraining as individual models**: just as chef Sébastien Bras was trapped by the Michelin star system, most people are embedded in status systems that dictate what counts as legitimate ambition.
- **Desires exist on a mimetic spectrum**: from near-zero mimesis (a mother's love for a newborn) to nearly pure mimesis (buying a stock because everyone else is), and desires can move along this spectrum over time.
- **Authentic authorship is possible**: even if a desire originates mimetically (e.g., Ferruccio Lamborghini's rivalry with Enzo Ferrari), one can take creative ownership of it, making it genuinely one's own through intentional pursuit.
- **Anti-mimetic virtues provide stability**: cultivating prudence, fortitude, courage, and honesty creates "anti-mimetic machinery" that keeps one grounded when social imitation pressures intensify.

## Key passages

> 'Man is the creature who does not know what to desire,' wrote Girard, 'and he turns to others in order to make up his mind.'

> 'We would like our desires to come from our deepest selves, our personal depths,' he said, 'but if it did, it would not be desire. Desire is always for something we feel we lack.'

> 'Two desires converging on the same object are bound to clash,' writes Girard. This means that mimetic desire often leads people into unnecessary competition and rivalry with one another in an infernal game of status anxiety.

> To be anti-mimetic is to be free from the unintentional following of desires without knowing where they came from; it's freedom from the herd mentality; freedom from the 'default' mode that causes us to pursue things without examining why.

> 'It isn't things themselves that disturb people, but the judgments that they form about them,' wrote the Stoic philosopher Epictetus. Mimetic desire gives significance to things because of the other people who want those things.

> There is no perfect model out there for the life you want to lead because you're a unique and unrepeatable person, and the stamp you leave on this world will be your own.
