## Abstract

This Economist article examines the evolving challenge organisations face when trying to measure the return on their AI investments, arguing that an early fixation on usage metrics ("tokenmaxxing") has given way to harder questions about genuine value creation. It identifies three distinct measurement layers — input-based, outcomes-based, and organisation-based — and explains why each alone is insufficient. The piece highlights complicating factors including the "J-curve" productivity dip during adoption, the risk of gaming output metrics, and fundamental uncertainty about where the technology is heading. The authors conclude that a mature measurement framework must balance short-term financial returns against longer-term organisational learning and capability-building.

## TL;DR

- Early AI adoption focused on input/usage metrics (e.g. token consumption), which fail to capture whether any business value is actually being created.
- Outcomes-based metrics (productivity, quality, customer satisfaction) are more meaningful but can be gamed and must account for both positive and negative AI effects, plus a J-curve productivity dip before gains materialise.
- A complete measurement framework requires a third layer of "organisation-based" metrics that track how well change is being managed and whether strategic capabilities are being built.

## Key claims

- Usage ("input") measures such as token consumption indicate adoption but reveal nothing about value creation.
- Individual time savings from AI often accrue to employees personally rather than to the employer, making them hard to capture as organisational value.
- Outcomes-based measures must be designed to detect negative effects as well as positive ones; a University of Surrey study found AI-assisted papers from a single health dataset jumped from ~4 per year (2014–2021) to 190 in the first nine months of 2024, raising quality concerns.
- [[companies/google|Google]] engineers are measured on three AI-influenced dimensions: speed, ease, and quality — with quality explicitly weighted to prevent fast but poor output.
- A paper by Kristina McElheran (University of Toronto) confirmed a "J-curve" effect in AI adoption among US manufacturing firms: productivity dips before it improves, especially at established organisations, partly because prior management practices that tracked performance were disrupted.
- Cutting headcount as the primary demonstration of AI financial returns is counterproductive; a better metric is the avoided cost of headcount expansion.
- Attributing incremental revenue to AI requires good baseline data and a culture of A/B testing.
- "Organisation-based" metrics — such as employee satisfaction with AI implementation and progress in building in-house expertise in non-automatable areas — are necessary alongside input and outcomes metrics to capture the learning dimension of AI adoption.
