## Abstract

This *Economist* Bartleby column argues that although employers increasingly prize soft skills—listening, empathy, communication—the conventional recruitment process is structurally ill-suited to detecting them. Early screening filters for hard, verifiable credentials, while later interview stages reward talking and impressing rather than the social attunement that actually predicts performance. Drawing on recent academic research, the article identifies "status acuity" and performance on the "Reading the Mind in the Eyes" test as promising proxies for social aptitude, and suggests practical workarounds such as gathering impressions from non-interviewing colleagues and asking candidates what genuinely worries them about the role. It closes with a warning that soft-skill hiring brings its own risks, including easier faking and amplified interviewer bias.

## TL;DR

- Employers want soft skills, but standard hiring processes are designed to surface hard skills and self-presentation, not genuine social aptitude.
- Two strands of recent research point to measurable proxies: "status acuity" (accurately perceiving who holds influence in a group) and performance on the "Reading the Mind in the Eyes" test.
- Practical fixes include gathering candid impressions from peripheral staff and probing for candidate humility, though soft-skill hiring also raises new risks of bias and gaming.

## Key claims

- A study by Raffaella Sadun and co-authors analysing ~5,000 C-suite job descriptions (2000–2017) found companies have shifted emphasis from financial/operational skills toward social skills.
- Cover letters, CV keywords, and recorded video interviews are poor instruments for assessing soft skills because they primarily measure self-presentation.
- Structured interview scripts enable comparability but suppress the spontaneity necessary for social skills to manifest.
- Siyu Yu of Rice University and colleagues found that people with "status acuity"—the ability to accurately gauge which team members hold influence—reduce group conflict and improve team performance.
- Ben Weidman and David Deming of Harvard University found that individuals who consistently made their groups outperform expectations did not differ on IQ or standard personality tests, but scored significantly higher on the "Reading the Mind in the Eyes" test.
- Interviewing a range of colleagues (including administrative and reception staff who interact casually with candidates) can surface softer-skill signals that formal interviewers miss.
- Research links candidate humility—revealed by asking what genuinely worries them about the job—to better performance outcomes.
- Soft-skill hiring may increase bias risk: perceived irritation toward a candidate could reflect the candidate's poor social skills, the interviewer's mood, or simple dissimilarity.

## Key passages

> "Soft skills matter to employers… companies have shifted away from emphasising financial and operational skills towards social skills—an ability to listen, reflect, communicate and empathise."

> "Putting the words 'team player' on a cover letter or a cv is proof of nothing save unoriginality."

> "People who can accurately gauge which members of a team wield influence are in possession of a magic power they call 'status acuity'. Such room-readers reduce group conflict and improve team performance."

> "Such people, they argued, are genuine team players, capable of making the whole greater than the sum of the parts. These wonderful creatures did not stand out from their peers on IQ or personality tests. But they did significantly better on the 'Reading the Mind in the Eyes' test."

> "Finding someone irritating may be a signal that someone lacks social skills. But it may also mean that they are nervous, that you are grumpy or that the two of you are not that alike."
