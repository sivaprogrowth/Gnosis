## Abstract

This *Economist* culture article examines the growing challenge of identifying prose generated by large language models (LLMs), framing AI as a "ghost writer" haunting the English language. It surveys the stylistic tells and statistical fingerprints — such as a preference for long, Latinate words, overuse of em-dashes, and characteristic hedging phrases — that researchers and editors use to distinguish AI-generated text from human writing. The piece raises the complicating question of whether such markers are reliable as models grow more sophisticated and writers increasingly use AI as an editing aid. Its broader concern is what the proliferation of [[concepts/ai-slop|AI slop]] means for the integrity of language and the livelihoods of professional wordsmiths.

## TL;DR

- AI writing has distinctive stylistic fingerprints — long words, em-dashes, hedging language — but these markers are becoming less reliable as models improve.
- Automated AI-detection tools exist but carry meaningful false-positive rates, risking misidentification of human authors.
- The spread of LLM-generated prose raises structural concerns for professional writers and for the trustworthiness of written information online.

## Key claims

- LLMs are described as versatile "ghost writers" capable of mimicking styles from Hemingway to office manuals, producing thousands of words per minute.
- Certain lexical and punctuation habits — elevated use of em-dashes, Latinate vocabulary, and formulaic hedging — have been identified as statistical markers of AI-generated prose.
- The reliability of these stylistic tells is eroding as [[companies/openai|OpenAI]], [[companies/anthropic|Anthropic]], and other model developers iterate their systems.
- AI-detection software exists but generates false positives, potentially wrongly flagging human-written text as machine-generated.
- The boundary between AI-generated and AI-assisted writing is increasingly blurred, complicating any detection regime.
- The phenomenon is culturally significant enough to be framed as a linguistic haunting — a structural shift in how English prose is produced and consumed.
- Professional writers ("wordsmiths") are characterised as "spooked" by the speed and versatility of LLM output.

## Key passages

> "A GHOST WRITER is haunting the English language. The linguistic spectre can turn its hand to prose, poetry, journalese and corporate jargon."

> "It is frightfully versatile: you can get it to mimic Shakespeare's sonnets or a schlocky beach read; Ernest Hemingway's taut prose or the office-printer manual."

> "It is frightfully fast, churning out thousands of words a minute. (Hemingway rarely produced as many in a day, and required much more booze.) Wordsmiths are spooked."
