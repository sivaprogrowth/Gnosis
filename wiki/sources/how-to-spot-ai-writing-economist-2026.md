## Abstract

This *Economist* article investigates the distinguishing stylistic hallmarks of large language model (LLM) prose by comparing AI-generated texts against a corpus of human writing spanning 55,940 sentences and 1.2 million words. The study pitted four leading LLMs — [[openai|ChatGPT]], [[companies/anthropic|Anthropic's Claude]], Google's Gemini, and [[companies/xai|xAI's Grok]] — against verified human writing from *The Economist*, CNN, the *New York Times*, the *Washington Post*, and excerpts from hit novels. Contrary to popular belief, many previously observed AI quirks (such as overuse of em-dashes and the word "delve") have largely disappeared with model updates, replaced by a tendency toward polysyllabic Latinate vocabulary, fewer commas and semicolons, longer sentences, and characteristic rhetorical constructions. The findings reveal that AI writing styles shift rapidly with software updates, making detection an evolving challenge for both humans and automated tools.

## TL;DR

- AI writing's previously infamous quirks (em-dashes, "delve", "tapestry") have faded with model updates; today's hallmarks are polysyllabic Latinate words, sparse punctuation, and long sentences.
- A corpus study of 55,940 sentences and 1.2 million words found consistent but changing stylistic gaps between LLM and human prose.
- AI detection tools and human intuition must both adapt continuously as LLMs are trained to mimic human feedback and shed flagged patterns.

## Key claims

- [[companies/openai|ChatGPT]], Claude, Gemini, and Grok all overuse polysyllabic words such as "significant", "increasingly", and "consequences" relative to human writers.
- LLMs favour nominalisations (e.g. "expansion" from "expand") and scientific lingo ("parameter", "methodology") at higher rates than humans.
- LLMs use more Latinate suffixes than human writers, consistent with what George Orwell called "pretentious diction".
- Contrary to widespread belief, only Claude still uses more em-dashes than human writers; ChatGPT now uses markedly fewer em-dashes than any writer in the study.
- LLMs use fewer commas, semicolons, and parentheses than humans, partly because they write longer sentences and rarely quote experts.
- Favourite LLM rhetorical devices include "not X but Y", "not only but also", and the rule of three; ChatGPT and Claude use these constructions most heavily.
- AI writing styles are converging toward human prose with each model update, making detection increasingly difficult over time.
- Pangram, a leading AI detection firm claiming 99.98% accuracy, has partnered with Substack, but detection tools may struggle in future as models continue to adapt.

## Key passages

> "Spotting AI texts can be tricky. This is in part because you need evidence beyond a few words or dashes: claiming that a text is by an LLM because it uses the word 'delve' is like claiming one is by Jane Austen because it uses 'imprudence'."

> "There is no single style of AI writing, explains Karolina Rudnicka, a linguist at the University of Gdansk in Poland, just as there is no single style of human writing."

> "Much of this language could be described as what George Orwell called 'pretentious diction'. He railed against writers who 'dress up simple statements' with complicated words and jargon to sound clever."

> "A better way to spot AI-generated writing would be to look for texts without much punctuation at all. LLMs are very Joycean about it: they use fewer commas and semicolons than humans (and hardly any parentheses)."

> "With every update, our study shows, AI writing is becoming more similar to human prose… LLMs are trained on human writing and learn from human feedback, notes Tommie Juzek of Florida State University, picking up things people find impressive and dropping things they do not."

> "Take ChatGPT: until very recently it used an em-dash in almost every sentence. When your correspondent asked an older model whether it thought AI overused the dash, it said: 'Ha—great question!' Ask the bot the same thing today and it soberly says 'they're best used sparingly.'"
