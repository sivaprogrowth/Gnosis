## Abstract

This article surveys the scientific literature on procrastination — its measurable harms and the interventions that show promise in curbing it. Drawing on studies ranging from a 22,053-person earnings analysis to a 2025 meta-analysis of 88 studies across 17 countries, it establishes procrastination as a significant contributor to financial loss, physical pain, anxiety, and depression. It then evaluates several remedies: mental contrasting with implementation intentions (MCII), physical exercise, dietary habits, and pharmacological approaches including lisdexamfetamine for ADHD. The piece closes by noting that modern life — remote work, smartphones, sleep disruption — is structurally amplifying the problem.

## TL;DR

- Procrastination is robustly linked to lower earnings, poorer health, anxiety, and depression across large, cross-national datasets.
- Behavioural techniques (MCII), exercise, and adequate sleep show the strongest evidence for reducing procrastination; dietary effects remain unproven.
- Smartphone use and remote work are compounding procrastination in the modern era, and no drug yet specifically targets it.

## Key claims

- A 2013 study of 22,053 people found each one-point rise on a procrastination scale was associated with a ~$15,000 drop in annual earnings.
- A 2023 Swedish study of 3,525 university students found high procrastinators more likely to suffer debilitating pain, anxiety, stress, and loneliness.
- A 2025 meta-analysis (*Frontiers in Psychiatry*, 88 studies, 63,323 people, 17 countries) identified procrastination-driven erosion of self-esteem as a predictor of depression.
- Mental Contrasting with Implementation Intentions (MCII) reduced delayed bedtimes by ~38 minutes per night versus a control group in two 2020 randomised trials.
- A 2025 Chinese trial found 12 weeks of strength training, HIIT, or a combination produced "significant reductions" in procrastination among male teenagers.
- A 2021 American RCT found adults with ADHD taking lisdexamfetamine showed significant gains in cognitive executive function, including the ability to initiate tasks.
- A 2022 review found all 18 assessed studies linked excessive smartphone use to procrastination, partly via sleep disruption that impairs executive function.

## Key passages

> Each increase of one point was associated with a drop of nearly $15,000 in annual earnings.

> A 2025 meta-analysis, published in *Frontiers in Psychiatry* and covering 88 studies involving 63,323 people in 17 countries, found procrastination and its resulting erosion of self-esteem to be a predictor of future depression.

> In two randomised trials reported in 2020 undergraduates were given online MCII exercises designed to reduce the delaying of bedtime… They missed their intended bedtimes by around 38 minutes less each night than those in a control group given advice about healthy sleeping habits.

> The researchers speculated that overcoming the stress of exertion gave them a "sense of mastery" that boosted confidence and motivation.

> Lack of sleep impedes executive function, leading to more failures of self-regulation.
