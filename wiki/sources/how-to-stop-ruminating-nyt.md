## Abstract

This New York Times wellness article, authored by Hannah Seo, explains what distinguishes normal overthinking from clinical rumination — a compulsive, uncontrollable thought loop that can exacerbate depression, anxiety, OCD, and other mental health conditions. Drawing on interviews with psychiatrist Dr. Tracey Marks, clinical psychologist Michael Greenberg, psychiatry professor Greg Siegle, and philosophy doctoral student Jodie Louise Russell, the piece outlines risk factors for rumination (including gender, perfectionism, chronic illness, and comorbid mental health disorders) and presents a tiered set of interventions. Mild rumination can often be addressed through distraction, trigger avoidance, scheduled "worry time," and grounding in the present moment, while severe or near-constant rumination typically requires formal therapy — such as cognitive behavioral therapy or medication — to address underlying conditions. The article closes by noting that rumination is not inherently harmful and can occasionally foster social bonds or catalyse productive life changes.

## TL;DR

- Rumination is compulsive, uncontrollable overthinking that can worsen depression, anxiety, and OCD — but it exists on a spectrum and is not a mental illness itself.
- Practical self-help strategies (distraction, trigger avoidance, scheduled worry time, grounding) can relieve mild rumination; severe cases require therapy.
- The key marker of problematic rumination is whether your thoughts are causing distress or pulling you away from important responsibilities.

## Key claims

- Rumination is distinguished from normal worry by being compulsive — the thinker wants to stop but cannot — and by focusing on problems with no actionable solution.
- Women, perfectionists, and people with insecurity are more prone to rumination; it is also common among those with chronic pain, cancer, or recent cardiac events.
- Rumination co-occurs with OCD, anxiety, depression, and bipolar disorder; people with depression who ruminate tend to experience worse and longer depressive episodes.
- Distraction is one of the most evidence-backed short-term interventions: studies show it mitigates negative mood prolonged by rumination, while mindfulness showed neither benefit nor harm in one 2008 study.
- Setting a "worry timer" — dedicating 10–30 minutes of permitted rumination time — can break the negative feedback loop where guilt about ruminating deepens the rumination.
- For severe rumination, therapist Michael Greenberg teaches patients to "stop trying to figure out the problem" and simply cease engagement with the thought, rather than suppressing or distracting from it.
- Treating rumination and treating underlying mental health conditions are intertwined; CBT, antidepressants, anti-anxiety medications, light therapy, and expressive writing all reduce rumination.
- Rumination is not universally harmful — shared rumination with a friend can deepen social bonds and, used productively, may prompt positive life decisions.

## Key passages

> "If you realize, 'I don't want to be thinking about this, but I feel like I can't stop,' that's when you know your thinking is compulsive and is considered rumination." — Michael Greenberg

> "Rumination prolonged negative moods, while distraction mitigated them. Mindfulness neither helped nor worsened their moods."

> "Setting aside 10 to 30 minutes of dedicated 'worry or rumination time' periodically can help relieve that pressure. Even the simple act of giving yourself permission to ruminate can help you to feel more relaxed."

> "Your job, he said, is to 'stop trying to figure out the problem.' When you tell yourself that you do not need to resolve the issue, and you believe that it's OK to refrain from trying to find a solution, that's often when your brain can let go."

> "There's potentially a lot of sort of secondary gain that can come from rumination, depending on how you use it." — Greg Siegle

> "What matters most when it comes to rumination is how your thinking makes you feel." — Dr. Tracey Marks
