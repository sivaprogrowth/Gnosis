## Abstract

This *Economist* leaders article surveys the rapid advances in large language models (LLMs) — exemplified by [[openai|OpenAI]]'s ChatGPT — and argues that the resulting mixture of excitement and fear calls for calibrated, proportionate policy rather than either laissez-faire or a blanket "pause." It reviews the spectrum of AI risks, from near-term harms (bias, misinformation, intellectual-property disputes) to long-run existential scenarios, and finds that most catastrophic risk arguments involve substantial speculation and leap far beyond current technology. After comparing the light-touch approaches of the US and UK, the EU's risk-tiered regulatory model, and China's politically motivated pre-approval regime, the article concludes that the EU framework is closest to appropriate — though a more principles-based, disclosure-focused version would be preferable — and that governments should begin building regulatory foundations now, with the option to escalate toward a CERN-style intergovernmental body if credible existential evidence emerges.

## TL;DR

- LLMs have surprised even their creators with emergent capabilities, raising legitimate near-term concerns about bias, misinformation, and job displacement alongside speculative long-run extinction risk.
- Current calls for a development "pause" are unenforceable and premature; regulation modelled loosely on the EU's risk-tiered approach — with mandatory disclosure and inspections — is more appropriate.
- Governments should build flexible regulatory foundations now, reserving heavier instruments (dedicated regulator, intergovernmental treaties, CERN-like safety body) for if and when credible evidence of existential risk materialises.

## Key claims

- [[openai|OpenAI]]'s ChatGPT reached one million users in a week and 100 million within two months of its November 2022 release, demonstrating unprecedented adoption speed for an AI product.
- LLMs exhibit "emergent" abilities — logic puzzles, code generation, emoji-based film identification — that were not explicitly trained for and that surprised their creators.
- A 2022 survey of AI researchers found that 48% assigned at least a 10% probability to AI causing "extremely bad" outcomes (including human extinction), but 25% assigned 0% probability and the median estimate was 5%.
- The nightmare misalignment scenario — a future AI pursuing goals misaligned with human values at massive scale — requires a large speculative leap from current technology and assumes unfettered access to energy, compute, and money that could be denied to a rogue AI.
- The Fear of job displacement follows a centuries-old pattern; so far new technology has created replacement jobs, though LLMs' ability to perform white-collar tasks (document summarisation, code writing) makes a sudden dislocation harder to rule out than in prior technological waves.
- Three regulatory archetypes exist: (1) light-touch / existing-rules-apply (UK, US); (2) risk-tiered mandatory disclosure with fines (EU AI Act); (3) politically motivated pre-approval with socialist-values requirement (China).
- The article advocates a principles-based variant of the EU model — mandatory disclosure of training data, operational transparency, and inspection regimes — as the right starting point, comparable to regulation of cars, aircraft, and medicines.
- A CERN-modelled intergovernmental body is proposed to study AI safety and ethics, filling the gap left by companies' insufficient incentives to invest in those areas.

## Key passages

> "Should we automate away all the jobs, including the fulfilling ones? Should we develop non-human minds that might eventually outnumber, outsmart...and replace us? Should we risk loss of control of our civilisation?"

> "LLMs can, in effect, be trained on the entire internet—which explains their capabilities, good and bad."

> "In a survey of AI researchers carried out in 2022, 48% thought there was at least a 10% chance that AI's impact would be 'extremely bad (eg, human extinction)'. But 25% said the risk was 0%; the median researcher put the risk at 5%."

> "Such scenarios should not be dismissed. But all involve a huge amount of guesswork, and a leap from today's technology. And many imagine that future AIs will have unfettered access to energy, money and computing power, which are real constraints today, and could be denied to a rogue AI in future."

> "If AI is as important a technology as cars, planes and medicines—and there is good reason to believe that it is—then, like them, it will need new rules."

> "Governments could form a body modelled on CERN, a particle-physics laboratory, that could also study AI safety and ethics—areas where companies lack incentives to invest as much as society might wish."
