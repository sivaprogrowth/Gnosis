## Abstract

Paul Graham argues that universities already possess everything needed to prepare startup founders — the main task is to teach rigorous technical subjects (computer science, engineering, biology, design) and to cultivate a culture where students feel that starting a startup is a viable, normal path. He contends that formal "entrepreneurship" curricula, business plan competitions, and dedicated innovation centres are not only unnecessary but actively misleading, because they train students to pitch investors rather than to build products users want. The two concrete changes he recommends are: normalising the idea of starting a startup among students, and giving students unstructured time to pursue their own side projects. These side projects are, he claims, the best source of deep knowledge, co-founder relationships, and actual startup ideas.

## TL;DR

- Universities should teach deep technical subjects and free up student time for self-directed projects — not add "entrepreneurship" classes or competitions.
- Seeing relatable, early-stage founders in person is the most effective way to make students believe starting a startup is achievable.
- Business plan competitions are actively harmful: they teach founders to optimise for investor stories rather than user-loved products.

## Key claims

- The YC partners look for one thing above all: people who are good at building things and have a habit of doing it.
- "Building" is broad — calligraphy informed Steve Jobs's design sense at Apple; [[companies/facebook|Zuckerberg]] was a psychology major, not a CS major.
- Harvard alumni apply to [[companies/facebook|YC]]-equivalent programmes at roughly twice the rate of Yale and Princeton alumni, suggesting startup culture — not innate talent — drives participation.
- Unstructured time is disproportionately valuable: both [[companies/microsoft|Microsoft]] and [[companies/meta|Meta]] were started during Harvard's reading period, a brief gap between classes and exams.
- Side projects are the best source of startup ideas precisely because the best ideas look implausible up front; conscious idea-searching would filter them out.
- Business plan competitions are "not merely useless but positively misleading" — they frame fundraising as the core of a startup when the real core is building a product users love.
- Business schools were designed to train managers of large industrial companies, not startup founders, and to the extent they influence university strategy for preparing founders they can only add error.
- The optimal preparation programme costs nothing extra: no deans of entrepreneurship, no innovation centres — just strong technical departments and room for students to explore.

## Key passages

> "What do the YC partners look for? It's surprisingly simple. They want people who are good at building things and have a habit of doing it."

> "In fact would-be founders should be doing exactly the opposite of what students do in business plan competitions. Instead of thinking about startups without building anything, they should be building things without thinking about whether they'll turn into startups."

> "Microsoft and Meta have something in common that few people realize. They both got started during reading period at Harvard… Merely eliminating it for a few weeks resulted in two trillion dollar companies."

> "The best startup ideas tend to seem so implausible at first that anyone consciously looking for startup ideas would reject them… So the way to discover the best startup ideas is not to look for startup ideas but just to work on whatever random projects seem interesting."

> "Running a startup is incompatible with being a full time student. The only way to learn how to start a startup is to do it. Those two statements are so obvious that they're practically truisms. And yet so many people manage to remain in denial about what they imply. You can't teach students how to start startups."

> "If you have spare money, give it to the people teaching computer science or mechanical engineering or molecular biology."
