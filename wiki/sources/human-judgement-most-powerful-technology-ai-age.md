## Abstract

Written from the perspective of the President of Iceland, this article uses the 40th anniversary of the 1986 Reykjavík Summit as a frame for arguing that human judgement, dialogue, and trust are the most essential capacities in an era of rapidly advancing artificial intelligence. The author draws on examples of AI-assisted warfare in Ukraine and Gaza—where autonomous or AI-guided systems have contributed to civilian casualties—to argue that surrendering human accountability to machines is a profound moral and strategic risk. A new declaration, *A Call from Reykjavík*, signed by three women presidents, is presented as a concrete step toward enshrining meaningful human control over powerful technologies. The piece ultimately contends that the quality of human conversation and the courage to connect across difference matter more than any technical capability.

## TL;DR

- The President of Iceland argues that human judgement, empathy, and dialogue are more critical than ever precisely because AI can now make lethal decisions at scale.
- AI-assisted warfare in Ukraine and Gaza illustrates the danger of removing human accountability from life-and-death decisions.
- *A Call from Reykjavík* — signed by three women presidents — demands that decisions about war and nuclear weapons remain firmly in human hands.

## Key claims

- AI-guided drones and AI-assisted targeting systems are already causing civilian casualties in active conflicts, raising urgent questions about machine-mediated lethal decision-making.
- The lesson of the 1986 Reykjavík Summit is fundamentally about human connection and dialogue, not just arms control — and that lesson applies directly to the AI age.
- Technology experts [[Tristan Harris]] and [[Aza Raskin]] participated in Iceland's discussions, centering the question of *what* AI should be allowed to do, not merely what it *can* do.
- Young Icelanders conducting community conversations across the country demonstrated that decisions made far from the people they affect are rarely wise ones.
- *A Call from Reykjavík* explicitly calls for meaningful human control over powerful technologies and insists decisions about war and nuclear weapons remain in human hands.
- The author distinguishes dialogue from consensus: its purpose is connection and trust-building, not agreement.
- Alongside technical skills, the author argues the next generation must be equipped with listening, critical thinking, empathy, and collaboration — framed as the most essential capacities in an age of intelligent machines.
- Three women presidents (Iceland, Slovenia, and former Irish President) convening at Höfði House is presented as symbolically expanding the range of voices in global governance.

## Key passages

> "As machines become more involved in decisions of life and death, are we losing sight of the human beings whose lives hang in the balance?"

> "We must not surrender human judgment, accountability, or conscience to machines."

> "In an age of intelligent machines, these may be our most essential skills."

> "The future will not be shaped only by the power of our technology or our weapons, but by the quality of our conversations—and the courage of the choices that follow."

> "Decisions made far from the people they affect are rarely wise ones."

> "Dialogue is more than exchanging opinions. At its best, it is an act of recognition: I see you. Your experience matters. Your future matters."
