## Abstract

India's 2023 annual budget proposed extending the country's so-called "angel tax" — originally a 2013 rule taxing investment received at valuations above "fair value" as ordinary income — to cover foreign investors, including registered VC firms and pension funds not registered with India's securities regulator. The rule was born of a money-laundering scandal but has long burdened domestic startups by imposing punishing compliance costs and deterring early-stage backers. The extension threatens to dry up the foreign capital that accounts for the lion's share of early-stage startup funding in India, arriving at a moment when VC investment has already fallen 80% year-on-year. The article argues the provision contradicts Prime Minister Narendra Modi's stated ambition to make India a "startup nation" and calls for the budget to be amended before it takes effect on 1 April 2023.

## TL;DR

- India's 2023 budget would extend the "angel tax" to foreign investors, treating above-fair-value fundraising rounds as taxable income for recipient startups.
- The rule creates prohibitive compliance burdens — costly valuations, tax authority interrogations, and enormous penalty exposure — that have already killed at least one documented startup.
- Indian VC leaders and entrepreneurs warn the extension is an existential threat to the country's startup ecosystem and are lobbying for an amendment before the April 1st effective date.

## Key claims

- VC investment in Indian startups fell 80% year-on-year in January 2023, and the angel tax extension compounds an already severe funding drought.
- The angel tax originally targeted a specific money-laundering scheme in southern India, not startups, but its design makes growth-stage tech companies especially vulnerable because their valuations rely on projected future profits, not current revenues.
- The new provision would cover any foreign investor — including institutional VC funds and pension funds — not registered with India's securities regulator (SEBI).
- Compliance requires startups to furnish sales projections and pay for third-party endorsements of fundraising valuations from accountants and bankers.
- Angel investors subjected to the tax face intrusive inquiries from tax authorities about the source of their funds, causing many to withdraw from deals.
- Nikunj Bubna's firm Whats Extra India was rendered defunct after a tax notice assessed 33% income tax plus 200% penalties on earlier funding rounds, requiring a 20% deposit just to appeal.
- Tushar Sachade of PwC reports being flooded with inquiries from foreign investors alarmed by the new provision; Indian founders report that pledged foreign capital has evaporated.
- India's tax authorities have a track record of pursuing multinationals retroactively — the Vodafone case dragged on for eight years before settling in 2021.

## Key passages

> A new tax provision buried in the latest annual budget… broadens a rule from 2013 that treats most investments from unregistered VC backers, such as rich individuals, family offices and other such "angel" investors, as the recipient's income if the accompanying valuation is "in excess of fair value".

> After that a notice arrived from the tax authority subjecting the earlier rounds to a 33% income tax and penalties equal to 200% of the total money raised. Appealing against the decision required a deposit amounting to 20% of the full amount owed, plus years in court.

> The process suffocated Mr Bubna's firm, which is now defunct. Not all startups shared its fate: until recently few had problems securing early backing. But the extension of the rules to foreigners, who are believed to account for the lion's share of those early backers, may put many more in peril.

> Siddarth Pai, a venture capitalist, has called it "a shame of a tax" that will drive entrepreneurs abroad.

> The prime minister, Narendra Modi, talks fondly of India as a "startup nation". He should tell that to his budget-drafters.
