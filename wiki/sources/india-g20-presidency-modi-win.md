## Abstract

The Economist argues that India's G20 presidency (2023) is primarily a domestic political win for Prime Minister Narendra Modi rather than a vehicle for meaningful multilateral progress. While India has positioned itself as the "voice of the global South" and set an ambitious agenda covering inclusive growth, climate finance, debt restructuring, and UN reform, the G20's deep geopolitical fault-lines — particularly over Russia's invasion of Ukraine and China's resistance to sovereign-debt restructuring — make consensus on any major item highly unlikely. The article contends that Modi's government is using the presidency as a year-long branding exercise ahead of the 2024 Indian general election, plastering cities with G20 and BJP-adjacent imagery, while India's own foreign-policy contradictions and grandstanding undermine any claim to genuine global leadership.

## TL;DR

- India's G20 presidency is structured to boost Narendra Modi's domestic standing ahead of the 2024 general election, more than to achieve multilateral outcomes.
- Deep divisions over Ukraine, debt restructuring, and climate finance make substantive G20 progress under India's stewardship extremely unlikely.
- Western governments are willing to overlook India's democratic backsliding and anti-Western posturing because they need India as a counterweight to China.

## Key claims

- The visual branding of India's G20 presidency closely mirrors BJP party imagery and is saturated with images of Modi, signalling a primarily political project.
- India frames itself as the "voice of the global South," seeking to elevate developing-country priorities such as climate finance and reform of multilateral institutions.
- India almost certainly cannot win a permanent UN Security Council seat and is using the G20 as a substitute forum for global influence.
- The G20's one standout success was the April 2009 coordinated financial-stability response; today members are not comparably united on any issue.
- At the inaugural finance ministers' meeting in Bengaluru, China and Russia refused to sign even a heavily qualified joint statement on Ukraine's economic consequences.
- China's unwillingness to participate in sovereign-debt restructuring and Western failures on climate-finance commitments are two additional structural deadlocks.
- India's foreign policy elevates "narrow self-interest, issue-by-issue" to doctrine, making the horse-trading required for consensus unlikely.
- Foreign Minister Jaishankar characterised Western criticism of Modi (over the Gujarat pogrom BBC documentary) as foreign interference, illustrating how grandstanding cuts across diplomatic credibility.

## Key passages

> "India will probably never get the permanent seat on the UN Security Council that it wants, so it's trying to work through other forums." — Mohan Kumar, former Indian ambassador to France

> "It elevates the pursuit of narrow self-interest, issue-by-issue and never mind the contradictions, to the level of doctrine."

> "There is a phrase called 'war by other means'. This is politics by other means…I don't know if election season has started in India, but for sure it has started in London and New York." — Subrahmanyam Jaishankar, India's foreign minister

> "It's becoming difficult for the G20 to engage in constructive discussion because of Russia's invasion of Ukraine." — Suzuki Shunichi, Japan's finance minister

> "China has made it clear it will not participate in restructuring, and Western countries will not accept haircuts only for the money to be spent on paying China back." — Heribert Dieter, German Institute for International and Security Affairs

> "The motto of India's presidency is: 'Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam', or 'One Earth, One Family, One Future'. And one leader, an Indian observer might add."
