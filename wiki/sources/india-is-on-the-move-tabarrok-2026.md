## Abstract

Alex Tabarrok argues that India's economic rise in the twenty-first century is one of history's great development stories, often undersold because observers fixate on unfavourable comparisons with China. India reached a GDP per capita of $10,039 (PPP, 2021 international dollars) in 2025—nearly five times its 1990 level and roughly equivalent to China's level in late 2009—while growing faster than China at approximately 6% per capita annually. Beyond headline income figures, the article documents dramatic improvements in electrification, sanitation, infant mortality, and life expectancy, alongside a collapse in extreme poverty from 47.5% in 1993 to 2.6% in 2023. Tabarrok closes with a broader structural argument: the historical correlation between large population and low income is dissolving, and none of the world's nine largest countries now qualify as low-income by World Bank standards.

## TL;DR

- India's GDP per capita has nearly quintupled since 1990 and now matches China's 2009 level, with growth running at ~6% per capita—enough to double living standards every twelve years.
- Extreme poverty has collapsed from 47.5% (1993) to 2.6% (2023), and basic infrastructure metrics (electricity access, sanitation, infant mortality) have improved dramatically.
- The era of "big and poor" is over: large population is no longer correlated with low income, reversing a centuries-long historical contingency.

## Key claims

- India reached $10,039 GDP per capita (PPP, 2021 int'l dollars) in 2025, nearly five times its 1990 level, lagging China by approximately 15 years—roughly the gap between Deng Xiaoping's 1978 reforms and India's 1991 dismantling of the [[license-raj]].
- At ~6% per-capita growth, India's living standards double every twelve years; continued growth would make India a $20,000-per-capita country within another decade and a half.
- Extreme poverty (World Bank $3.00/day, 2021 PPP) fell from 47.5% in 1993 and 27.1% in 2011 to just 2.6% in 2023.
- Electricity access rose from ~60% in 2000 to 99.9% by 2024; open defecation fell from 73.2% to 6.7% over the same period.
- Infant mortality fell 72% since 1990 (from 84.4 to 23.3 per 1,000 live births); life expectancy rose nearly fourteen years to 72.2.
- The historical "big-and-poor" correlation was a contingency of which nations adopted capitalism first, not a causal relationship—India and China's rises are restoring historical normality.
- None of the world's nine largest countries are now classified as low-income by the World Bank; poverty is increasingly concentrated in smaller, fragile, conflict-ridden states.
- Key remaining challenges include freer labor and land markets, cleaner air, faster courts, and greater state capacity.

## Key passages

> India reached a GDP per capita of $10,039 in 2025, (PPP constant 2021 international dollars), nearly five times its 1990 level. India now has the same GDP per capita China had in late 2009! India has lagged behind China but by only about 15 years, approximately the time difference between Deng Xiaoping's 1978 economic opening and India's 1991 dismantling of the License Raj–a blink in historical time.

> At 6% per-capita growth, living standards double every twelve years. Another decade and a half of this and India is a $20,000-per-capita middle-income country, looking more like today's Brazil or Mexico than the India of Mother Theresa.

> Extreme poverty in India fell from 47.5 percent in 1993—and 27.1 percent as recently as 2011—all the way down to 2.6 percent in 2023 (using the World Bank's extreme poverty line, $3.00 a day in 2021 PPP). Hundreds of millions of people climbed out of destitution in a single generation.

> In 2000, four in ten Indians lacked electricity and nearly three-quarters practiced open defecation. By 2024, electricity reached 99.9 percent and open defecation had collapsed from 73.2 percent to 6.7 percent.

> Many observers, notably Paul Ehrlich, mistook that correlation for causation and panicked about a "population bomb." But big and poor wasn't causal—it was a historical contingency that a handful of smaller nations adopted capitalism first. It didn't have to be that way, and today we are returning to historical normality: a world where gaps in GDP per capita no longer overwhelm _capita_, and big countries are rich countries too.

> Today, none of the world's nine largest countries are classified as low-income by the World Bank. Only Ethiopia—tenth on the list—remains in the World Bank's low-income tier, and even Ethiopia has more than tripled its income per person since 1990.
