## Abstract

This *Economist* piece examines how India's state governments use high-profile investor summits — most famously "Vibrant Gujarat," launched by Narendra Modi in 2003 — to attract foreign and domestic capital in an era of "competitive federalism." Using the March 2023 "Advantage Andhra Pradesh: Global Investors Summit" in Visakhapatnam as a case study, the article argues that such summits serve multiple functions beyond the headline memoranda of understanding (MoUs): marketing the state's business climate, signalling political commitment to investment inside the bureaucracy, and surfacing the operational needs of companies. Although actual investment materialises at only a quarter to a third of announced figures, analysts and economists suggest the summits' softer functions make them valuable policy instruments regardless.

## TL;DR

- Indian state governments routinely stage investor summits where billions in MoUs are signed, but actual capital inflows typically reach only 25–33% of announced totals.
- The summits originated with Narendra Modi's "Vibrant Gujarat" in 2003 and have spread to nearly every Indian state as a tool of competitive federalism.
- Beyond headline numbers, summits market states to businesses, discipline bureaucracies, and open dialogue about what companies actually need to invest.

## Key claims

- Narendra Modi pioneered the investor-summit model with "Vibrant Gujarat" in 2003, partly to rehabilitate his image after the Gujarat riots of 2002.
- Investment summits now span Indian states of widely varying economic size, from "Magnetic Maharashtra" (2019–20 GDP: $340bn) to "Investgarh Chhattisgarh" ($43bn).
- The Andhra Pradesh Global Investors Summit 2023 generated 352 MoUs worth ₹13trn (~$159bn) with a projected 600,000 jobs over two days.
- Investigative reporting consistently finds that actual investment flows are only a fraction — typically one quarter to one third — of figures announced at such summits.
- According to Suyash Rai of Carnegie India, post-1991 liberalisation shifted investment policy power to the states, making interstate competition the dominant dynamic.
- Economist Sonal Varma of Nomura describes summits as "a starting point," noting that the aggregate business climate ultimately determines whether companies invest.
- Summits function as an internal governance signal: by roping in government departments, political leaders communicate urgency to civil servants around licences and permits.
- Cluster effects are a potential spillover: attracting one firm in a sector can seed broader industry clusters by drawing competitors and suppliers.

## Key passages

> "The model was established in 2003 by Narendra Modi, now India's prime minister. The previous year the state of Gujarat had witnessed religious riots in which more than 1,000 people were killed, mostly Muslims. The episode turned Mr Modi, the state's chief minister at the time, into a pariah. With the support of sympathetic CEOs, he organised the first 'Vibrant Gujarat' summit."

> "Such numbers attract headlines and make voters feel good. But enterprising journalists combing through data have pointed out that the actual investment coming in is a fraction—usually a quarter to a third—of the announced figures."

> "Summits serve at least three functions other than signing deals. The first is marketing... The second function is to show state bureaucracies that the political leadership is serious about investment... Third, the summits are a way to understand the needs of businesses."

> "Summits are 'a starting point,' says Sonal Varma, chief India economist at Nomura, a bank. 'But ultimately the aggregate business climate is going to determine whether companies invest.'"

> "Attracting a firm may lure its competitors. The presence of one or two companies in a particular sector could help seed a cluster."
