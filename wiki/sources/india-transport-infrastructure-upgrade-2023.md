## Abstract

India is undergoing a sweeping transport infrastructure overhaul under Prime Minister Narendra Modi, spanning railways, roads, and airports at a scale not seen outside China. The government is spending 1.7% of GDP on transport infrastructure — roughly twice the level of most Western nations — with ambitions to cut logistics costs from 14% of GDP to 8% by 2030. Economic research on past Indian infrastructure projects (the British-era railways and the Golden Quadrilateral road network) supports the expectation of broad gains in trade, wages, and agricultural incomes. However, persistent structural obstacles — including land acquisition difficulties, judicial backlogs, policy unpredictability, and subdued private investment — threaten to limit the full transformative potential of the build-out.

## TL;DR

- India is executing a historic transport infrastructure expansion across rail, road, and air, framed by the Modi government as essential to future economic growth.
- Research on previous Indian infrastructure programmes shows clear positive effects on trade costs, wages, and agricultural incomes, supporting optimism about the current push.
- Structural impediments — land acquisition, weak contract enforcement, policy capriciousness, and low private investment — remain serious constraints on the programme's success.

## Key claims

- The Vande Bharat Express, indigenously designed and built, cuts travel time between Varanasi and Delhi by 130 minutes versus the next fastest service, and 500 more services are planned within three years.
- A genuine high-speed rail line (exceeding Amtrak Acela speeds) is under construction between Mumbai and Ahmedabad with Japanese assistance, cutting journey time from six hours to two.
- India is adding 10,000 km of highway per year; the rural road network has grown from 381,000 km in 2014 to 729,000 km in 2023, and the number of civilian airports has doubled from 74 to 148.
- India's logistics costs stand at approximately 14% of GDP, far above international benchmarks, and the stated policy goal is to reduce this to 8% by 2030.
- MIT economist Dave Donaldson's research on the British colonial railway network found it "reduced the cost of trading, reduced inter-regional price gaps, and increased trade volumes," providing an academic basis for expecting similar gains today.
- Research by Simon Alder (University of North Carolina) found that China's strategy of connecting every city over 500,000 population produced broader-based growth than India's more hub-focused approach, suggesting India's current network expansion could narrow regional disparities.
- Private investment has remained subdued despite the infrastructure push — falling to 22% of GDP in 2020 from 31% in 2011 — partly attributed to demonetisation, a messy GST rollout, and concerns about regulatory independence.
- The Golden Quadrilateral road network was shown to boost economic activity, reduce transport costs, increase trade gains, and raise wages, especially for skilled workers.

## Key passages

> "Long known for its interminable, rattling train journeys, snarled roads and grotty airports, the country is experiencing an infrastructural makeover unseen outside China."

> "India will spend 1.7% of GDP on transport infrastructure this year, around twice the level of America and most European countries. The stated aim of the splurge is to cut the cost of logistics within India from around 14% of GDP today to 8% by 2030."

> "The railways, Mr Donaldson writes, were responsible for 'bringing them out of near-autarky and connecting them with the rest of India and the world'. Mr Modi can reasonably expect his infrastructure splurge to have a more advanced version of the same effect."

> "In the late 1990s [China] set out to connect every city with a population greater than 500,000. Had Vajpayee done something similar, India would have seen more broad-based growth across rich and poor parts of the country."

> "The biggest [roadblock] is land acquisition, which acts as a brake on building anything in India. Its billion-plus citizens have rights and its courts move slowly... higher courts have 6m pending cases and lower ones face a backlog of 42.6m cases."

> "Mr Modi's faith in the transformative power of new infrastructure is well-judged. It is a precondition for the high growth that India — including above all millions of poor and emerging middle-class citizens — desperately needs. But without additional reforms, even the prime minister's shiny new railways, roads and bridges will not be enough."
