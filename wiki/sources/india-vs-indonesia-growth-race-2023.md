## Abstract

This *Economist* article compares India and Indonesia as the two fastest-growing top-20 economies in 2023 and the following five years, according to IMF forecasts. Both countries are pioneering alternative development models at a time when the classic export-led manufacturing playbook — exemplified by South Korea, Taiwan, and China — is faltering due to protectionism, automation, and geopolitical fragmentation. The article analyses each country across four dimensions: leading export sector, industrial policy, geopolitical stance, and voter-management strategy. It concludes that India is likely to grow faster thanks to its deeper private sector and capital markets, but that its increasingly Hindu-nationalist politics poses a longer-run structural risk.

## TL;DR

- India (tech services) and Indonesia (green commodities) are the IMF's top two growth picks among the world's 20 largest economies through 2028, each pioneering distinct post-globalisation development models.
- India's deeper capital markets, 108 unicorns, and Western geopolitical alignment give it a growth edge over Indonesia, but its majoritarian politics is a rising long-term risk.
- Indonesia's "downstreaming" resource-nationalism strategy and Jokowi's pluralist coalition offer a more politically stable — if slower — path to middle-income status.

## Key claims

- The IMF forecasts India and Indonesia to be the two fastest-growing top-20 economies in 2023 and across the next five years, with a combined population of 1.7 billion.
- The classic export-led manufacturing development model (South Korea, Taiwan, China) is increasingly unviable for today's democracies due to protectionism, robotics, and authoritarian prerequisites.
- India's GDP expanded 71% over the past decade versus Indonesia's 52%; both economies are service-dominated and largely informal (90% of India's workers and 60% of Indonesia's are in the grey economy).
- India's comparative advantage is technology services — accounting for 15% of global IT services spending in 2021 — driven by producing roughly 500,000 engineers per year.
- Indonesia's comparative advantage is "green commodities" such as nickel; by 2030 it aims to be the world's fourth-largest producer of commodities used in batteries and grids, and to manufacture 140 GWh of electric-car battery capacity.
- Modi's industrial policy relies on $30 billion in production-linked incentives across 14 priority sectors; Indonesia's "downstreaming" policy uses export bans on raw materials (e.g., raw nickel since 2014) to force local value-added processing.
- India's MSCI index is worth ~$830 billion (24% of GDP) and hosts 108 unicorns, while Indonesia's index is worth ~$123 billion (10% of GDP) and has fewer than a dozen unicorns.
- A key political divergence: Jokowi governs through a broad pluralist coalition that co-opts Islamist parties, whereas Modi mobilises voters through anti-Muslim Hindu nationalism, which the article identifies as a long-run growth risk.

## Key passages

> "Both are pioneering strategies for getting richer in an era of de-globalisation, fraught geopolitics, automation and energy shifts, even as they seek a political formula that wins elections and avoids social unrest."

> "Many countries are rowdy democracies, not authoritarian states (as South Korea and Taiwan were when they industrialised). Protectionism challenges export-led growth. Factories use more robots."

> "There are a lot of unemployed youth, which is a waste of [India's] demographic dividend." — Raghuram Rajan, former governor of the Reserve Bank of India

> "The Indonesian government's flagship industrial policy, 'downstreaming', is focused on natural resources. It uses sticks more than carrots. It hopes that by banning exports of selected raw materials, it will push multinationals to build refineries locally."

> "Arvind Subramanian, a former economic adviser to the Indian government, points out that the zaibatsu conglomerates in Japan and the chaebol in South Korea operated in tradable sectors, forcing them to compete with international rivals. But Adani and other favourites mainly service the domestic market, leaving them sheltered."

> "In Indonesia the government moulds and placates public opinion; in India it sometimes incites and directs public anger. In the short term that may not matter much. In the long run, it may be a serious problem."
