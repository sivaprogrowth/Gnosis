## Abstract

This *Economist* Bartleby column argues that an internal locus of control — the belief that one's actions meaningfully shape outcomes — is among the most valuable traits a person can possess. Drawing on research by economist Deborah Cobb-Clark, psychologist Julian Rotter, and several experimental studies, the article demonstrates that internals search harder for work, save more, parent more actively, and are more likely to act on entrepreneurial ambitions. The piece extends the argument from individuals to organisations, citing new research on AI adoption in Shenzhen manufacturing firms, and concludes with practical advice for managers on how to cultivate high-agency cultures.

## TL;DR

- People with an **internal locus of control** systematically outperform externals across employment, savings, entrepreneurship, and parenting outcomes.
- Most people chronically underestimate their power to influence circumstances — nudges such as a coin-toss can unlock latent agency.
- Organisations should test for, reward, and culturally reinforce agency because it predicts both individual performance and knowledge-sharing around new technologies like AI.

## Key claims

- Julian Rotter developed the concept of locus of control in the 1960s; it distinguishes people who believe they shape outcomes ("internals") from those who attribute outcomes to luck or external forces ("externals").
- Unemployed Germans with a more external locus of control searched less intensively for jobs and accepted lower reservation wages than internals, per research by Cobb-Clark et al.
- Households with a greater sense of agency save a higher proportion of their wealth.
- Internals with entrepreneurial aspirations are more likely to actually found a company than externals with the same aspirations.
- The locus of control of expectant mothers is correlated with active parenting effort, suggesting the trait has intergenerational effects (Lekfuangfu et al.).
- Research by Francis Flynn and Vanessa Lake shows people systematically overestimate how many strangers they must approach to complete tasks — direct requests are refused less often than expected.
- Steven Levitt's virtual coin-toss experiment found that people nudged toward change reported greater happiness than those who maintained the status quo, implying many people lack the agency to act on preferences without external prompting.
- In Shenzhen manufacturing firms, internal managers are more likely to view AI as a skill to master and share knowledge about it; external managers treat AI as a job threat and hoard information (Feng Xu et al.).

## Key passages

> "People with an internal locus of control believe that they are able to shape the world around them; those with an external locus of control tend to think that luck, destiny or other people determine their fate."

> "Why spend time hunting for jobs, after all, if outcomes are disconnected from actions?"

> "The actual numbers were much lower than the estimates, not because people are unbelievably nice, but because it is awkward to reject a direct request for help."

> "Those who did reported being much happier than those who did not. That suggests many people who would benefit from change lack the sense of agency to seize control of their own destiny without a nudge."

> "Andrej Karpathy, a quotable AI researcher, has said that as a trait, agency is both more powerful and scarcer than intelligence."

> "'Ask forgiveness, not permission' is a mantra that can have some pretty ugly consequences if you have lots of high-agency people and no guardrails. But the idea behind it is sound."
