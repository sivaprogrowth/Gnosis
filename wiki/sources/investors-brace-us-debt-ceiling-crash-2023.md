## Abstract

Published in May 2023 under *The Economist*'s Buttonwood column, this article examines the market implications of the United States approaching its $31.4 trillion debt ceiling, with Treasury Secretary Janet Yellen warning that the government could exhaust its cash as soon as June 1st. The piece explains how even the credible threat of default distorts the pricing of Treasury bills — widely treated as the world's risk-free asset — and cascades into collateral markets, stock prices, and global financial contracts. It documents concrete market stress: wild swings in short-term T-bill yields, surging credit-default-swap premiums on five-year Treasuries, and traders manually overriding settlement software to guard against vanishing collateral. The article concludes that while default remains the least likely outcome, it is no longer unthinkable, and even a successful last-minute deal would drain market liquidity by forcing a glut of Treasury issuance to rebuild the government's cash buffer.

## TL;DR

- The U.S. debt ceiling standoff of 2023 has caused dramatic dislocations in Treasury-bill yields and credit-default-swap markets, as investors price in a non-trivial default risk for the first time.
- A sovereign default would destroy the risk-free-asset status of T-bills, freeze collateral markets, and — per 2013 Fed simulations — could cause stocks to fall ~30% and the dollar ~10%.
- Even the best-case resolution triggers market pain: a post-deal surge in Treasury issuance would drain liquidity and push yields higher.

## Key claims

- The U.S. debt ceiling (currently $31.4 trn, ~117% of GDP) is a political device that Congress must vote to raise or waive; failure to do so can cause the Treasury to miss bond payments or spending obligations.
- Janet Yellen warned in May 2023 that the government could run out of cash and accounting maneuvers as soon as June 1st, 2023.
- One-month T-bill yields swung from 4.7% to 3.4% and then surged by more than two percentage points within weeks, reflecting extreme demand for bills maturing before the potential default date and cratering demand for those maturing after it.
- Traders were manually overriding settlement software to prevent defaulted T-bills from "vanishing" from their systems — illustrating deep operational, not just market, risk.
- The cost to insure five-year Treasuries against default via credit default swaps quadrupled over the 12 months preceding the article.
- PIMCO analysis shows the S&P 500 has historically fallen an average of 6.5% in the month leading up to a debt-ceiling deadline, even when the deadline was always ultimately met.
- A 2013 Federal Reserve simulation of a one-month U.S. default estimated stock prices would fall 30% and the dollar would fall 10%.
- Even a resolved standoff would be followed by a large surge of Treasury issuance to replenish the government's depleted cash buffer, draining market liquidity regardless of outcome.

## Key passages

> "Most of the time, the impossibility of America defaulting on its sovereign bonds is taken as a fundamental axiom of the financial system."

> "If the government stiffs corporate treasurers, companies will miss payments to one another and the wheels of commerce will grind to an agonising halt. Make traders' collateral vanish, and financial contracts of all stripes will start to fall apart, unleashing chaos in global markets."

> "One trading boss describes having her team attempt to manually override their settlement software, in order to ensure that bills which mature without being paid do not simply vanish from the system."

> "Even the best-case scenario, in other words, would drain liquidity from the market and may push yields higher."

> "In 2013, during a previous debt-ceiling stand-off, Fed officials simulated the effects of a month-long default. They estimated that stock prices would fall by 30% and the dollar by 10%."

> "Default remains the least likely outcome. But as investors are acutely aware, it is no longer unthinkable."
