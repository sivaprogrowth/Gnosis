## Abstract

This *Economist* leaders article examines whether China's decades-long economic ascent is levelling off and what that inflection point means for global geopolitics. It surveys the demographic, productivity, and geopolitical headwinds constraining China's growth, then critiques the "Peak China" thesis advanced by scholars [[hal-brands]] and [[michael-beckley]], who argue that a declining-but-still-powerful China becomes *more* dangerous. The piece concludes that the most probable outcome is prolonged US–China economic near-parity rather than either Chinese dominance or collapse, and that this stalemate gives both sides incentive to avoid open conflict over Taiwan. The article frames the coming era as one of "superpower parity" demanding careful management of mutual misperception.

## TL;DR

- China's GDP growth is slowing due to demographic decline, productivity stagnation, and geopolitical blowback, with forecasters now expecting near-parity with the US rather than Chinese dominance.
- The "Peak China" thesis warns that a China losing confidence in its trajectory could become aggressively revisionist, especially over Taiwan.
- The *Economist* judges the most likely path to be a prolonged period of near-parity in which both powers have strong incentives to avoid catastrophic conflict.

## Key claims

- China's working-age population has been declining for roughly a decade, and the UN projects it could fall by over a quarter by mid-century.
- [[companies/goldman-sachs]] revised its forecast from China surpassing the US in 2026 (by 50%+ at mid-century) to surpassing it only in 2035 and peaking at less than 15% larger.
- Capital Economics argues China's economy will *never* become the world's largest, peaking at ~90% of US GDP in 2035.
- Xi Jinping's autocratic governance is suppressing entrepreneurial dynamism and may reduce China's long-run capacity to innovate.
- US semiconductor export controls are expected to cut directly into China's GDP growth.
- China's navy could be 50% larger than America's by 2030 and its nuclear arsenal could nearly quadruple by 2035, even as economic growth slows.
- The "Peak China" thesis posits that China is "strong enough to aggressively disrupt the existing order but is losing confidence that time is on its side."
- A flatter economic trajectory, combined with near-parity with the US, gives China rational incentives to eschew hubris and resist invading Taiwan.

## Key passages

> "China's rapid rise is slowing down. Mr Xi promises a 'great rejuvenation' of his country in the coming decades, but the economy is now undergoing something more prosaic: a great maturation."

> "The country faces decay, they argue, and has reached 'the point where it is strong enough to aggressively disrupt the existing order but is losing confidence that time is on its side'."

> "Capital Economics, a research firm, argues that the country's economy will never become top dog, instead peaking at 90% of America's size in 2035."

> "Mr Xi stokes a dangerous nationalism, to persuade ordinary Chinese that critics of his rule are slighting China itself."

> "Beijing's economic power may be peaking, but no other country is so capable of challenging America globally." — Brands & Beckley

> "Faced with decades of being a near-peer of America, China has good reason to eschew hubris and resist invading Taiwan."
