## Abstract

This *Economist* article profiles an unexpected cohort of Japanese companies profiting from the AI boom — not chip designers or hyperscalers, but century-old niche manufacturers whose obscure materials and components have become indispensable to AI chip production. Ajinomoto, Toto, Hoya, Sakura, and Nitto Boseki each dominate narrow but critical segments of the semiconductor supply chain, often as sole or near-sole global suppliers. Two structural forces explain this pattern: Japan's legacy as a 1980s semiconductor superpower that seeded a deep supplier ecosystem, and a cultural tendency to sustain R&D investment for decades before demand materialises. The article notes, however, that this same patient culture translates into frustratingly slow capacity ramp-ups when demand suddenly surges.

## TL;DR

- Century-old Japanese consumer and industrial firms (MSG maker, toilet manufacturer, lens company, stationery brand, textile spinner) have become critical — often monopoly — suppliers of AI chip materials.
- Their dominance traces to Japan's 1980s semiconductor heyday and a cultural norm of sustained, long-horizon R&D even without obvious near-term demand.
- Their Achilles heel is slow capacity expansion, which frustrates the fast-moving AI supply chain.

## Key claims

- Ajinomoto controls over 95% of the market for Ajinomoto Build-up Film (ABF), an insulating material for AI processors that originated as a by-product of MSG manufacturing; its share price rose ~65% YTD, roughly 3× the Nikkei index gain.
- Toto, the world's largest toilet-maker, is a leading producer of electrostatic chucks — ceramic plates that hold silicon wafers during chip etching — with advanced ceramics now accounting for more than half of Toto's total operating profit.
- Hoya (primarily known for spectacles and contact lenses) is a leading supplier of photomask blanks used in lithography to etch chip designs onto silicon wafers.
- Sakura (a stationery brand) has adapted coloured-pencil technology to detect defects in chip-manufacturing processes.
- Nitto Boseki (Nittobo), founded as a textile company in 1923, is the **sole** global supplier of T-glass, an ultra-thin glass fibre essential for AI chip packaging.
- Japan held more than half of global chip production in the 1980s; six of the world's ten largest chip firms in 1989 were Japanese, creating a dense local supplier base that persists today.
- Analyst David Dai of Bernstein argues Japanese companies sustain R&D even without obvious demand, building deeper knowledge and credibility than newer rivals — Ajinomoto started ABF work in the 1970s but only saw chipmaker adoption in 1999.
- Despite expansion plans, Japanese suppliers' capacity ramp responses are described as "distinctly unhurried" relative to AI's pace of advance.

## Key passages

> Ajinomoto controls more than 95% of the market. Booming demand for AI chips has made the film scarce, pushing Ajinomoto's share price up by 65% since the start of the year, around three times the gain in Japan's benchmark Nikkei index.

> Nitto Boseki (or Nittobo), which began life as a textile company in 1923, is today the sole supplier of "T-glass," an ultra-thin glass fibre essential for packaging AI chips.

> David Dai of Bernstein, a broker, argues that Japanese companies keep developing technology even when demand is not yet obvious, and rarely abandon it. That lets them deepen their knowledge over decades. When the opportunity finally appears, they are ready, armed with better technology and more credibility than newer rivals.

> Ajinomoto began work on ABF in the 1970s, as it looked for ways to apply the chemistry behind MSG elsewhere. Only in 1999 was the material first adopted by a major chipmaker.

> When demand spikes, Japanese suppliers can be maddeningly slow to respond… their responses look distinctly unhurried when compared with the breakneck advances in AI.
