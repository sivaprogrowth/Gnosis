## Abstract

Published alongside Jet Li's memoir *Beyond Life and Death*, this New York Times feature presents a video interview with the 62-year-old actor and martial artist in which he reflects on ten personal touchstones — from mala beads collected from Buddhist teachers to a fondness for stir-fried potatoes that recalls childhood poverty. Li traces the arc of a life shaped by early athletic discipline (sent to the Beijing Sports and Exercise School at age 8), a globally successful action-film career, and a thirty-year inward journey through Tibetan Buddhism prompted by chronic injury pain. The piece matters as a primary source capturing Li's own voice on the themes of compassion, awareness, and the search for life's meaning that underpin the memoir.

## TL;DR

- Jet Li's memoir *Beyond Life and Death* centres on his thirty-year pursuit of meaning through Tibetan Buddhism, triggered by injury pain in 1997.
- His personal values — punctuality, compassion, daily meditation — were forged by a rigorous sports-school childhood and a career defined by physical sacrifice.
- Li frames his outward fighting persona as fundamentally at odds with his inner desire to help rather than harm people.

## Key claims

- Li was placed in martial arts at the Beijing Sports and Exercise School at age 8 (1971) essentially by chance, which determined his entire career trajectory.
- His investigation of Tibetan Buddhism began in 1997 when he sought relief from chronic, accumulative injury pain sustained during both training and filmmaking.
- Over nearly thirty years Li has travelled to India, Nepal, and Tibet to study with multiple teachers, collecting a prayer bead from each as a physical reminder of their teachings.
- Li identifies the goal of meditation not as relaxation but as *awareness* — "knowing without judgment" — which he practises for an hour daily and tries to sustain throughout the day.
- His reverence for Ming-dynasty herbalist Li Shizhen reflects a stated desire to help people, which he contrasts explicitly with his on-screen fighting persona.
- Childhood poverty left a lasting imprint: stir-fried potatoes remain his comfort food because they recall his mother's cooking during lean years.
- A coach's punishment regime — running 400-metre laps for every instance of lateness — instilled a lifelong habit of arriving 10–15 minutes early to any appointment.
- Li frames the past thirty years as mental training parallel to physical training, arguing that few people understand how to train the mind for happiness the way they train the body for health.

## Key passages

> "I learned martial arts and made movies, but I couldn't find the meaning of life. For almost 30 years, I have gone to teachers in India, Nepal, Tibet — many, many places."

> "The goal of meditation is awareness, knowing without judgment. I do an hour daily, and try to maintain awareness the rest of the day."

> "People know how to train their body — be healthy, exercise — but not so many understand how to train their mind. For the past 30 years I've been training my mind to figure out the meaning of life and be happier."

> "I fight people in movies, and some think it's cool. But deep inside, I want to help people."

> "If you tell me eight o'clock, I will be there 10 or 15 minutes before and wait."
