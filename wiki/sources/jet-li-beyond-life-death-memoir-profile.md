## Abstract

This New York Times piece profiles martial arts actor Jet Li on the occasion of his new memoir, *Beyond Life and Death*, in which he recounts his journey from being enrolled at age 8 in Beijing's Sports and Exercise School to becoming a globally recognised action film star. The article touches on key career milestones — from *Shaolin Temple* (1982) through the *Once Upon a Time in China* franchise, *Hero*, *Fearless*, and Hollywood productions like *The Expendables* — while foregrounding Li's decades-long spiritual quest through Tibetan Buddhism, pursued as a response to chronic injury-related pain from 1997 onward. Presented as a "10 things that mean a lot to me" video interview conducted from his Singapore home, the excerpts reveal personal touchstones including mala prayer beads collected from teachers and a childhood comfort food (stir-fried shredded potatoes) that connect Li's present life to his impoverished upbringing.

## TL;DR

- Jet Li's new memoir *Beyond Life and Death* blends action-film career retrospective with an account of his Tibetan Buddhist spiritual search spanning nearly 30 years.
- Li credits a near-random assignment to martial arts at age 8 with shaping his entire life and career trajectory.
- Simple personal objects — prayer beads and a humble potato dish — serve as the memoir's emotional anchors, linking celebrity to humility and memory.

## Key claims

- Li was assigned to the martial arts section of Beijing Sports and Exercise School in 1971 at age 8, a placement he describes as essentially random.
- His film breakthrough came with *Shaolin Temple* (1982), leveraging his competitive martial arts training.
- He starred in four installments of the *Once Upon a Time in China* franchise (1992–1997), as well as *Hero* (2004) and *Fearless* (2006).
- Li began investigating Tibetan Buddhism in 1997 specifically to find relief from injury-related pain.
- He states he spent "almost 30 years" seeking teachers in India, Nepal, and Tibet, unable to find the meaning of life through martial arts or filmmaking alone.
- Li hosts a YouTube video series called "So Be It" with his daughter Jada, who also assisted with translation during the interview.
- Li collects mala prayer beads from each of his teachers as physical reminders of their teachings, with compassion and wisdom being the core lessons.
- His fondness for stir-fried shredded potatoes is rooted in childhood poverty and serves as a continuing connection to his mother.

## Key passages

> "I learned martial arts and made movies, but I couldn't find the meaning of life."

> "For almost 30 years, I have gone to teachers in India, Nepal, Tibet — many, many places."

> "They remind me of them and their teachings. That's become a habit. They teach me the most important thing is compassion and wisdom."

> "When I grew up, my family was very poor and my mom always cooked potatoes. Since then, wherever I go, I just ask, 'Do you have a fresh potato?'"
