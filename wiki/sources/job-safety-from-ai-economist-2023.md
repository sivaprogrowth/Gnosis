## Abstract

This *Economist* article argues that despite extraordinary hype around generative AI and large-language models, predictions of imminent economic transformation — including mass job displacement and explosive GDP growth — are almost certainly overstated. Drawing on the history of technological change (railways, electricity, the personal computer), it contends that new technologies rarely reshape economies as fast or as decisively as contemporaries expect, and that structural factors such as regulation, slow adoption, and the limits of AI in physical and human-contact work will substantially dampen impacts. Financial markets, the article notes, are themselves pricing in only modest AI-driven growth over a 30–50 year horizon, lending weight to the sceptical case.

## TL;DR

- History of technological change — from railways to PCs — consistently shows that transformative economic effects arrive far more slowly than predicted.
- AI job displacement will be slowed by regulation, unionisation, and the inherent limits of LLMs in physical and human-contact work.
- Productivity gains from AI are real but modest, and AI could even reduce productivity through distraction, legal complexity, spam, and fraud.

## Key claims

- [[goldman-sachs|Goldman Sachs]] research projects AI could add 7% (~$7 trn) to annual global GDP over ten years, but financial markets imply investors do not believe a high-probability AI growth acceleration will occur within 30–50 years.
- Research by Basil Halperin of MIT and colleagues finds that inflation-adjusted long-term interest rates have *fallen* since the AI hype began in November 2022, contradicting the expectation of an imminent productivity boom.
- Robert Fogel's Nobel-winning work on US railways showed that rail's economic impact was far more modest than assumed — a useful blueprint for assessing AI.
- Tyna Eloundou of [[openai|OpenAI]] and colleagues estimate ~80% of the US workforce could have at least 10% of their tasks affected by LLMs, but even under these estimates the net job loss would be ~15% — not an instantaneous wipeout.
- Automated telephone switching was invented in 1892, yet human telephone operators kept growing in number until mid-century and the occupation didn't mostly disappear until the 1980s — illustrating the multi-decade lag between technological invention and labour-market disruption.
- Heavily regulated sectors (education, healthcare, policing) are both the most exposed to AI and the least likely to adopt it quickly, blunting displacement effects.
- David Autor of MIT found that ~60% of US jobs in 2018 did not exist in 1940, suggesting AI will create new occupational categories currently unimaginable.
- AI could *reduce* productivity by enabling legal bloat (longer contracts, cheaper litigation), spam, fraud, and tailored entertainment that distracts workers.

## Key passages

> "Financial markets, the researchers conclude, 'are not expecting a high probability of…ai-induced growth acceleration…on at least a 30-to-50-year time horizon.'"

> "The automated telephone switching system—a replacement for human operators—was invented in 1892. It took until 1921 for the Bell System to install their first fully automated office. Even after this milestone, the number of American manual telephone operators continued to grow, peaking in the mid-20th century at around 350,000. The occupation did not (mostly) disappear until the 1980s, nine decades after automation was invented."

> "About 60% of the jobs in America did not exist in 1940. The job of 'fingernail technician' was added to the census in 2000. 'Solar photovoltaic electrician' was added just five years ago. The ai economy is likely to create new occupations which today cannot even be imagined."

> "In 1987 Robert Solow, an economist, famously declared that the computer age was 'everywhere except for the productivity statistics'."

> "'There don't appear, today, to be any systemic moats in generative ai,' a team at Andreessen Horowitz, a venture-capital firm, has argued."

> "gpt-4 is a godsend for a nimby facing a planning application. In five minutes he can produce a well written 1,000-page objection. Someone then has to respond to it."
