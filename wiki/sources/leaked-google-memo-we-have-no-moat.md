## Abstract

A leaked internal [[google|Google]] memo titled "We Have No Moat" argues that open-source AI development has advanced so rapidly that it threatens the competitive advantages of well-funded proprietary AI labs. The memo points to low-rank adaptation (LoRa) fine-tuning and the leaked [[meta|Meta]] LLaMa model as evidence that powerful large language models (LLMs) can now be trained or adapted for as little as $100 on consumer hardware. The Economist uses this memo as a lens to assess whether a handful of large firms — primarily [[google]] and [[companies/openai|OpenAI]] — will control AI, concluding that democratisation of access is more likely than monopoly. The article weighs both positive implications (cheaper access, faster innovation, greater research transparency) and negative ones (easier misuse, disinformation, erosion of export controls, and regulatory difficulty).

## TL;DR

- Open-source AI, powered by techniques like LoRa fine-tuning and leaked models like LLaMa, can now match proprietary LLMs at a fraction of the cost — undermining the "moat" of big AI labs.
- The barrier to entry for running and customising a capable LLM has collapsed from tens of millions of dollars to roughly $100 and an evening's work on a laptop.
- Democratised AI accelerates innovation and reduces monopoly risk, but also empowers bad actors and makes regulation significantly harder.

## Key claims

- A leaked [[google]] memo titled "We Have No Moat" challenges the assumption that large, expensive proprietary models will dominate AI.
- Low-rank adaptation (LoRa) allows an existing LLM to be fine-tuned for a specific task far more quickly and cheaply than training from scratch.
- [[companies/meta|Meta]]'s LLaMa model, leaked in March 2023, catalysed an explosion of open-source AI activity; its smallest version (7bn parameters) was fine-tuned to rival early [[companies/openai|OpenAI]] ChatGPT performance on some tasks.
- The cost of fine-tuning an LLM has fallen to approximately $100, achievable in a few hours by a single person on consumer hardware.
- The memo's author concludes that [[google]] has no defensive moat against open-source competitors — and neither does [[companies/openai|OpenAI]].
- Democratised access to powerful AI will make monopolistic control by a handful of companies far less likely and will improve researcher access to model internals, boosting transparency.
- Easier access also enables bad actors to fine-tune models for disinformation, undermines Western AI export controls, and makes AI harder to regulate.
- The article draws a historical parallel to Bill Gates's 1995 "Internet tidal wave" memo and Jeff Bezos's 2002 "API mandate" memo as comparably significant turning-point documents in computing.

## Key passages

> "The barrier to entry for training and experimentation has dropped from the total output of a major research organisation to one person, an evening, and a beefy laptop."

> "Open-source has some significant advantages that we cannot replicate." Hence the memo's title: this may mean Google has no defensive 'moat' against open-source competitors. Nor, for that matter, does OpenAI.

> "Activity in open-source AI exploded in March, when LLaMa, a model created by Meta, Facebook's parent, was leaked online… it was quickly fine-tuned to produce results comparable to the original version of ChatGPT on some tasks."

> "It will make access to AI much cheaper, accelerate innovation across the field and make it easier for researchers to analyse the behaviour of AI systems (their access to proprietary models was limited), boosting transparency and safety."

> "It means Western attempts to prevent hostile regimes from gaining access to powerful AI technology will fail. And it makes AI harder to regulate, because the genie is out of the bottle."
