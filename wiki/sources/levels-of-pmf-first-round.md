## Abstract

First Round Capital open-sources the first session of its "PMF Method" program — a 14-week intensive for pre-seed B2B SaaS founders — arguing that product-market fit is neither binary nor a vague spectrum but a four-level progression that can be systematically navigated. The framework introduces three dimensions of PMF (Satisfaction, Demand, and Efficiency), four concrete levels (Nascent, Developing, Strong, and Extreme), and four levers founders can pull to get unstuck (Persona, Problem, Promise, Product). Drawing on data from 500+ investments and case studies from companies like Looker, Ironclad, Verkada, and Plaid, the article contends that for sales-led B2B startups, luck can be meaningfully reduced as a factor in finding PMF. Central to the argument is the concept of "repeatability" — the ability to reliably find, win, serve, and retain the next "marginal customer" — as the true hallmark of advancing through PMF levels.

## TL;DR

- Product-market fit for B2B SaaS progresses through four discrete, measurable levels (Nascent → Developing → Strong → Extreme) rather than being a binary on/off state.
- Three dimensions — Satisfaction, Demand, and Efficiency — must all be tracked, with Efficiency being the most commonly overlooked; a business must eventually be efficient to have real PMF.
- Founders can pull four levers (Persona, Problem, Promise, Product) to get unstuck at any level, with repeatability in finding and serving the "marginal customer" as the core signal of progress.

## Key claims

- Extreme PMF is defined as "widespread demand for a product that satisfies a critical need and can be delivered repeatably and efficiently to each customer" — repeatability is the differentiating element most existing definitions omit.
- The journey to extreme PMF for B2B companies typically takes two to six years and is a "two-way ladder" — levels can be regressed, not just unlocked.
- Efficiency (CAC, sales conversion, gross margin, burn multiple, NRR) is a full co-equal dimension of PMF, not an afterthought; founders who ignore it risk building a "$1-in, $100-out" mirage.
- At Level 1 (Nascent PMF), the benchmark is 3–5 customers, $0–$500K ARR, demand sourced mostly from personal networks, and 1-in-10-to-20 warm-intro conversion — repeatability is not expected at this stage.
- Looker is presented as a Level 4 (Extreme PMF) case study: ~800 customers, $27M revenue, 141% NRR, 77.6% gross margin, and net negative churn sustained over 18 months, reached after five years.
- The four levers — Persona, Problem, Promise, Product — map onto how companies like Plaid (persona pivot), Lattice (problem pivot), Ironclad (promise repositioning), and Alma (product pivot) actually unlocked the next level.
- The "Promise" lever (unique value proposition and how it is communicated) is identified as the most overlooked of the four levers by most founders.
- Founders at Level 1 should prioritize Satisfaction above Demand and almost ignore Efficiency; the priority ordering shifts deliberately as a company advances through levels.

## Key passages

> Extreme product-market fit is a state of widespread demand for a product that satisfies a critical need and — crucially — can be delivered repeatably and efficiently to each customer.

> Our contention is that product market fit is not binary — and it's not something that you get to overnight, despite what all of those "we just went viral" founding stories may have you believe.

> At Ironclad, the early 'aha moments' came from watching our customers use the product. It enabled us to pick a niche audience that we could go after — legal operations — and the specific task they were doing — routine transactions.
— Jason Boehmig, Ironclad

> When you have any signs of early demand pull, you have to follow it and work as hard as it takes to just make it happen.
— Filip Kaliszan, Verkada

> I had no qualms about sinking a disproportionate amount of resources here. In SaaS, focusing on making your customer successful is a retention strategy, not a cost center. Complicated products require education. If you're selling a complicated product, you are in the education business — so the support you provide is everything.
— Lloyd Tabb, Looker

> When I think back to the earliest stage of Plaid's product-market fit journey, the challenge wasn't finding a product that people would buy. It was creating a market around the product that we believed would be very important for the future, but was yet to be proven.
— Zach Perret, Plaid
