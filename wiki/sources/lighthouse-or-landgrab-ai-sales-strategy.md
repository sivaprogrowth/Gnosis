## Abstract

This article, published via [[companies/andreessen-horowitz|a16z]]'s newsletter, presents a framework for AI founders choosing between two enterprise go-to-market strategies: the **Lighthouse** (win marquee customers to generate social proof and create a category) and the **Landgrab** (win on ROI math and move fast across a fragmented market). The choice hinges on two diagnostic questions: how exposed is the buyer who signs, and does social proof travel across the target market? Using case studies from [[companies/harvey|Harvey]], Hebbia, Decagon, and Stuut, the article argues that many AI founders default to the Lighthouse when their market actually calls for a Landgrab, costing them speed and competitive position against both startups and incumbents. The piece closes with a sequencing note: the best companies earn the transition from Lighthouse to Landgrab deliberately, vertical by vertical.

## TL;DR

- AI founders must choose between the **Lighthouse** strategy (marquee customers → social proof → category creation) and the **Landgrab** strategy (math-driven ROI → speed → market coverage), and picking the wrong one is often fatal.
- Two questions determine the right strategy: (1) How exposed is the buyer who signs? and (2) Does social proof travel across the market?
- The most common mistake today is defaulting to the Lighthouse because AI "feels new," when many buyers just want the math to work and will buy from whoever shows up first.

## Key claims

- The Lighthouse strategy is appropriate when buyer exposure is high (regulated industries, systems of record, external-facing outputs) AND social proof travels — as in legal tech ([[companies/harvey|Harvey]]) and financial research (Hebbia).
- The Landgrab strategy is appropriate when mistakes are recoverable and proof does not travel well — as in customer support (Decagon) and accounts receivable automation (Stuut).
- [[companies/harvey|Harvey]] required Allen & Overy and Paul Weiss as lighthouse customers because law firms are risk-averse and no firm wanted to go first; those logos unlocked hundreds of millions in ARR.
- Decagon scaled from zero to eight figures in ARR in 18 months by running rapid, demo-driven landgrab sales, signing 100+ enterprise customers in 2025 alone.
- In landgrab markets, incumbents adding AI copilots (e.g., Zendesk) threaten startups even if inferior, because incumbents already own distribution — speed is therefore the entire game.
- Sales cycle length is a reliable gut check: cycles longer than 60 days with custom POC work signal Lighthouse territory; buyers asking "what does it cost?" before "is this safe?" signal Landgrab territory.
- Companies should sequence deliberately — win a bellwether in one vertical, dominate it, then find adjacent verticals — using the Affirm/Casper pattern as a model.
- Common failure modes include "pilot purgatory" (endless POCs that never convert), "a lighthouse for one" (over-customisation for a single logo), and "grabbing land you can't hold" (scaling coverage before the product is stable).

## Key passages

> The founders don't care that the revenue barely registers. The logo is the whole point. Meanwhile the buyers who actually need the software, and would pay full price for it, have never heard the company's name.

> In these markets, sacrificing speed is deadly because founders are not only competing with other startups, but also with incumbents building AI … you need to get distribution before the incumbent gets innovation.

> Concentrated, status-driven markets carry proof while fragmented ones make you earn every deal on the math.

> When exposure is high and proof travels, you're in lighthouse territory … When mistakes are recoverable and proof travels less, you're in landgrab territory: math closes the deal, and coverage wins the market.

> The signal that you've earned it is buyers approaching you with allocated budgets and asking for a demo rather than asking who went first. When that happens, the lighthouse worked, and it's time to run the landgrab.

> Your buyer doesn't purchase the future; they purchase either proof or math. If they need proof, go win the logo that gives it to them. If they need math, get on a plane and show it to them before your competitor or the incumbent does.
