## Abstract

This article is a curated AI reading list published by Lightspeed Venture Partners, reflecting the firm's broad intellectual engagement with artificial intelligence across investing, strategy, philosophy, education, and finance. Lightspeed frames AI not as a fad but as a durable platform shift, noting over $850 million deployed across 30 AI-related portfolio companies. The list spans foundational books, startup strategy debates (including AI moats in SaaS), societal implications (mental health, education, regulation), financial applications, and academic resources. It serves as a snapshot of the key questions, controversies, and thinkers shaping the AI discourse as of mid-2023.

## TL;DR

- Lightspeed shares a broad May 2023 AI reading list covering strategy, philosophy, education, finance, and academic research.
- Key debates highlighted include whether AI moats exist in B2B SaaS, whether [[companies/openai]] has a durable lead, and how regulation should be structured.
- The list points to both foundational texts (Hofstadter, Russell, Mitchell) and emerging newsletters/aggregators for staying current on AI developments.

## Key claims

- Lightspeed has invested over $850 million across 30 AI-related portfolio companies, treating AI as a long-term platform shift rather than a trend.
- The likely defensible moat for most B2B AI businesses will be not the LLM itself but "scaffolding" such as complex workflows, data integrations, and advanced permissioning, per Emergence Capital's Jake Saper.
- [[companies/openai]] and [[companies/anthropic]] are seen by Spark Capital's Fraser Kelton as the small group most likely to dominate large, capable model provision.
- A leaked [[companies/google]] internal memo argued that neither Google nor [[companies/openai]] has a durable moat, with open-source posing a significant competitive threat.
- FTC Chairwoman Lina Khan argues for a regulatory framework where the FTC is best positioned to ensure equitable AI use and prevent excesses reminiscent of the Web 2.0 era.
- Jaron Lanier contends in *The New Yorker* that there is no AI in the popular imagination sense — current systems are complex tools, not emergent minds.
- Packy McCormick argues that AI tools are fundamentally about *composability* of knowledge, functioning more like an alphabet of complex ideas than an emergence of true intelligence.
- BloombergGPT, a bespoke 50-billion-parameter LLM, represents an early attempt to automate financial tasks with a domain-specific large language model.

## Key passages

> "Successful platform shifts always rest on a set of underlying foundational primitives and abstractions built to support the new paradigm. The AI revolution is no different."

> "Jake highlights that the likely moat for most b2b AI businesses will be not the LLM itself but the 'good old fashioned building blocks of SaaS, like complex workflows, data integrations, advanced permissioning.' This 'scaffolding' will create defensible generative AI-enabled businesses."

> "Fraser Kelton writes that he doesn't 'see how there's anything but a small number of groups providing the largest, most capable models. At this point it seems clear that it will be OpenAI and Anthropic and then tbd on whether anyone else will join them.'"

> "Computer scientist and philosopher Jaron Lanier argues in The New Yorker that 'There is No AI' in the way it's being conceived in the popular imagination — as something that has the potential to become an equal — or a competitor — to the human mind."

> "Packy McCormick makes a case that what the new generation of AI tools really do is allow for composing knowledge and complex problem solving. They're not so much an emergence of intelligence as an alphabet of complex ideas that can be arranged to derive meaning."

> "We're at the cusp of using AI for probably the biggest positive transformation that education has ever seen." — Sal Khan
