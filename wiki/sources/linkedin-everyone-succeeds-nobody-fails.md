## Abstract

Stand-up comedian Papa CJ contrasts the brutal, immediate feedback loop of live comedy with the relentlessly curated positivity of LinkedIn, where every setback is euphemised into a "new chapter" and every mediocre outcome is reframed as a strategic learning. The piece argues that Silicon Valley's "fail fast" mantra is pure lip service: organisations publicly celebrate only victories, causing employees to believe that competent people simply do not fail. This distortion, the author contends, makes people skilled at hiding failure rather than learning from it, because failure becomes evidence of incompetence rather than a normal cost of attempting hard things. The comedy stage is offered as a counter-model: instant, unambiguous feedback that forces real iteration rather than narrative polish.

## TL;DR

- LinkedIn operates as a curated alternate universe where failure is systematically invisible, replaced by euphemisms like "new chapter," "invaluable learnings," and "gaining clarity."
- Stand-up comedy offers a sharper performance-management model: a joke that bombs tells you immediately and forces genuine iteration.
- When leaders narrate only victories, failure becomes synonymous with incompetence, which incentivises hiding problems rather than solving them.

## Key claims

- LinkedIn posts systematically filter out failure, confusion, and mediocrity, presenting careers as perfectly linear arcs from startup to keynote.
- Common corporate euphemisms — "embark on a new chapter" (fired), "invaluable learnings" (company failed), "gain clarity" (made a terrible decision) — mask reality rather than reflect it.
- The Silicon Valley "fail fast" ethos is contradicted in practice: professionals share only promotions, never the experiments that didn't work.
- Comedy functions as one of the most efficient performance-management systems available because audience reaction is instantaneous and unambiguous.
- Failure in comedy is treated as information: a joke that bombs triggers word changes, timing adjustments, or premise rewrites — iterative improvement in real time.
- When failure is hidden, organisations lose the institutional learning that comes from "this didn't work" narratives.
- The cultural conflation of failure with incompetence (rather than with ordinary risk-taking) is identified as the root cause of professional dishonesty on platforms like LinkedIn.
- The author is not calling for a "global festival of inadequacy" but for occasional candid posts acknowledging average outcomes.

## Key passages

> "LinkedIn exists in an alternate universe where everyone has 'been humbled', 'been honoured', 'been thrilled', 'been deeply grateful'... No one has ever been 'mildly confused,' 'spectacularly ignored,' or 'asked to leave.'"

> "There's the Silicon Valley-enabled lip service: Fail fast. Experiment. Take risks. Learn from your mistakes. But then, what gets posted is only the promotion."

> "Comedy teaches you that failure is information. When a joke bombs, you change a word, move a pause, rewrite the premise, or eventually accept that the brilliant idea you've been polishing for three weeks was not ahead of its time. It was just rubbish."

> "If leaders only narrate victories, everyone else starts believing competent people don't fail. And once failure becomes evidence of incompetence rather than a normal consequence of trying difficult things, people become extremely good at hiding it."

> "Nobody gets fired. They 'embark on a new chapter.' Nobody's company fails. They 'take invaluable learnings into their next venture.' Nobody makes a terrible decision. They 'gain clarity.' At this rate, bankruptcy will soon be called 'an exciting opportunity to explore alternative liquidity models.'"

> "Perhaps, life would become significantly more useful, and certainly more entertaining, if just occasionally, someone posted: 'Today I gave a presentation. It was average. Nobody cried. Nobody called me visionary. One person asked where the samosas were. Overall, a decent Tuesday.'"
