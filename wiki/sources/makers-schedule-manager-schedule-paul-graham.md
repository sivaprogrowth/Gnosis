## Abstract

[[people/paul-graham|Paul Graham]] argues that there are two fundamentally incompatible types of daily schedule: the "manager's schedule," which slices the day into one-hour intervals and treats meetings as costless, and the "maker's schedule," which requires large uninterrupted blocks of time to do creative or technical work. Because most powerful people operate on the manager's schedule, they inadvertently impose it on makers — even a single meeting can destroy a half-day of productive work and suppress the ambition needed for hard projects. Graham illustrates how Y Combinator navigates this tension using "office hours" clustered at the end of the day, and calls for broader awareness of the conflict so that managers understand the true cost their scheduling habits impose on makers.

## TL;DR

- The manager's schedule (hourly blocks) and the maker's schedule (half-day+ blocks) are structurally incompatible — meetings that cost a manager nothing can destroy a maker's entire afternoon.
- Even a single anticipated meeting can produce a cascading morale effect that kills ambitious work earlier in the day.
- "Office hours" — batching meetings at the end of the working day — is the canonical workaround for preserving maker time inside organisations that also need managerial coordination.

## Key claims

- Managers operate on an hourly appointment-book model; makers (programmers, writers) need units of at least half a day to produce meaningful work.
- A meeting on the maker's schedule is "like throwing an exception" — it changes the mode of work entirely, not just the task.
- The anticipation of an afternoon meeting reduces the likelihood of starting ambitious work in the morning, amplifying the meeting's actual cost.
- Most investors and powerful people default to the manager's schedule and assume everyone else does too, creating friction with maker-schedule organisations.
- Y Combinator intentionally runs on the maker's schedule and uses clustered "office hours" at day's end to simulate manager-schedule availability without interrupting maker time.
- "Speculative meetings" — informal coffee chats to explore potential collaboration — are effectively free on the manager's schedule but devastatingly expensive on the maker's.
- Graham predicts that founders will increasingly resist converting to full manager-schedule operation, just as they once resisted the expectation to wear suits.

## Key passages

> "When you're operating on the maker's schedule, meetings are a disaster. A single meeting can blow a whole afternoon, by breaking it into two pieces each too small to do anything hard in."

> "For someone on the maker's schedule, having a meeting is like throwing an exception. It doesn't merely cause you to switch from one task to another; it changes the mode in which you work."

> "If I know the afternoon is going to be broken up, I'm slightly less likely to start something ambitious in the morning. I know this may sound oversensitive, but if you're a maker, think of your own case."

> "Since most powerful people operate on the manager's schedule, they're in a position to make everyone resonate at their frequency if they want to. But the smarter ones restrain themselves, if they know that some of the people working for them need long chunks of time to work in."

> "Speculative meetings are terribly costly if you're on the maker's schedule. Which puts us in something of a bind. Everyone assumes that, like other investors, we run on the manager's schedule."

> "Those of us on the maker's schedule are willing to compromise. We know we have to have some number of meetings. All we ask from those on the manager's schedule is that they understand the cost."
