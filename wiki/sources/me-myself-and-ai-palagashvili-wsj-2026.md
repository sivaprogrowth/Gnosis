## Abstract

This Wall Street Journal opinion piece by Liya Palagashvili, a senior research fellow at George Mason University's [[companies/mercatus-center|Mercatus Center]], argues that AI's first major labor-market shock may not be mass job loss but rather a migration of workers away from traditional firms into solo self-employment. Drawing on new Census Bureau business-formation data and federal labor-market statistics, she shows that solo business applications have surged nearly 27% since early 2024 in high-AI-adoption sectors (professional services, information, education, finance, and insurance), while payroll-intent applications in those same sectors fell 6.4%. The piece contends that AI is lowering the cost and friction of operating as a one-person business by substituting for the junior staff and institutional resources that formerly kept knowledge workers inside firms. It closes by flagging a policy mismatch: labor-market institutions such as unemployment insurance, health insurance, and retirement plans remain anchored to the employer-employee relationship even as that relationship weakens.

## TL;DR

- AI is enabling a wave of solo self-employment in knowledge-work sectors, not primarily mass unemployment.
- Census Bureau data show solo business applications up ~27% in high-AI sectors since early 2024, while payroll-intent applications fell ~6.4% in the same sectors.
- Policy frameworks (unemployment insurance, employer-sponsored health/retirement benefits) have not caught up with this structural shift.

## Key claims

- The first major AI labor-market shock may be **worker migration from firms to solo self-employment**, not mass job loss.
- Since early 2024, solo business applications rose ~27% in high-AI-adoption sectors (professional services, information, education, finance, insurance) and were essentially flat in low-AI sectors (construction, wholesale trade).
- Business applications with payroll intent fell 6.4% in AI-exposed sectors, while applications unlikely to hire employees rose 26.8% — suggesting new businesses are being formed without the intent to employ others.
- Federal data show solo self-employment in the most AI-exposed occupations rose ~20% from 2022 to 2025, versus near-zero growth in the least-exposed occupations.
- For management analysts (a proxy for consulting), overall employment grew 12% from 2022 to early 2026, while solo self-employment within that occupation grew more than twice as fast.
- AI tools allow a single consultant to replace the functions previously performed by junior analysts, editors, and designers, reducing dependence on the firm.
- Labor-market institutions — unemployment insurance, employer-sponsored health insurance, retirement plans — remain structured around a single-employer model that AI is eroding.
- The central near-term policy question is whether AI is making workers **less dependent on the firm** while policy remains anchored to it.

## Key passages

> "AI is making it easier for one worker to do tasks that once required a small team. In industries where AI can handle research, drafting, coding, editing and analysis, the result isn't necessarily unemployment. It may be independence."

> "Since early 2024, solo business applications have risen nearly 27% in professional services, information, education, finance and insurance—sectors that have among the highest AI-adoption rates. In construction and wholesale trade, where AI is less likely to enable independent work, solo business applications have been essentially flat."

> "Business applications with payroll intent fell by 6.4% in AI-exposed sectors, while applications unlikely to hire employees rose by 26.8%. People are starting businesses, but not businesses designed to employ workers."

> "Among occupations most exposed to AI, solo self-employment rose about 20% from 2022 to 2025. In the least AI-exposed occupations, it barely moved."

> "The firm isn't disappearing, but for some knowledge work, the advantages of being inside one are. Labor-market institutions have yet to adjust."

> "The most immediate question isn't whether AI will eventually replace workers. It is whether AI is already making workers less dependent on the firm while policy remains anchored to it."
