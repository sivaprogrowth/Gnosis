## Abstract

In this Wall Street Journal opinion piece, Charlie Munger — vice chairman of Berkshire Hathaway — argues that the United States should enact a federal ban on cryptocurrencies. He contends that crypto is neither a currency, commodity, nor security, but rather a gambling contract structured with a near-100% edge for the house, enabled by a regulatory gap that state-level competition in laxity has exploited. Munger draws on two historical precedents — China's recent ban and England's century-long prohibition on public trading in new common stocks following the South Sea Bubble — to argue that such bans are not only defensible but have historically coincided with periods of extraordinary national progress. The piece is notable for its bluntness, brevity, and the stature of its author as one of the most prominent value investors of the twentieth century.

## TL;DR

- Munger argues crypto is a gambling contract with a near-100% house edge, not a legitimate financial instrument, and the U.S. should ban it outright.
- He cites China's crypto ban and England's 18th-century stock-trading ban as sound historical precedents.
- The regulatory gap exists because crypto fits no existing category — currency, commodity, or security — leaving it subject only to lax, competing state gambling laws.

## Key claims

- Cryptocurrency is not a currency, commodity, or security; it is best classified as a gambling contract with a nearly 100% edge for the house.
- The absence of federal regulatory oversight of crypto stems from a definitional gap that existing securities, commodity, and currency frameworks do not cover.
- Promoters routinely acquire large blocks of cryptocurrency at near-zero cost before retail investors buy in at much higher prices, constituting a structural pre-dilution scheme.
- State gambling regulations, which govern crypto by default, are weakened by interstate competition in laxity, providing insufficient investor protection.
- China's communist government banned cryptocurrency because it concluded the harm outweighed the benefit — a decision Munger endorses as "uncommon sense."
- England's Parliament banned public trading in new common stocks for roughly 100 years following the South Sea Bubble collapse in the early 1700s, and that period coincided with England's dominant contributions to the Enlightenment and the Industrial Revolution.
- Munger implies that removing crypto speculation could similarly free capital and attention for productive national endeavors in the U.S.

## Key passages

> "A cryptocurrency is not a currency, not a commodity, and not a security. Instead, it's a gambling contract with a nearly 100% edge for the house, entered into in a country where gambling contracts are traditionally regulated only by states that compete in laxity."

> "In some cases, a big block of cryptocurrency has been sold to a promoter for almost nothing, after which the public buys in at much higher prices without fully understanding the pre-dilution in favor of the promoter."

> "Obviously the U.S. should now enact a new federal law that prevents this from happening."

> "What the English Parliament did in its anguish when this crazy promotion blew up, was direct and simple: It banned all public trading in new common stocks and kept this ban in place for about 100 years. And, in that 100 years, England made by far the biggest national contribution to the march of civilization as it led strongly in both the Enlightenment and the Industrial Revolution."

> "What should the U.S. do after a ban of cryptocurrencies is in place? Well, one more action might make sense: Thank the Chinese communist leader for his splendid example of uncommon sense."
