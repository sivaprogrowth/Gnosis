## Abstract

Neoclouds — companies that purchase specialist AI chips and rent them to model-makers and hyperscalers — have become among the fastest-growing players in the AI industry, with the five largest listed neoclouds growing combined annual revenue from $3bn in 2024 to $18bn over the past 12 months. Their rise has been driven by hyperscaler capacity shortfalls and active financial support from [[companies/nvidia|Nvidia]], which is eager to diversify its customer base. However, this growth is financed by rapidly mounting debt: the same five neoclouds now carry $61bn in borrowings — quadruple the level of 12 months prior — while none generates an operating profit. The article argues that neoclouds face two existential risks: a credit-market shock that raises borrowing costs, and a potential demand slowdown that could cause hyperscalers and model-makers to cut capacity leases, leaving highly leveraged middlemen exposed.

## TL;DR

- The five largest listed neoclouds grew revenue 6× in one year but now carry $61bn in debt and none is operationally profitable.
- [[companies/nvidia]] has financially supported neocloud expansion to reduce its dependence on hyperscalers, fuelling a debt-driven capacity race.
- Survival likely requires neoclouds to move beyond pure capacity provision into software services or government infrastructure contracts.

## Key claims

- The five largest listed neoclouds (CoreWeave, Nebius, IREN, Applied Digital, Core Scientific) grew combined annual revenue from $3bn (2024) to $18bn over the past 12 months.
- Collective debt on neocloud balance sheets quadrupled in 12 months to $61bn, plus $18bn in operating leases; none of the five generates an operating profit.
- Neocloud data-centre capacity (1.5–2.5 GW combined) is roughly doubling annually — about triple the expansion rate of the major hyperscalers ([[companies/amazon]], [[companies/microsoft]], [[companies/google]], [[companies/meta]]).
- [[companies/nvidia]] has provided various forms of financial support to neoclouds to reduce its reliance on hyperscaler custom.
- Interest payments are severe: Nebius's interest costs reached one-fifth of revenue in the most recent quarter; CoreWeave's reached one-quarter.
- Approximately 200 neoclouds now compete globally, and mid-sized entrants such as Oracle and [[companies/softbank-vision-fund|SoftBank]]'s SB Neo are intensifying the rivalry.
- The two primary existential risks are a credit-market shock (widening spreads between neocloud bonds and broader junk bonds are already visible) and a demand slowdown that causes hyperscalers and model-makers to abandon third-party capacity.
- Differentiation strategies include targeting Western government infrastructure contracts and adding software service layers, as CoreWeave and Nebius are attempting.

## Key passages

> "The combined annual revenue of the five largest listed neoclouds—CoreWeave, Nebius, IREN, Applied Digital and Core Scientific—has risen to $18bn over the past 12 months, up from $3bn in 2024."

> "The five listed neoclouds now have $61bn-worth of borrowing on their balance-sheets, quadruple the amount just 12 months ago, as well as $18bn-worth of operating leases. None is generating an operating profit, and all are deeply in the red once interest bills are added."

> "In the most recent quarter Nebius's interest payments amounted to a fifth of its revenue; CoreWeave's reached a quarter."

> "Fierce rivalry compounds the two big risks facing neoclouds. The first is a credit-market shock that pushes borrowing costs higher, further squeezing margins. Investors already seem jittery: the spread in yields between neoclouds' bonds and junk bonds as a whole is widening."

> "A second—and even bigger—danger is that supply and demand rebalance. Right now, vast amounts of infrastructure are being built on the assumption that use of the technology will continue to soar. If the pace of adoption slows, however, model-makers and hyperscalers alike may quickly decide they no longer need the neoclouds."

> "To survive, the neoclouds may need to be more than middlemen."
