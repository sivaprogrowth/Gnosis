## Abstract

The Economist argues that [[companies/nvidia|Nvidia]], now the world's most valuable company at ~$5.4trn, has evolved far beyond chipmaking into a de facto financial institution for the AI industry. To counter the threat posed by hyperscalers developing their own chips and to sustain demand, CEO Jensen Huang has assembled an estimated $300bn in financial backstops, equity stakes, revenue guarantees, and co-investment vehicles with major Wall Street firms. The article draws pointed comparisons to the vendor-financing excesses of the dotcom era — when Cisco and Lucent lent heavily to their own customers — and examines two foundational assumptions underlying Nvidia's entire financial edifice: that its chips will hold their value and that demand for compute will keep expanding rapidly. Both assumptions are contested, and critics including Michael Burry warn that even merely disappointing (not catastrophic) demand growth could trigger Nvidia's guarantees while simultaneously depressing its revenues.

## TL;DR

- Nvidia has pledged over $300bn in financial support to AI customers — backstops, equity stakes, and revenue guarantees — earning it the nickname "central bank of AI."
- The strategy is partly defensive: hyperscalers like [[companies/amazon|Amazon]], [[companies/google|Google]], [[companies/meta|Meta]], and [[companies/microsoft|Microsoft]] are designing their own chips, threatening Nvidia's core revenue.
- Nvidia's exposure rests on two fragile assumptions — that AI chips hold their value and that compute demand keeps booming — and critics warn that even a modest demand shortfall could cascade into serious losses.

## Key claims

- Nvidia reached a $5.4trn valuation and is projected to generate ~$1trn in annual revenue by 2029, with next-year sales expected nearly to double.
- Jensen Huang has committed Nvidia to over $70bn in startup investments and ~$300bn in financial support for customers over the past three years, including a $105bn guarantee behind an [[companies/openai|OpenAI]] data centre in Ohio.
- Hyperscalers — accounting for roughly half of Nvidia's revenue — are building custom chips costing one-fifth to one-third the price of Nvidia's; Bloomberg Intelligence predicts custom chips will reach ~50% of the AI processor market by end of decade.
- The core financial mechanism involves Nvidia backstopping neocloud revenues: promising to buy unused compute capacity at a floor price, making neocloud cash flows predictable enough to attract cheaper debt financing.
- Nvidia has agreed to acquire [[companies/hugging-face|Hugging Face]] for $12.9bn and licensed Poolside's AI coding software for $6bn plus a $1bn equity stake, aiming to propagate open-weight models and diversify demand beyond hyperscalers.
- Morgan Stanley estimates Nvidia's "all-in" debt will rise from $53bn in early 2027 to $200bn by early 2029 as guarantees come into effect, offset by $99bn in liquid assets and ~$200bn in projected annual cash generation.
- SemiAnalysis estimates Nvidia's neocloud backstop exposure alone could reach $175bn by end-2028 if the programme continues expanding at its current pace.
- Analogous vendor-financing dynamics are spreading: [[companies/google|Google]] assembled a consortium to backstop [[companies/anthropic|Anthropic]]'s $35bn chip purchase, and AMD has offered equity stakes to [[companies/openai|OpenAI]] and [[companies/meta|Meta]] in exchange for large chip contracts.

## Key passages

> Some have taken to calling Nvidia the "central bank of AI", because it plays so pivotal a role in financing the industry. That will indeed, as Mr Huang argues, help the industry grow—but it also carries risks.

> As Jay Goldberg of Seaport Research Partners puts it, Nvidia is walking a fine line between "enabling demand" and "creating it". He does not yet think Nvidia has crossed that line, although it is "getting pretty close".

> Underpinning this web of obligations are two fundamental assumptions: that Nvidia's chips will retain their value and that demand for compute will continue to grow at a rapid pace. Neither is assured.

> This scenario does not hinge on a collapse in demand; merely disappointing growth could conceivably spell trouble.

> Andy Li of CreditSights worries that it will keep "pushing the pedal" until "something breaks".

> Mr Huang believes that the AI infrastructure buildout is "at full steam". But Nvidia's investors should be aware that, the more of this boom they finance, the more of any bust they will have to bear.
