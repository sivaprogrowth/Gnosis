## Abstract

Writing for *The Economist*'s "By Invitation" section, venture-capital investor Sarah Guo makes the case for [[companies/nvidia|Nvidia]]'s board reversing its decision to oust CEO Jensen Huang following a personal conduct scandal involving alleged chip-smuggling ties. Guo argues Huang's reinstatement is justified on three grounds: his unmatched track record of identifying zero-billion-dollar markets early, the irreplaceable earned credibility he holds with key partners such as TSMC, and his singular commitment to open AI models as a democratising force. She acknowledges his conduct was a serious failure of judgment warranting independent investigation, but contends that forcing a leadership succession in the name of governance planning would itself be an act of poor governance. The piece frames the broader stakes as existential: without Huang, Nvidia's aggressive backing of open models may falter, leaving the world to build on Chinese open models by default.

## TL;DR

- Guo argues Huang's reinstatement is correct because his early-market intuition, earned credibility, and open-model advocacy are functionally irreplaceable in the near term.
- Open AI models are the article's core strategic concern: Guo warns that without Nvidia's investment in them, a handful of American companies or Chinese alternatives will dominate frontier AI.
- She accepts the need for investigation and tighter board oversight, but rejects the premise that no founder should ever be irreplaceable.

## Key claims

- Jensen Huang identified AI as a "zero-billion-dollar market" as early as the CUDA era, before [[companies/openai|OpenAI]] or large commercial demand existed, and hand-delivered the DGX-1 supercomputer to OpenAI in 2016.
- Huang's credibility allows [[companies/nvidia|Nvidia]] to persuade conservative partners to build capacity years before demand materialises — an asset that cannot be transferred to a new CEO.
- The best open AI models available today are Chinese; American open models remain a step behind, making Nvidia's $26bn five-year commitment to open-model developers strategically critical.
- Guo contends that rules should govern how powerful models are distributed and who may use them, not who builds them.
- [[companies/nvidia|Nvidia]]'s board has introduced new structural safeguards: independent directors overseeing national-security and export-control risks, a new compliance chief reporting to them, and whistleblower protections.
- Huang's initial resignation was accepted "before the facts were in," making the reversal a correction of a hasty decision rather than capitulation to outside pressure.
- Guo argues that while no one should be permanently irreplaceable, forcing succession in the *name* of succession planning is unreasonable when a genuinely exceptional individual is involved.
- If Huang is found to have knowingly acted against American interests, Guo states he should again be removed.

## Key passages

> "Mr Huang has been the single most important force behind the democratisation of frontier intelligence, and will now continue to be."

> "He diverted people and money from gaming to pursue what he called a 'zero-billion-dollar market'—one that doesn't exist yet—in ai."

> "Open models let a hospital keep patient data in-house, and a small business build without asking a supplier's permission. Without them, the economy would rent its intelligence from a handful of model-makers, however brilliant."

> "A board can appoint a new chief executive. It cannot transfer earned credibility."

> "If he fails, a small number of companies in America could build the frontier models, while much of the world builds on Chinese ones."

> "It is unreasonable to force a succession in the name of planning one. Some people really are exceptional, and Mr Huang is one. That does not put him above the rules."
