## Abstract

This Economist piece reviews *Money to Burn*, a forthcoming book by William Cohan about Apollo Global Management and its succession drama. The article traces Apollo's origins to the collapse of Drexel Burnham Lambert and Michael Milken's junk-bond empire, showing how Leon Black and other Drexel alumni went on to reshape modern private equity and alternative asset management. It argues that Apollo — part insurer, hedge fund, bank, and asset manager — best embodies the industry's transformation from aggressive 1980s corporate raiding to today's complex, bank-like financial intermediation. The piece also functions as a broader obituary for the Milken-era financial generation, noting that Drexel's diaspora built several of Wall Street's most influential firms and that their era is now drawing to a close.

## TL;DR

- Apollo Global Management grew directly from Drexel Burnham Lambert's 1990 collapse, with founder Leon Black and peers building firms that now rival large banks in systemic importance.
- Marc Rowan's Athene insurance vehicle — which pairs illiquid high-yield debt with insurance liabilities — became Apollo's defining modern innovation and has been widely copied across Wall Street.
- Leon Black's resignation in 2021, triggered by his $158m payments to Jeffrey Epstein, brought Apollo's succession drama to a head and forms the dramatic centrepiece of Cohan's book.

## Key claims

- Michael Milken, operating out of Drexel Burnham Lambert's Los Angeles office, effectively created the market for risky corporate debt — an achievement the article likens to "having discovered fire."
- Drexel's 1990 collapse scattered alumni who went on to found Apollo, Ares, Cerberus, and Moelis, and one former Drexel banker now runs Goldman Sachs.
- Apollo's initial competitive advantage was buying distressed Drexel-era junk bonds cheaply from failed insurer Executive Life, funded by France's Crédit Lyonnais — a relationship that later embroiled Crédit Lyonnais in costly litigation.
- Marc Rowan's 2009-onward assembly of life-insurance portfolios into Athene gave Apollo a permanent, large-scale source of capital and a structurally superior yield model that competitors have since rushed to replicate.
- The 2007 promotion of Rowan and Josh Harris to "co-founder" status set up an unusual two-horse succession race in which Harris ultimately lost, with Black describing him as a "psychopath."
- Leon Black paid Jeffrey Epstein $158m, ostensibly for estate and tax advice; the scandal forced his resignation from Apollo in 2021.
- The book is described as the first detailed history of a giant post-crisis alternative asset manager, given that peers like Jane Street and Citadel remain private and opaque.
- The article frames the entire narrative as an obituary: as Drexel alumni retire, an entire epoch of Wall Street finance passes with them.

## Key passages

> "What is Apollo? No firm better embodies the transformation of the private-equity industry from the pin-striped corporate raiders of the late 20th century to the post-modern financial savants of today: it is part insurer, hedge fund, bank and university finance department."

> "He more or less created the market for risky corporate debt. On Wall Street, that is equivalent to having discovered fire."

> "Apollo was started by Leon Black, a Milkenite who ran Drexel's merger-advice department. Two other investment firms, Ares and Cerberus, were built by Drexel alumni. So was Moelis, an investment bank. Even Goldman Sachs is now run by a former Drexel banker."

> "The simple idea that insurers should own illiquid debt with higher yields, and progressively more complicated ideas about how to manufacture such debt, have made Apollo a fortune. Seemingly everyone on Wall Street has rushed to copy some version of Mr Rowan's trade, making another Executive Life disaster seem inevitable."

> "'Money to Burn' is a remarkable work about financial genius and human failure... The book is partly an obituary for a financial system that Mr Milken created and of which Mr Black was one of the last big names."

> "Since trading giants like Jane Street and Citadel, the other big disrupters, are private institutions, the world may be waiting a while for the next account. After reading this book, their bosses will be keen to keep it that way."
