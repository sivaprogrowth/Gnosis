## Abstract

This *Economist* review covers *On the Mark*, a new book by researcher Florence Hazrat (Basic Books, 352 pp.) that traces the history of punctuation from ancient *scriptura continua* manuscripts through the Renaissance "punctuation boom" to the modern era. Hazrat argues that punctuation is not a minor typographic concern but a force that has shaped religious texts, legal documents, and literary canons. The review highlights how the printing press standardised marks by around 1600, how the exclamation mark evolved from a signal of wonder to a tool of hysteria and propaganda, and how canonical writers from Dickinson to Twain fought fiercely to protect their chosen marks. The book is praised as a rollicking, fact-dense history, with only mild criticism of Hazrat's own stylistic experiments.

## TL;DR

- Punctuation has materially shaped religion, law, and literature since antiquity — misplacing a pause in the Bible could throw Christ's divinity into doubt.
- The Renaissance and the printing press standardised all major marks by ~1600; the exclamation mark alone went from poetic wonder to Nazi propaganda tool.
- Canonical writers (Dickinson, Baudelaire, Twain) treated their punctuation as inviolable, revealing how high the stakes of a single mark can be.

## Key claims

- Early Latin manuscripts used *scriptura continua* — no spaces or marks — and medieval Christian scholars introduced punctuation specifically to prevent theological misreadings of scripture.
- Florence Hazrat contends that "the history of punctuation is the history of humanity," positioning dots and dashes as central rather than peripheral to civilisation.
- Cicero and other Roman orators considered punctuation amateurish; interpretive ambiguity was seen as a feature, not a bug, for skilled speakers.
- Irish monks in the eighth century were among the first to introduce spaces between words, a key precursor to modern punctuation norms.
- Italian Renaissance scholars invented curved commas, semicolons, and parentheses; the printing press spread and standardised these signs across Europe.
- The exclamation mark, invented by an Italian poet c. 1360 to signal "admiration and wonder," had by the 19th century become associated with emotion and hysteria, and was later weaponised by Nazi propagandists.
- Shakespeare's *Antony and Cleopatra* had only 16 exclamation marks in the First Folio; modern editions contain roughly 200, most clustered in Cleopatra's speeches.
- Major writers treated punctuation as a creative absolute: Emily Dickinson felt "defeated" by a cut dash; Charles Baudelaire insisted on a specific comma; Mark Twain threatened to shoot a proofreader who altered his marks.

## Key passages
