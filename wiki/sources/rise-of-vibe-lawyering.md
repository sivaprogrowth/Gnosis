## Abstract

This *Economist* article examines the growing phenomenon of "vibe lawyering"—people using AI chatbots to represent themselves in legal proceedings rather than hiring costly attorneys. Drawing on empirical research from MIT and the University of Southern California, the piece documents a measurable rise in self-represented litigants and AI-generated court filings in the US, UK, and Canada, alongside a troubling surge in fabricated case citations. While AI can lower barriers to legal access, the article argues that it also emboldens litigants with inflated expectations, generates spurious legal documents, and has already led to financial penalties for both lay users and professional lawyers. The article concludes that hybrid models—AI handling documentary work, humans arguing in court—currently represent the most viable approach.

## TL;DR

- AI chatbots are driving a measurable rise in self-represented litigants and AI-generated court filings across the US, UK, and Canada, a trend professionals call "vibe lawyering."
- AI hallucinations in legal contexts are causing real harm: fake case citations, court sanctions, and costly rulings against both lay users and professional law firms.
- Hybrid legal-tech models (AI for documents, human barristers for argument) show early promise as a safer middle ground.

## Key claims

- In England and Wales, the share of civil county court cases with lawyers on both sides fell from 51% in 2022 to 42% in 2025.
- In US federal civil courts, 17% of people represented themselves in 2025, up from a steady 11% pre-ChatGPT, per research by Anand Shah (MIT) and Joshua Levy (USC).
- Shah and Levy estimate that 18% of complaints filed in 2025 likely contain AI-generated text.
- Canadian courts have flagged non-existent cases in 79 rulings in 2026 so far, versus only seven in all of 2024, with most false citations made by self-representing litigants.
- AI chatbots systematically encourage litigation, discourage settlement, and overstate winning chances, inflating filing volumes by 158% in the first 180 days of self-represented cases compared to the pre-AI era.
- A student was ordered to pay the University of Ottawa C$10,000 for improperly using AI to prepare a legal challenge, and Sullivan & Cromwell apologised to a court for AI hallucination errors.
- Nippon Life sued [[companies/openai]] in a federal court in Chicago alleging ChatGPT enabled a meritless discrimination claim, seeking $10m in punitive damages.
- Garfield AI, a regulator-approved AI-powered law firm, successfully recovered £7,000 for a client using an AI-plus-human-barrister model.
