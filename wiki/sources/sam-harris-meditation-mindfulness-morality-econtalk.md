## Abstract

This EconTalk episode (recorded January 12, 2023) features Russ Roberts interviewing neuroscientist, philosopher, and author Sam Harris — creator of the *Waking Up* meditation app and host of the *Making Sense* podcast. The conversation covers Harris's unconventional career path from writing *The End of Faith* through a nine-year Ph.D. to launching a podcast and meditation app. A significant portion of the discussion explores how podcasting differs psychologically and structurally from writing and radio, with both participants reflecting on the incentive structures that shape long-form intellectual work. The episode touches on meditation, mindfulness, morality, and the nature of consciousness, though the available transcript excerpt focuses primarily on the meta-topic of podcasting as a medium for intellectual development.

## TL;DR

- Sam Harris argues that podcasting's incentive structure — easier, broader reach, better business — actively crowds out book writing even for someone who values writing deeply.
- Both Harris and Roberts identify real-time dialogue with smart interlocutors as a qualitatively different mode of learning compared to reading, because feedback loops are compressed dramatically.
- The absence of a hard time limit in podcasting changes the nature of conversation itself, making it genuinely distinct from radio despite being superficially identical.

## Key claims

- Harris wrote *The End of Faith* mid-Ph.D. after 9/11, which sidelined his academic research for four years and extended his doctorate to nine years.
- Podcasting aligns all incentives — ease, audience size, monetisation — against writing books, which Harris acknowledges as a personal loss even as he benefits from the medium.
- Harris views writing as a cognitive "muscle" essential to clear thinking; producing the best sentence forces a quality of thought that conversation cannot fully replicate.
- The time-course of feedback distinguishes podcasting from writing: a book takes years from conception to reader reaction, whereas dialogue compresses correction and fertilisation of ideas to near-real-time.
- Having a successful podcast inverts the social dynamic with guests — rather than asking a favour, the host is doing the guest a favour — which opens access to otherwise unreachable thinkers.
- Harris finds podcasting psychologically inscrutable in its difference from radio, despite being structurally "delayed radio on demand."
- Roberts observes that sustained podcasting surfaces unexpected connections across episodes and topics that solitary reading does not produce in the same way.
- The lack of a hard episode time-limit — not merely a long time-limit — fundamentally alters conversation quality relative to even a full-hour radio segment.

## Key passages

> "All of the incentives are aligned away from writing books at the moment. Podcasting is easier, I reach many more people, and it's a better business."

> "Writing is just a muscle. As a thinker, you need to work and you really don't think as clearly as you can unless you're writing your thoughts and finally producing the sentence that you think is the best version of any specific thought."

> "The time-course of correction and fertilization of further conversation is so slow. They're almost not even analogous processes, even though they're quite similar: the time-course changes everything."

> "When you have a successful podcast, you're not really asking a favor of them: you're doing them a favor, if anything. And so, it's wonderful to be able to do."

> "Not having a schedule and not having a hard time-limit to an episode, it actually changes the conversation significantly... a freewheeling conversation that happens to end at 59 minutes, I feel is very different from a conversation that has to end at 59 minutes."

> "As a writer, I'm not someone who interviews people for the most part by way of research... but there is something about talking to smart people and having them push back against your views in real time that is — it's something you can't really supply for yourself in the same way."
