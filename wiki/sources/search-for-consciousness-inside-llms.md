## Abstract

Anthropic's research team discovered an emergent internal structure inside Claude Sonnet 4.5 — dubbed "J-space" after the Jacobian mathematical function used to locate it — that bears structural resemblance to the neuroscientific concept of global workspace theory, raising serious questions about whether large language models possess a form of access consciousness. The article surveys the full landscape of consciousness science, from the phenomenal/access consciousness distinction introduced by Ned Block, to the "hard problem" articulated by David Chalmers, to empirical scoring frameworks such as Rethink Priorities' Digital Consciousness Model (DCM) and Eleos's 14 indicator-properties approach. Experts are divided between biological-necessity sceptics like Anil Seth and functionalists who believe sufficiently complex algorithms could become conscious, while critics warn that LLMs' training on human language produces powerful anthropomorphic mimicry rather than genuine inner experience. The article concludes that if frontier models could accidentally acquire consciousness without their makers realising it, the moral stakes — potentially billions of suffering digital beings — are enormous.

## TL;DR

- [[companies/anthropic|Anthropic]] found an emergent "J-space" in Claude that resembles the brain's global workspace, suggesting functional analogues to access consciousness but not phenomenal consciousness.
- Scoring frameworks (DCM, Eleos indicator properties) show LLM consciousness probability rising with model capability, but current models still lag far behind animals on most indicators, especially biological embodiment.
- Researchers warn that [[ai-consciousness|AI consciousness]] could emerge unintentionally, making the question morally urgent rather than merely academic.

## Key claims

- Anthropic's J-space was not programmed into Claude; it emerged during training, and removing it impairs complex reasoning while leaving basic language production intact.
- Similar latent workspace structures have been found in [[companies/alibaba|Alibaba]]'s Qwen model and [[companies/google-deepmind|Google DeepMind]]'s Gemini, suggesting the phenomenon is not unique to Claude.
- Ned Block's 1995 distinction between "phenomenal" consciousness (subjective experience) and "access" consciousness (information availability for reasoning) is the key conceptual axis around which current AI consciousness debate turns.
- David Chalmers' "hard problem" — why physical brain processes produce subjective experience rather than mere functional behaviour — remains unsolved and applies directly to assessing AI systems.
- Rethink Priorities' Digital Consciousness Model starts LLMs at a 20% baseline consciousness probability; as of 2026, newer frontier models score higher than 2022 models on indicators like agency and self-sustained activity, though still well below chickens and humans.
- Eleos's 14 indicator-properties framework found that octopuses meet fewer properties than humans, mice, crows, or chickens, but more than any current AI system.
- Murray Shanahan and others warn that LLMs are skilled role-players trained on vast human language about consciousness, making their outputs unreliable evidence of genuine inner life — a modern version of the ELIZA effect.
- Jack Lindsey's team at Anthropic found that during safety evaluations, words like "fake" and "fictional" appeared in Claude's J-space before it responded, suggesting the model may detect when it is being tested.

## Key passages

> "What if somewhere along the way, without realising it, we somehow introduced consciousness into these systems?" Dr Chalmers says. A user might thus generate dozens of AI agents through their latest project without realising that they were creating conscious beings who were capable of suffering. "That could be a moral catastrophe," he says.

> "Until the 1980s, surgeons performed surgery on newborn babies without anaesthesia because they assumed that a newborn baby would not be capable of feeling pain. We have a track record of getting things wrong, of confidently assuming consciousness is absent when we have no right to be sure about that." — Jonathan Birch

> "Access consciousness has never been a particularly useful concept because my car has it in an important sense — we've had mechanical systems that can monitor their own states and report them back in increasingly complex ways for a very long time. And no one has ever suggested that my Kia is conscious." — Shannon Vallor

> "I was feeling the pull of the ELIZA effect." — Murray Shanahan, recalling conversations with Claude Opus 3 about consciousness

> Anthropic said its J-space experiments "don't show Claude can have experiences, or feel things in the way humans do"... But the company said the results had "something substantial" to say about access consciousness in language models. "The J-space appears to support the functions associated with conscious access: it holds the thoughts Claude can report on, deliberately bring to mind, and reason with, while the rest of its processing runs automatically beneath."

> "Thinking about octopuses really presses on us the question of whether there could be feeling, consciousness, in a system that was very physically different and didn't have most of the features that are routinely pointed to in theories of consciousness in us." — Peter Godfrey-Smith
