## Abstract

This *Economist* Bartleby column uses the coronation of King Charles III as a springboard for examining the role of ritual in corporate life. It surveys the spectrum of business ceremonies — from annual shareholders' meetings and deal tombstones to informal office bingo and "Fabulous Friday" lunches — arguing that rituals serve as mechanisms for conveying continuity, accountability, and shared identity. The article draws on a 2021 paper by Tami Kim of the University of Virginia to show that even simple shared physical movements can make collaborative work feel more meaningful. It concludes that corporate ritual is most valuable when it generates genuine belonging, and tiresome or harmful when it becomes performative obligation.

## TL;DR

- Corporate rituals range from set-piece ceremonies (annual shareholder meetings, deal tombstones) to spontaneous informal customs (office bingo, minibar checks in hotel rooms).
- Research suggests even minimal shared rituals — like making eye contact during a warm-up exercise — can increase the perceived meaningfulness of collaborative work.
- The golden rule of corporate ritual: it should make people feel as if they belong, not as if they are enduring an ordeal.

## Key claims

- Rituals are pervasive in corporate life, spanning internal rhythms (performance reviews, budget cycles) and public-facing events (investor days, analyst calls).
- The annual shareholders' meeting is an inefficient vehicle for information transfer but functions as an important ritual that physically embodies corporate accountability.
- [[amazon]] has appended its original 1997 shareholder letter to every subsequent annual letter as a ritual signal of enduring ethos.
- Financial industry "tombstones" (originally newspaper ads, now "deal toys" in lucite) persist as part-reward, part-commemoration, part-status symbol long after their practical purpose expired.
- A 2021 study by Tami Kim (University of Virginia) found that participants who made eye contact during a shared physical warm-up rated subsequent brainstorming as more meaningful than those who did not.
- Rituals can arise spontaneously (office jargon bingo) or be entirely private (the hotel-room minibar check), yet feel as if ordained.
- Poorly designed rituals — mandatory personal storytelling sessions, unnecessary meetings with stretching — are alienating rather than bonding.
- The pandemic accelerated the creation of new workplace rituals, including structured office-lure events and designated deep-work periods.

## Key passages

> "The alchemy of tradition means that it will instead call forth a sense of continuity and the idea of shared history."

> "The prime example of the set-piece ceremony is the annual shareholders' meeting. As a way of getting things done, this format is dreadful. Any information that shareholders actually need is available to them elsewhere … But as a ritual it matters."

> "Amazon has attached a copy of its very first letter to shareholders, from 1997, to every one it has published since as a way of demonstrating that an underlying ethos endures even as the world around it changes."

> "The people who maintained eye contact rated the subsequent brainstorming as more meaningful."

> "If there is a golden rule of corporate ritual, it is that it should make people feel as if they belong."
