## Abstract

In this first-person essay for the *Financial Times*, columnist Simon Kuper recounts a forced experiment at a Paris WeWork after his private work flat underwent renovation. Having worked alone for 25 years, Kuper found co-working spaces — and modern offices in general — deeply hostile to concentrated work, citing rising office density, constant social exposure, and the exhausting nature of being perpetually seen. He draws on WeWork's own internal 2017 report, which found that members were not forming meaningful communities, to undercut the company's core "community" pitch. The piece arrives as WeWork teeters near collapse, its valuation down ~99% from its $47bn peak, and argues that for knowledge workers with quiet home space, remote work is straightforwardly superior.

## TL;DR

- A 25-year solo worker's forced stint at WeWork confirmed that modern co-working offices are poorly designed for deep, concentrated work.
- WeWork's own 2017 internal report admitted its "community" premise was false — members were not socially connected to one another.
- For workers who have adequate home-office space, the article concludes remote work wins unambiguously over shared offices.

## Key claims

- Kuper resigned from the FT in 1998 partly because open-plan office interruptions made sustained work impossible, and his WeWork experience rekindled the same frustration.
- WeWork was modelled on the coffee-shop aesthetic pioneered by Brooklyn co-working precursor Green Desk (founded 2008), but the density in WeWork spaces is far higher than a typical café.
- Office space per worker has nearly halved in some US cities over 20 years, per data from real-estate firms CoStar and Cushman & Wakefield.
- In the UK, average desk density worsened from 14.8 m² per desk in 2001 to 9.6 m² in 2018, according to the British Council for Offices.
- WeWork's valuation collapsed from $47bn to $503mn — a ~99% decline over four years — as the company restructured its debts.
- WeWork's 2017 internal report "Are Our Members Friends?" found "less of a community at WeWork than we imagined" and that the average member was not socially connected to others in their building.
- Approximately 35% of American workers who *can* work from home now do so full-time, with many more desiring to, per Pew Research data cited in the article.
- Kuper recommends that knowledge workers use the office only when absolutely necessary and prefer actual coffee shops over co-working spaces for the rare colleague interaction.

## Key passages

> I've hardly even spoken to anybody at WeWorks. My life mantra is that the solution to most human problems is earplugs

> It's exhausting to be ceaselessly seen, and to have your concentration broken every time someone near you starts to chat.

> "There is less of a community at WeWork than we imagined . . . The average WeWork member isn't socially connected with others in their building . . . This result was incredibly surprising since it contradicts a lot of our rhetoric about the strength of our community."

> Space per office worker in some US cities has nearly halved in 20 years, report real-estate companies CoStar and Cushman & Wakefield.

> My recommendation for office workers: if your employer leaves you any choice in the matter, and you have a workspace at home that's larger and quieter than the typical spot in a modern office, just stay in your dressing gown and ditch the commute.
