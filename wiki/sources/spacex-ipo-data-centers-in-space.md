## Abstract

Written on Stratechery, this article uses SpaceX's S-1 filing and its eye-popping $2 trillion valuation as a lens to explore whether "data centers in space" — satellite-rack constellations running agentic AI workloads — could eventually be viable and necessary. The author traces Elon Musk's pattern of making all-in bets that change the rules of a market rather than competing within them (Tesla, Starlink), then evaluates the SpaceX IPO's absurd near-term financials against a longer-horizon thesis: that terrestrial compute buildout will ultimately hit hard limits in power and zoning, making orbital racks not just an alternative but potentially the only option. The piece concludes that while the numbers do not yet pencil out, the dream is physically possible and strategically coherent enough to warrant serious attention — and cautious public investment.

## TL;DR

- SpaceX's S-1 is financially indefensible today but the "data centers in space" thesis is physically plausible and addresses a real future constraint on AI compute.
- Agentic inference — large-scale AI workloads with no human in the loop — is the workload best suited to satellite racks because latency tolerance is high and demand scales with compute, not people.
- Musk's historical pattern is to convert shared capital-market belief into physical infrastructure; SpaceX is an extension of that same playbook.

## Key claims

- Starlink generated $8.7 billion in revenue and $4.4 billion in profit last year, making it the consumer-facing engine of SpaceX's business.
- SpaceX is seeking a $2 trillion valuation on $18.67 billion in revenue and $4.9 billion in losses, with the $5.1 billion in AI R&D for xAI dragging results negative.
- The S-1's claimed $26.5 trillion AI TAM is more than 13× the space-and-connectivity opportunity combined — a figure the author treats as illustrative of Musk's meme-driven fundraising strategy.
- Individual rack-satellites are physically feasible today: a Starlink V2 Mini Direct-to-Cell satellite is already comparable in size to an Nvidia NVL72 rack; the main gap is power (25 kW vs. 135 kW) and thermal dissipation.
- Agentic inference tolerates latency, uses relatively slower/cheaper memory, and scales with compute rather than human users — making it the natural workload for orbital data centers.
- Terrestrial data center buildout faces an unexpected hard constraint in zoning and community opposition, not just power; this "stick" makes the space alternative more viable over time.
- SpaceX is already monetising xAI's Colossus 1 data center at $15 billion/year for 300 MW, establishing a pricing precedent for orbital rack capacity.
- The IPO is framed as a return to what an IPO should be: retail investors contributing capital to build speculative but potentially transformative infrastructure, analogous to a Series A bet.

## Key passages

> We believe we have identified the largest actionable total addressable market ("TAM") in human history. We estimate that our quantifiable TAM is $28.5 trillion…For illustrative purposes of sizing our addressable market opportunity, we exclude China and Russia from our global estimates.

> Musk companies at their best don't win the game; they change the rules through scale, such that billionaires buy economy cars because they actually drive themselves (with supervision), and airlines transform the consumer experience on their own dime.

> What seems very plausible in the long run is that the demand for compute ends up being so large that there eventually is nowhere left to build, making the vast expanses of space not just an alternative but in fact the only choice.

> Issuing more stock was not diluting existing shareholders; it was extending the opportunity to propagate the TSLA meme to that many more people…The end result is that instead of infrastructure leading to a movement, a movement, via the stock market, funded the building out of infrastructure.

> SpaceX has already invented a lot, and its early investors are going to make a lot of money with this IPO; at the same time, there is still so much more to invent that there remains a lot of upside — and, to be very clear, a lot of risk. It's a testament to SpaceX's ambitions that retail investors get to play VC.

> I am in fact concerned about our ability to muster enough compute to fully realize the gains from AI, and am very worried about a replay of nuclear power, where our failure to build denied us the opportunity to even imagine what could be invented in a world of unlimited energy.
