## Abstract

This article from Collaborative Fund presents a 17-level spectrum (Levels 0–16) mapping the continuum between complete financial dependence and full financial independence, applied equally to individuals and businesses. Each level describes a distinct relationship between a person or company and those they rely upon financially — from panhandling and family-backed startups at the bottom to philanthropy-as-the-only-drain at the top. The framework argues that true independence is less about raw asset size and more about the ability to say "no" and control one's time. A key insight is that lifestyle inflation and social-status signalling are hidden forms of dependence that undermine apparent wealth.

## TL;DR

- Financial independence exists on a 17-level spectrum, not as a binary state, and most people operate somewhere in the middle.
- True independence — the ability to say "no" to bosses, banks, and customers — first meaningfully arrives around Level 11 (a year or more of liquid savings).
- Lifestyle desires compound faster than almost any asset, making frugality a prerequisite for genuine independence rather than just a virtue.

## Key claims

- Level 0 represents dependence on strangers with no vested interest in your success; Level 16 represents assets so large that philanthropy is the only way they won't outpace spending.
- Levels 4 and below feel like independence but are not — a boss or customer still "owns your day and can dictate your future."
- Level 9 — freedom from status-signalling via expensive consumer goods — is a hidden but critical milestone; the compulsion to signal is framed as an invisible form of debt.
- Level 10 explicitly includes freedom from mortgage debt, which the author views as keeping one "beholden to others, owning a piece of your future decisions."
- Level 11 is defined as the "first true stage of independence," requiring liquid savings sufficient to live on for a year or more.
- Level 12 notes that independence at this stage owes more to a slim lifestyle than to huge assets, because lifestyle desires compound faster than assets.
- Level 13 is the threshold at which assets and their expected returns cover basic living expenses for longer than the owner's life expectancy — the point of full freedom from bosses and clients.
- Charlie Munger is quoted as framing the goal as getting *independent*, not getting *rich* — a distinction the entire framework reinforces.

## Key passages

> "I did not intend to get rich. I just wanted to get independent." – Munger

> "Level 4: Ability to fully support yourself by adding value for others, but value that is marginal and easy to replace… It smells like independence, but a boss or customer still owns your day and can dictate your future."

> "Level 9: Becoming comfortable enough with your social status that you don't feel the need to flash your peacock feathers with expensive consumer goods whose only value is in signaling. The inability to do this is a hidden form of debt and dependence that exists, and it piles up on people who mistakenly think they're wealthy enough to be independent."

> "Level 11: Few realistic situations would cause you, your company, or your family to be pushed back below Level 5… It's the first true stage of independence. You can now say 'No' to almost anyone, with high odds of recovering from the repercussions."

> "Level 12: Interest and dividends cover more than half of your living expenses. Most of this independence is owed to a slim lifestyle, rather than huge assets. You realize that lifestyle desires compound faster than almost any asset."

> "Everyone's different. But while almost everyone can adapt to their stuff, having control over your time through independence will always feel great when you have it and always be a miserable burden when you don't."
