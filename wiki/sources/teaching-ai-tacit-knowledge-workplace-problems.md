## Abstract

This *Economist* article examines the challenge of transferring tacit knowledge — the intuitive, experience-born know-how that workers cannot fully articulate — into AI systems operating within organisations. It surveys several approaches: letting models absorb tacit knowledge implicitly from existing data corpora, using video observation to surface behaviours workers cannot describe (illustrated by Monumental AI's brick-laying robots), intensifying workplace monitoring of keystrokes and screen activity (as Meta's Model Capability Initiative attempts), and using human expert evaluation to progressively refine AI on subjective tasks. The piece argues that each approach runs into serious tensions around worker trust, surveillance acceptability, and data ownership, and raises the deeper question of how machine capture of expertise may ultimately erode the human processes by which that expertise is acquired and passed on.

## TL;DR

- Tacit knowledge — know-how that workers cannot fully explain — is one of the hardest inputs to feed into workplace AI, and no single technique reliably captures it.
- Video observation, intensive monitoring, and expert-evaluation feedback loops are the main strategies, but each faces significant technical or social limits.
- Embedding AI more deeply into knowledge capture raises unresolved questions about data ownership, surveillance, and the long-term erosion of human expertise.

## Key claims

- Some organisational context can be codified explicitly or extracted from enterprise data (e.g. by process-mining tools like Celonis), but tacit knowledge resists both approaches.
- AI's ability to detect patterns humans cannot describe (facial recognition, chess) means it can sometimes absorb tacit knowledge without explicit instruction, simply by training on domain-specific data corpora.
- Monumental AI discovered bricklayers' imperceptible hand-vibration technique only through video footage, not worker interviews, and then engineered it into its robots — demonstrating the power and necessity of observation over self-report.
- Meta's Model Capability Initiative — tracking keystrokes and mouse clicks to train AI — provoked strong employee backlash, illustrating the social friction intensive monitoring creates.
- Research by Danielle Li (MIT) found that workers believe they hold large amounts of uncodified organisational knowledge and are willing to withhold it; workers shown how data trains AI were measurably less willing to sell their data.
- For subjective tasks where correctness is fuzzy (design aesthetics, research quality), human expert rating of AI outputs can progressively align models with professional standards.
- The article identifies three unresolved normative questions: who owns uncodified know-how, how much surveillance is acceptable, and how AI capture of expertise will affect the future acquisition and transmission of human skill.

## Key passages

> "We can know more than we can tell." — Michael Polanyi

> The bricklayers knew more than they could tell. What they could not articulate for themselves, hours of video footage could reveal. Monumental saw, for instance, that masons were vibrating their hands a little bit when they pushed a brick into the mortar. This small movement helps push mortar into the pores of the bricks, creating a stronger adhesive bond.

> Meta's employees have reacted noisily to the firm's Model Capability Initiative, a programme to track keystrokes and mouse clicks in order to train AI.

> Ms Li's survey found that workers think they have the ability to withhold a lot of valuable information from nosy employers. They may well choose to do so. In an experiment, the researchers offered to buy survey data from participants; those who had been shown a video on how data could be used to train AI were less willing to sell.

> As machines learn more and more, how will that affect the way that people acquire, practise and pass on the expertise that was previously gained through experience?
