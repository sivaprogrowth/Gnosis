## Abstract

New empirical research tests eight common e-commerce promotional mechanisms — scarcity badges, countdown timers, strike-through pricing, vouchers, bundles, social proof, assurance signals, and star ratings — across four AI models ([[entities/chatgpt|GPT-4.1-mini and GPT-5]] and Gemini 2.5 Pro/Flash Lite) in over 16,000 simulated shopping rounds. The study finds that only star ratings and price behave consistently across all models and product categories in ways that mirror human buyer behaviour; every other classic persuasion tactic produces unstable, model-dependent, and sometimes counter-productive effects. The research challenges the widespread executive assumption that human-facing persuasion cues transfer reliably to [[concepts/agentic-ai|AI agents]], and argues that advanced reasoning models may actively penalise overt promotional signals. It concludes that firms must treat AI model type as a new segmentation variable and build ongoing simulation infrastructure to keep pace with model updates.

## TL;DR

- Only **price** and **star ratings** consistently influence AI shopping agents the way they influence human buyers; all other classic e-commerce persuasion tactics are unreliable.
- More capable [[concepts/agentic-ai|AI]] reasoning models (GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Pro) are *less* susceptible to promotional cues and in some cases actively penalise them — meaning aggressive marketing can backfire.
- Marketers should treat each AI model as a distinct buyer segment, invest in simulation testing infrastructure, and research the prompt structures consumers use when delegating shopping to agents.

## Key claims

- Across 16,000+ simulated choice situations, star ratings was the **only** badge that consistently increased agent selection across all four models and all four product categories.
- Non-reasoning models (Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite, GPT-4.1-mini) were generally **more** responsive to promotional cues; reasoning models (GPT-5, Gemini 2.5 Pro) were **less** responsive — and sometimes reacted negatively.
- [[concepts/big-brand-bias|Scarcity cues]] had no effect on some models, and GPT-5 reacted negatively to them in certain product categories — a direct inversion of the human [[concepts/artificial-scarcity|scarcity]] response.
- Strike-through pricing produced no consistent anchoring effect; for Gemini 2.5 Pro, more extreme discount cues produced *weaker* additional persuasive effect, not stronger.
- An exploratory survey of 50 US/UK e-commerce executives found the majority had already noticed traffic or conversion shifts attributable to AI agents, yet most believed human-oriented cues would work similarly on agents.
- Advanced models appear to **penalise** overt persuasion cues, interpreting them as signals of low quality or manipulation — meaning persuasion can become counterproductive as models improve.
- Because model updates, fine-tuning, and safety alignment can shift agent responses, any fixed "agent optimisation strategy" has a short shelf life, requiring versioned behavioural databases.
- Understanding consumer **prompt structures** — how buyers instruct their agents — is identified as a new, critical form of consumer research analogous to traditional segmentation.

## Key passages

> Persuasion tactics refined by marketers over decades, built on well-documented patterns in human cognition, do not work the same way on AI agents. Some don't work at all. Some backfire.

> Only ratings consistently pushed choices upward across all four models and product categories, mirroring the well-established human reliance on quality signals. Every other badge produced effects that varied by model and product category, sometimes dramatically.

> More advanced models like GPT-5 and Gemini 2.5 Pro were less responsive to certain promotional tactics but they were not simply ignoring them. In several cases they appeared to penalize overt persuasion cues, as though interpreting them as signals of low quality or manipulation.

> The direction of travel is not toward agents that simply ignore your marketing; it is toward agents where more persuasion produces less selection.

> Any fixed "agent optimization strategy" has a short shelf life. Firms should be building simulation environments where they can systematically run AI agents against their product pages across models, categories, and promotional configurations.

> Understanding the most common prompt structures in your category is a new and important form of consumer research. Firms should begin studying what consumers are asking their agents to optimize for.
