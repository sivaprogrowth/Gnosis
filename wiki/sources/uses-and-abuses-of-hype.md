## Abstract

This *Economist* Bartleby column examines how hype functions as both a tool and a trap for entrepreneurs navigating waves of technological excitement. Drawing on the trajectories of the metaverse, blockchain, crypto, NFTs, and now AI/ChatGPT, it argues that hype can unlock funding and accelerate adoption but also breeds deceit, erodes credibility, and limits a startup's ability to pivot. The article synthesises academic research on ICOs and social-investment exchanges to show that entrepreneurs must balance distorting reality enough to attract early support without over-inflating expectations to the point of collapse.

## TL;DR

- Hype can be a legitimate public good for bootstrapping new technologies, but systematic exaggeration carries serious long-term risks for entrepreneurs.
- The [[hype-cycle]] (popularised by Gartner) reliably produces a peak of inflated expectations followed by a trough of disillusionment — even when the underlying technology ultimately proves transformative.
- Managing hype well — leaning on expert validation and incrementalism rather than headline-grabbing promises — is a key determinant of startup survival.

## Key claims

- Hype cycles are real and repeatable: the metaverse, blockchain/crypto/NFTs, and now AI have all followed a pattern of explosive excitement and subsequent backlash.
- A 2021 paper by Paul Momtaz (UCLA Anderson) found that ICO issuers who systematically overstated prospects raised more money faster than those who were accurate — demonstrating rational short-term incentives for deception.
- Over 100 new cryptocurrencies bearing "ChatGPT" in their name illustrate that the temptation to exploit hype via misleading branding persists even as specific bubbles deflate.
- Excessive hype can reduce a startup's strategic flexibility by over-associating it with a single technology or narrative, making pivots harder.
- Research by Danielle Logue (University of Technology Sydney) and Matthew Grimes (Judge Business School) contrasts a high-profile, hype-heavy London social-investment exchange that collapsed with a quieter, incrementalist Canadian peer that survived.
- ChatGPT's own trajectory illustrates both sides: hype made it the fastest-growing consumer technology in history, but it also caused its flaws to attract outsized negative attention.
- [[companies/microsoft]]'s integration of ChatGPT into Bing and subsequent usage restrictions show how even well-resourced incumbents must manage hype-driven expectations in real time.

## Key passages

> "Hype need not end in disappointment. Some technologies are less speculative than others; the metaverse is still largely notional, for example, whereas AI is an established field. Even when bubbles burst, they can leave world-changing companies behind."

> "Some think of hype as a public good, vital in enabling new technologies to get going. But it can also lead to problems. The question is how to manage hype for the best."

> "Mr Momtaz found that not only did issuers systematically overplay their tokens' prospects but that investors fell for it. Exaggerated claims raised more money in less time than accurate ones."

> "Being too associated with a specific technology can reduce the room that startups have to pivot to a new product or business model."

> "The authors contrast the glitzier approach of an exchange in London, which attracted high-profile endorsements, promised a financial revolution and subsequently collapsed, with its more successful Canadian peer, which has relied more on expert advice and incrementalism."

> "Entrepreneurs who are pushing entirely new products are expected to distort reality without overinflating expectations. How they handle hype can help determine whether they can pull off this difficult balancing act."
