## Abstract

This *Economist* leader argues that the post-2021 venture capital downturn — in which global startup investment fell by a third and private valuations tumbled 56% — is pushing the VC industry back toward practices it abandoned during the zero-interest-rate boom. Three structural shifts are identified: a renewed focus on small, profitable early-stage firms over late-stage growth stories; a pivot toward strategically important sectors likely to benefit from government industrial policy (chips, clean tech); and a tightening of governance norms, including VCs reclaiming board seats after the FTX debacle. The article concludes that while the sobriety is welcome, the cyclical nature of the industry means excess will eventually return.

## TL;DR

- The 2022 VC crash is reversing boom-era habits: profitability, strategic alignment with government policy, and board-level governance are back in favour.
- Investors like [[companies/andreessen-horowitz]] are explicitly targeting firms that benefit from U.S. and European industrial policy (chips, clean tech).
- The FTX collapse crystallised the governance failure of hands-off VC; Tiger Global and similar passive investors are retreating.

## Key claims

- Global startup investment fell by roughly a third in 2022 after nearly doubling to ~$640bn in 2021.
- Private startup valuations dropped 56% between Q4 2021 and Q4 2022.
- Unprofitable tech stocks fell two-thirds from November 2021 per a Goldman Sachs index, shifting VC preference toward profitable, early-stage bets.
- VCs are returning to an older model of backing strategically important firms — echoing early semiconductor investments made to win government contracts.
- [[companies/andreessen-horowitz]] launched an "American Dynamism" fund targeting companies that draw on U.S. government support.
- Temasek and other sovereign-wealth-linked investors now explicitly expect portfolio investments to align with states' strategic aims.
- FTX's collapse exposed that major VC and sovereign-fund investors held no board seats, leaving Sam Bankman-Fried unchecked; this is prompting a sector-wide governance reset.
- Tighter VC funding may push startups to IPO sooner, and antitrust scrutiny of big-tech acquisitions reinforces that pressure.

## Key passages

> "Until last year, venture capital had been riding high. With interest rates close to zero and little yield to be found elsewhere, large companies, hedge funds and sovereign-wealth investors began ploughing cash into startups, sending valuations upwards. In 2021 alone the amount of money flowing to startups doubled to nearly $640bn."

> "Today, however, stockmarkets are volatile, making it hard for venture investors to gauge the value of late-stage startups. As interest rates have risen, lossmakers have fallen out of favour: according to an index compiled by Goldman Sachs, the stock prices of unprofitable tech companies have fallen by two-thirds since November 2021."

> "In an echo of vc's earliest days, when investors often backed semiconductor-makers that vied to win huge public contracts, many today are eyeing up firms in areas that stand to gain from governments' new fondness for industrial policy."

> "After the spectacular blow up last year of ftx, a venture-backed crypto exchange, it became clear that none of FTX's big venture- and sovereign-fund investors had taken seats on the startup's board, leaving Sam Bankman-Fried, the founder, and his colleagues entirely to their own devices."

> "This new sobriety will not last for ever. Venture capitalists are, by nature, excitable: look at the buzz over generative artificial intelligence. Some hedge funds have left venture investing after previous downturns only to return when valuations adjusted."
