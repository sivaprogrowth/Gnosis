## Abstract

The Economist examines how the world's largest venture-capital investors—Silicon Valley incumbents like [[companies/sequoia-capital|Sequoia Capital]] and [[companies/andreessen-horowitz|Andreessen Horowitz]], private-market tourists like Tiger Global, Coatue, and SoftBank, and sovereign-wealth funds like Singapore's GIC, Temasek, and Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund—are navigating the severe VC downturn that followed the 2021 peak. Global startup funding fell by a third in 2022, new unicorn creation collapsed by 86%, and SoftBank's Vision Funds alone lost more than $60bn. The article argues that each group of "VC whales" is retrenching in distinct ways shaped by their structure, time horizon, and political mandates, with private outsiders pulling back most sharply while sovereign funds lean toward strategically important deep-tech. The piece projects a faster recovery than the post-dotcom era but warns the experience will permanently reshape which kinds of startups attract large-scale capital.

## TL;DR

- The 2022 tech crash wiped out huge VC gains: SoftBank lost $60bn+, Tiger torched $42bn, and Sequoia's crossover fund lost ~40% of its value.
- The three "subspecies" of VC whale—Silicon Valley stalwarts, private-market tourists, and sovereign-wealth funds—are each retrenching differently, with private tourists hit hardest (deal counts down 76%).
- Capital is rotating toward early-stage companies, climate/defence-tech backed by government subsidies, and strategically important deep-tech; China is being avoided.

## Key claims

- Between Q4 2021 and Q4 2022, the average value of recently listed US tech stocks dropped 63%; late-stage private valuations fell 56%.
- Global VC funding fell by a third in 2022; mega-rounds (>$100m) dropped 71%; new unicorn formation contracted 86%.
- New money flowing into VC funds in Q4 2022 hit $21bn, its lowest level since 2015 (Preqin data).
- Funds worth more than $1bn accounted for 57% of all US VC capital in 2022, up from 20% in 2018, concentrating power further in mega-funds.
- US VC "dry powder" sat at nearly $300bn at year-end 2022; the five private whale firms alone held ~$50bn undeployed.
- [[companies/sequoia-capital|Sequoia]] doubled its seed-deal count in 2022 vs 2021; [[companies/andreessen-horowitz|Andreessen Horowitz]] launched both an accelerator and an [[concepts/american-dynamism-fund|American Dynamism]] fund targeting government-procurement startups like Anduril.
- Sovereign funds are pivoting toward nation-building mandates: PIF is committing $38bn to e-sports by 2030; Temasek is backing food-tech aligned with Singapore's 30%-local-food-by-2030 goal.
- The share of whale deals involving Chinese startups fell from 22% to 16% of total deals between 2021 and 2022, reflecting deepening US–China tension and CCP policy unpredictability.

## Key passages

> "Between 2012 and 2021 annual global investments grew roughly ten-fold, to $638bn. Conventional VC firms faced competition from a new breed of investor from beyond Silicon Valley."

> "Activity 'was a bit unhinged,' says Roelof Botha, boss of Sequoia Capital, 'but rational,' given that low interest rates meant that money was virtually free. And 'if you weren't doing it, your competitor was.'"

> "SoftBank's mammoth Vision Funds, which together raised around $150bn, lost more than $60bn, wiping out their previous gains."

> "Last year Andreessen Horowitz launched an 'American Dynamism' fund, which partly invests in firms that rely on government procurement, such as Anduril, a defence-tech startup."

> "An executive at a big venture fund says that in the past, foreign investors in China knew that the government would be respectful of their capital. Now, he sighs, it feels like the government 'has pulled the rug out from underneath us'."

> "After the dotcom crunch VC investments needed nearly two decades to return to their previous peak. Today's tech industry is more mature, startups' balance-sheets are stronger and... their peak valuations relative to sales are lower than in 2000-01."
