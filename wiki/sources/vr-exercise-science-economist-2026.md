## Abstract

This article from *The Economist* surveys the emerging science of virtual-reality (VR) exercise, asking whether playing motion-based VR games constitutes meaningful physical activity. Drawing on multiple peer-reviewed studies, it finds that several VR titles — most notably "The Thrill of the Fight" and certain modes in "Supernatural" — qualify as vigorous exercise by standard metabolic measures, and that virtual sport can produce weight-loss outcomes comparable to real sport. A notable finding is that exercisers in VR consistently underestimate their exertion while reporting higher enjoyment, though the article cautions that VR cannot replicate real-world resistance and therefore does not support strength training. Long-term adherence remains an open question due to limitations in the existing research.

## TL;DR

- Several VR games meet the scientific threshold for "vigorous" exercise (≥6 MET), with "The Thrill of the Fight" clocking 9.3 MET — comparable to football.
- A *Nature Medicine* study found virtual sport produced the same weight loss (~5 kg in eight weeks) as real sport among adolescents.
- VR exercisers consistently underestimate their exertion and enjoy exercise more, but evidence on long-term adherence is still unclear.

## Key claims

- The metabolic equivalent of task (MET) is a standard measure: rest = 1 MET, walking = 3 MET, football = 10 MET; ≥6 MET is classified as vigorous exercise.
- San Francisco State University researchers found "The Thrill of the Fight" rated 9.3 MET, placing it in the vigorous category.
- Research published in *JMIR Serious Games* found "Supernatural"'s "Flow" and "Boxing" modes also rated ~8 MET (vigorous).
- A 2024 *Games for Health Journal* study found "The Thrill of the Fight" matched treadmill walking in aerobic intensity and heart-rate elevation over ten minutes.
- A *Nature Medicine* study (n=227 adolescents, five groups) found no significant difference in weight loss between real and virtual sport participants; both groups lost ~5 kg in eight weeks versus zero in controls.
- "Beat Saber" rated only 2–3 MET (light exercise), illustrating large intensity variation between VR games — greater than the variation between real and virtual versions of the same sport.
- VR cannot provide real-world resistance, making it unsuitable for strength training in standard form; UCLA researchers addressed this by coupling a cable-resistance machine with a VR tower-defence game, yielding greater strength, endurance and peak leg power than resistance training alone.
- Participants using the VR-augmented resistance machine perceived their exertion as lower than it actually was — a consistent finding across multiple VR exercise studies.

## Key passages

> "The Thrill of the Fight" (which is, in fact, exhausting) was a 9.3 MET activity.

> There was no significant difference in weight loss between the real and virtual groups. Members of both lost about 5kg after eight weeks. Those in the control group lost none.

> The variation in intensity between different games is greater than that between real and virtual exercise.

> Virtual objects cannot put up any real-world resistance, so VR games are no good for strength training.

> People exercising in VR consistently underestimate how hard they are working. They also find it more fun than conventional exercise.

> All that said, you don't need a trial to show that VR exercise is better than none.
