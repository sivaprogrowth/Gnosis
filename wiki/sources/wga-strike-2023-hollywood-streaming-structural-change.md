## Abstract

This *Economist* "Schumpeter" column, published in May 2023, examines the Writers Guild of America (WGA) strike through the lens of structural change in the Hollywood business model. It argues that streaming — led above all by Netflix — has transformed what was once a hit-driven, high-margin industry into a volume-driven subscription business, compressing writer pay, shrinking writers' rooms, and eroding residual income streams. While the strike attracts genuine sympathy, the column contends that the strikers' leverage is limited: Netflix holds a large release slate, studios have consolidated under the AMPTP, and unscripted and non-US content providers are ready to fill the gap. The piece concludes that Hollywood's structural transformation is irreversible and that writers must reckon with that reality.

## TL;DR

- Streaming shifted Hollywood from a hit-driven profit model (the "New York skyline") to a volume-driven subscription model (the "Los Angeles skyline"), fundamentally undermining writers' income.
- Netflix is the principal target of the WGA strike but is better insulated than its peers thanks to profitability, a global content slate, and a large subscriber base.
- Writers' leverage is structurally weak: studios are unified under AMPTP, global non-union labour is available, and unscripted/digital content competes directly for viewers.

## Key claims

- The 2023 WGA strike is the first Hollywood writers' strike in 15 years and reflects deep structural, not merely cyclical, tensions in the industry.
- Streaming converted Hollywood from a value business (a few monumental hits generating outsized margins) into a volume business (a sprawl of content sustaining subscription counts), as characterised by MoffettNathanson's "New York vs. Los Angeles skyline" analogy.
- In the pre-streaming era, a moderately successful TV show guaranteed writers steady income via large writers' rooms, year-long production cycles, and lucrative residuals from home video and syndication.
- Early streaming was a golden age for writers: Netflix, Apple, and [[companies/amazon|Amazon]] paid up front regardless of ratings and offered unusual creative freedom, giving rise to a "golden age of TV."
- Investor pressure on ballooning streaming budgets has since sharply curtailed writers' working conditions — shows are shorter, writers' rooms are smaller, and on-set writing work has "virtually ground to a halt."
- Netflix ended March 2023 with 232.5 million subscribers worldwide and is the only major streamer whose streaming service is profitable, allowing it to reinvest in content and absorb production costs at scale.
- Artificial intelligence is a looming threat in the background, with the potential to reshape film and TV economics as profoundly as — or more than — the internet did.
- The global reach of streamers gives them the ability to source non-unionised foreign content creators, materially undermining the WGA's negotiating leverage.

## Key passages

> "Streaming turned an industry with a profit pool that looked like New York's skyline into the Los Angeles skyline." — MoffettNathanson talent agent quote

> "The early days of streaming were, if anything, even better. Not only did Netflix, and deep-pocketed tech giants such as Apple and Amazon, spray cash on content to attract subscribers. They made payments up front, regardless of success (they kept most of the viewing figures to themselves). They gave writers unusual creative freedom. The streaming wars gave rise to a golden age of TV."

> "Since investors have taken fright at the ballooning budgets, the money-spigot has been turned off. Shows are shorter than in the pre-streaming era, and work is intermittent."

> "The global reach of the streamers could undercut American content creators; there are plenty of non-unionised foreigners keen to step into their shoes."

> "This is a world where unscripted fare, including YouTube and TikTok, competes with traditional media for viewers' attention. The skyline has changed. It is foolish to think Hollywood will not change with it."
