## Abstract

This *Economist* culture piece reviews two books — Jill Lepore's *The Rise and Fall of the Artificial State* and Daron Acemoglu's *What Happened to Liberal Democracy?* — through the lens of a dog-domestication analogy: just as humans once tamed wolves to gain competitive advantages over Neanderthals, AI may be a powerful but potentially unruly companion technology. Lepore focuses on the recklessness of AI tycoons such as Sam Altman and Elon Musk, warning that unchecked corporate power could undermine liberal democracy and the natural world. Acemoglu argues that digital technologies have already aggravated inequality and social atomisation, and that AI could deepen these harms unless deliberately steered toward "pro-worker" applications. The reviewer finds both books ultimately disappointing for failing to resolve the hardest questions about a technology whose future capabilities remain deeply uncertain.

## TL;DR

- Both books frame AI's social impact as contingent on whether the technology complements or replaces human labour, mirroring the dog-domestication analogy.
- Lepore warns that AI moguls' escape-hatch mentality makes them reckless stewards of civilisational risk; Acemoglu proposes "pro-worker AI" but leaves the how unresolved.
- Public pessimism about AI is rising, yet geopolitical rivalry between the US and China means neither will voluntarily slow development.

## Key claims

- The dog-domestication analogy frames AI as a potentially transformative complement to human capability that nonetheless carries real risks of "going rogue."
- Jill Lepore argues that AI tycoons are constructing a future in which humans are ruled and replaced by corporation-owned machines, threatening liberal democracy and the natural world.
- The reviewer contends Lepore overestimates the importance of individual billionaires, arguing that AI's societal effects will depend more on the technology itself and its uses than on any mogul's eccentricities.
- Daron Acemoglu contends that digital technologies raised inequality by favouring cognitive over manual labour and enabled a liberal elite to provoke a working-class backlash culminating in Donald Trump's election.
- Acemoglu warns AI could cause widespread joblessness, deepen social atomisation via loverbots and political fakery, and precipitate a full-blown democratic crisis.
- Acemoglu's prescription — "pro-worker AI" — envisions workers using AI as a force multiplier (e.g., electricians diagnosing grid faults, teachers offering tailored instruction) rather than being displaced by it.
- Twice as many Americans are pessimistic about AI's long-term social effects as are optimistic, yet US–China rivalry ensures neither side will voluntarily slow development.
- Both books are judged disappointing for failing to produce persuasive guidance on a technology changing faster than analysis can keep pace with.

## Key passages

> "The optimistic case for AI is that it will be like dogs: complementing rather than replacing human labour, and remaining obedient to its masters. But what if it isn't? AI evolves much faster. Soon it could be creating new, improved versions of itself at a pace dog-breeders can only dream of."

> "Sam Altman, the boss of Open AI, has said it would be 'awesome' to replace human presidents with an AI that could 'go around and talk to every person on Earth, understand their exact preferences' and 'optimise' for all of them. Elon Musk has said humans may end up like 'pet labradors' for superintelligent robots."

> "She is right that today's tech bosses are not people you would trust to write the rules for the future of civilisation. But she overestimates the importance of individual billionaires. How AI shapes society will not depend on Mr Musk's eccentric political views, any more than the effects of cars depended on Henry Ford's."

> "Digital technologies, in Mr Acemoglu's account, aggravated inequality by raising the demand for brains over brawn. They also boosted the 'social power of college graduates', allowing a new liberal elite to impose its values on a resentful working class, provoking a backlash that led to the election of Donald Trump."

> "AI will certainly cause less social turmoil if it complements human labour rather than making it redundant. But Mr Acemoglu leaves unresolved the question of how this is to be accomplished."

> "The countries pioneering AI technology, America and China, are rivals. Neither wants to be the second to develop a superintelligent ally, for fear of ending up like those hapless, dogless Neanderthals."
