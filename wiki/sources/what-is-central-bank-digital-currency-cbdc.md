## Abstract

This [[companies/mckinsey|McKinsey]] explainer defines central bank digital currencies (CBDCs) as the digital form of government-issued fiat currency, issued and operated by central banks rather than private actors. It surveys the major CBDC models currently being piloted worldwide—from Jamaica's JAM-DEX and Nigeria's eNaira to China's e-CNY—and identifies the four macro-trends driving central bank interest: declining cash usage, rising private digital assets, eroding central banks' role as payments innovators, and the globalisation of payment systems. The piece weighs potential benefits (cost savings, speed, financial inclusion, security) against real concerns (traceability/taxability, weak business cases, technological instability) and concludes with a stakeholder-by-stakeholder preparation guide for a CBDC-enabled future.

## TL;DR

- CBDCs are state-issued digital currencies distinct from cryptocurrencies and stablecoins, with 87 countries (>90 % of global GDP) actively exploring them.
- China's e-CNY is the most advanced deployment to date; Jamaica's JAM-DEX is the first formally ratified as legal tender.
- Key benefits—cost reduction, financial inclusion, KYC efficiency—must be weighed against concerns about traceability, technological fragility, and a potentially weak business case in countries with already-fast payment rails.

## Key claims

- CBDCs are state-issued and state-operated, making them fundamentally different from decentralised cryptocurrencies and from privately issued stablecoins.
- At the time of publication, 87 countries representing more than 90 % of global GDP were exploring CBDCs.
- Jamaica's JAM-DEX (launched June 2022) is the first CBDC formally ratified as legal tender; it is not blockchain-based.
- China's e-CNY pilot logged 260 million transactions worth more than 83 billion renminbi and 4.5 million merchant wallets as of May 2022.
- Financial-service providers could save $400 billion annually in direct costs by shifting from physical to digital financial infrastructure.
- In 2016 there were approximately 1.6 billion unbanked people globally; CBDCs accessible via mobile devices represent a potential financial-inclusion tool, though cash's anonymity remains a competing draw.
- Some central banks (Canada, Singapore) have concluded there is no strong current case for launching a CBDC, as legacy instant-payment infrastructure already delivers comparable speed.
- The emergence of CBDCs could hinder the growth of private crypto ecosystems by providing a regulated, state-backed alternative.

## Key passages

> Central bank digital currencies (CBDCs) are the digital form of a government-issued currency that isn't pegged to a physical commodity. They are issued by central banks, whose role is to support financial services for a nation's government and its commercial-banking system, set monetary policy, and issue currency.

> Unlike cryptocurrencies, which are decentralized, CBDCs are state issued and operated.

> When money becomes digital, it also becomes traceable and therefore taxable. McKinsey analysts anticipate this to become a hurdle to voluntary adoption.

> As of May 2022, 4.5 million merchant wallets and 260 million transactions worth more than 83 billion renminbi had been performed through the e-CNY pilot.

> The widespread use of M-PESA, a mobile money transfer service, has established a strong social and financial infrastructure for the potential future use of CBDCs.

> Investors in popular and speculative cryptocurrencies should anticipate how CBDCs will affect their assets. The emergence of central-bank solutions could hinder the growth of crypto ecosystems.
