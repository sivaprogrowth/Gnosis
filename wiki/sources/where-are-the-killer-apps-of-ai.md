## Abstract

Tim O'Reilly argues that AI has yet to produce a true "killer app" — a paradigm-shifting application that changes what ordinary people can do — analogous to VisiCalc for the PC, Amazon for the web, or Uber for mobile. Drawing on historical technology cycles (PC, web, mobile), he constructs a calendar-alignment argument showing that AI, measured from the transformer paper (2017) through ChatGPT (2022), is roughly at the equivalent of spring 1997 on the web timeline: platforms exist, early apps are live, but the breakthrough applications haven't yet crystallised. He identifies missing mechanisms (identity, payment, accountability for agents) and the dynamics of financial-bubble infrastructure buildout — drawing on Carlota Perez's framework — as reasons why killer apps may still be absent despite unprecedented capital deployment, and invites readers to surface evidence that changes his assessment.

## TL;DR

- The enabling platforms of AI (LLMs, coding agents) are analogous to web browsers and BASIC — necessary plumbing, not killer apps themselves.
- Aligning technology calendars, October 2026 in AI corresponds to roughly spring 1997 on the web timeline; the killer apps are overdue but not unprecedented in their lateness.
- Missing mechanisms for agentic AI (payment, identity, accountability) may be delaying breakthrough applications the way the absence of HTTPS and pay-per-click delayed ecommerce and search monetisation.

## Key claims

- A "killer app" is defined as a capability so transformative it rapidly becomes taken for granted, changing what ordinary people or businesses can do — not the enabling platform itself.
- [[companies/openai|ChatGPT]], [[companies/anthropic|Claude]], and Gemini are "the browsers of this era" — important platforms, but not killer apps, just as Netscape was not the killer app of the web.
- Claude Code, Codex, and Cursor are characterised as "plumbing" — the BASIC or database-framework layer that must exist before true applications can be built.
- Mapping the transformer paper (June 2017) to Tim Berners-Lee's 1989 web proposal and ChatGPT (November 2022) to Mosaic, O'Reilly places AI in roughly the equivalent of spring 1997 on the web timeline.
- Silicon Valley's pattern of prematurely canonising winners and flooding capital into infrastructure (the current data centre buildout) mirrors the telecom bubble of the late 1990s, potentially crowding out alternative approaches and slowing discovery of killer apps.
- Carlota Perez's framework predicts that financial capital installs infrastructure, a bubble forms and bursts, and the deployment period that reshapes the economy is built by different actors on top of cheap post-crash assets.
- AI agents lack the equivalent of HTTPS and pay-per-click advertising — mechanisms for payment, identity verification, accountability, and value allocation — that are prerequisites for a true agentic economy.
- The article's diagnostic heuristic: if the AI conversation is dominated by programming pipelines and developer productivity rather than what that productivity is being used to *build*, the killer apps have not yet arrived.

## Key passages

> A killer app is at first a startling window onto an undreamed-of possibility, but one that so changes the paradigm that it rapidly gets taken for granted. In the meantime, everyone tries to make sense of the new world. Usually, their sensemaking is to try to reduce it to what they already know. The breakthrough entrepreneurs see more deeply and go farther.

> Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, and the other coding agents may be the BASIC of this era, or like the tools and frameworks that made database-backed web applications possible. They are plumbing. Important plumbing, and plumbing that has to be in place before the future can truly happen. But still just plumbing.

> When you listen to the AI conversation and mostly hear about people's programming pipelines and how much more productive their programmers are, and very little about what they are using that productivity to build, you can be pretty confident that the killer apps haven't arrived yet.

> The current narrative pitting AI model providers against each other sounds a lot like Microsoft versus Netscape to me. It is the platform war, the war for the layer that will become the common experience on which the revolutionary future will be built. ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini are the browsers of this era.

> The current data center buildout and the massive investments in frontier AI may not be the future. They are the infrastructure on which the future will be built. We have to ask ourselves what will we build once competition and overshoot and the bursting of the data center bubble make intelligence infrastructure cheap.

> By 2002, only 2% to 5% of the fiber in the ground was actually lit. More than 60 telecom companies went bankrupt. Once the data centers and dark fiber of the telecom bubble could be bought for pennies on the dollar, they enabled a generation of new applications like streaming video. YouTube was founded in 2005, after the crash, on cheap bandwidth that someone else had paid for before they went out of business.
