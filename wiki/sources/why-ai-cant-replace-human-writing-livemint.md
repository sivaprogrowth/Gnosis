## Abstract

This opinion piece argues that writing is fundamentally a mode of inquiry and self-knowledge — not merely a communication tool — and therefore cannot be meaningfully outsourced to AI without losing its essential value. The author distinguishes between functional writing (business memos, emails), where AI assistance is legitimate, and literary or journalistic writing, where toil, lived experience, and authenticity are intrinsic to the work's worth. The Commonwealth Short Story Prize scandal serves as a case study in the breach of faith that occurs when writers pass off AI-generated text as their own. The author also explores AI's positive role as a language equalizer for non-native English speakers, while warning that it simultaneously flattens the human "tics" and individuality that make prose distinctive.

## TL;DR

- Writing is a mode of inquiry and self-discovery; outsourcing it to AI strips the process of its core value, even if the output is polished.
- AI is a legitimate equalizer for non-native English speakers and functional writing, but literary and journalistic work demands human toil and transparency about authorship.
- The Commonwealth Short Story Prize controversy illustrates that the scandal is not about AI quality but about a breach of faith — writers lying about who authored the work.

## Key claims

- Writing, at a philosophical level, is "a mode of inquiry" — a form of conscious daydreaming that coheres human experience into understanding, not merely a tool for arriving at correct answers.
- Unlike mathematics, writing has no single correct answer; treating it purely as a tool overlooks the irreplaceable importance of lived experience.
- The controversy around Commonwealth Short Story Prize winners is less about output quality and more about a breach of faith — writers took a shortcut and misrepresented their authorship.
- Serious long-form fiction and non-fiction is described as an "endurance sport," where the struggle and persistence are inseparable from the work's legitimacy.
- Transparent AI co-authorship (e.g., books credited to "Jamie Metzl and GPT-5") is presented as an acceptable emerging model, contrasted with covert use.
- AI functions as an equalizer for non-native English speakers, allowing them to be judged on ideas rather than syntax — but it simultaneously "flattens human tics" and levels individual voice.
- The author's pedagogy shifted from teaching writing mechanics ("Elements of Writing") to writing as self-excavation ("Write to Know"), using AI for polishing prose only after the human thinking is done.
- Prose is described as a "thumbprint in words" — inherently unique to each person — and therefore worthy of celebration and protection from [[concepts/cognitive-offloading]] to machines.

## Key passages

> "Writing is a mode of inquiry. It is how we observe and ponder. It is how we cohere human experience into understanding. It is conscious daydreaming."

> "Those who use LLMs reach the finish line of the marathon first, but without the pounding chest, the drenched body and the buckling legs. What is the point of running the race at all, one could ask."

> "The hue and cry over the Commonwealth short story winners is less about quality and more about a breach of faith. The writers lied, took a shortcut and were then caught out."

> "As a teacher, I tell them to use writing as a tool of excavation, before handing it over to AI for the gilding. They cannot outsource their thinking to a machine, but they can rely on it for polishing their prose."

> "LLMs will likely empower millions who lack felicity in the English language. But just as it is an equalizer, it is a leveller too. It flattens human tics."

> "At a time when being a snowflake is considered uncool, writing, in the best way, reminds us that we are all snowflakes. Our prose is unique to each one of us, it is our thumbprint in words."
