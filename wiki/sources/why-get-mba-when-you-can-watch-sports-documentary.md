## Abstract

This *Economist* column argues that sports documentaries — particularly those flooding [[companies/amazon|Amazon]] and [[companies/youtube|Netflix]] — have become a richer source of practical management insight than contemporary business schools, which the author suggests have exhausted their supply of big ideas. Using [[companies/amazon|Amazon]]'s "A Beautiful Obsession", a fly-on-the-wall series following Pep Guardiola's final season at Manchester City, as its central case study, the piece extracts lessons on leadership style, performance measurement, and the management of rare talent. The article also notes the growing strategic importance of sports franchises as investment assets that may be relatively resistant to AI-driven disruption. Together, these threads suggest that the touchline — not the lecture hall — is where the most consequential management thinking is now on display.

## TL;DR

- Sports documentaries on streaming platforms now offer more vivid and practical management lessons than business-school curricula, which have run dry of genuinely new ideas.
- Pep Guardiola's documented leadership of Manchester City illustrates the "schizophrenic" boss archetype — emotionally volatile, data-driven, and intensely focused on winning — relevant to AI labs, hedge funds, and other prima-donna-dependent organisations.
- Sports franchises are increasingly seen as AI-resistant assets, and investors buying them at record prices are advised to study documentary evidence of how deeply fans are emotionally invested.

## Key claims

- Business schools have run out of big ideas while sports dressing rooms appear full of them, reversing a decades-long dynamic.
- The pandemic-era success of "Drive to Survive" (Formula 1, Netflix) and "The Last Dance" (Chicago Bulls, Netflix) triggered a flood of sports management documentaries on streaming platforms.
- A full MBA curriculum could theoretically be assembled from men's football documentaries released in a single year on Netflix alone.
- Guardiola's management of Manchester City — a ~$1bn-revenue operation with both local and global brand identity — is presented as a rare, scored-weekly leadership laboratory.
- Business-school research findings (e.g., machine-learning studies showing that CEOs are aged by stress) are shown to have obvious, real-time analogues in elite sports coaching.
- Much of the economy is driven by organisations reliant on the "maximal use of the rare talents of a very small number of prima donnas," making elite sports coaching unusually instructive for such contexts.
- Sports franchises are argued to be broadly immune from AI disruption because live sport cannot be synthetically generated, and because a post-employment society will concentrate identity and attention around teams.
- Poor investor due diligence — illustrated by LIV Golf's failure and Joshua Kushner's abortive attempt to privatise world football — could be mitigated by watching documentaries that reveal fan sentiment.

## Key passages

> "Nowadays it is business schools that have run out of big ideas and dressing rooms that seem to be full of them."

> "Amazon's recent 'A Beautiful Obsession', which follows Josep 'Pep' Guardiola during his final season in charge of Manchester City, a top English football team, is a rare management gem."

> "Before one fixture he insisted on dressing-room hugs. After losing the match, not so much: 'When I see your fucking face complaining about something, my decisions, I fucking kill you,' he growled."

> "Much of the economy is driven by organisations, such as AI labs and hedge funds, which rely on the maximal use of the rare talents of a very small number of prima donnas. Views into the workings of such firms are extraordinarily rare, which is what makes Mr Guardiola worth watching."

> "The expansive version is that we are approaching a world where the jobless need something to fill their time, and that most of their attention (and perhaps their identity) will revolve around sports teams. In such a world the team manager becomes society's foremost philosopher, and the fly-on-the-wall Netflix series his magnum opus."
