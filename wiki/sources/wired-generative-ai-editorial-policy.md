## Abstract

This article presents WIRED's official editorial policy on the use of generative AI tools across text and image generation. It draws a firm line against publishing AI-generated or AI-edited text and images in editorial contexts, while leaving room for AI-assisted brainstorming, headline suggestion, research, and idea generation. The policy is motivated by concerns over factual errors, bias, originality, plagiarism risk, and unresolved copyright issues around AI training data. WIRED frames itself as both a technology enthusiast and a responsible actor, acknowledging that the policy may evolve as AI capabilities change.

## TL;DR

- WIRED prohibits publishing AI-generated or AI-edited text and images in editorial work, treating undisclosed AI-generated copy as tantamount to plagiarism.
- AI tools may be used internally for brainstorming story ideas, suggesting headlines, social media copy, and as a research aid — but always with human editorial oversight.
- The ban on AI-generated images is partly driven by unresolved copyright litigation over the use of artists' work as training data.

## Key claims

- WIRED will not publish stories — or even snippets — written by AI text generators, nor will it publish text that has been edited by AI, except when the AI-generated nature is itself the editorial subject and is fully disclosed.
- Using AI to edit or condense existing text is treated as equally problematic as writing from scratch, because editing requires judgment about relevance, originality, and audience that AI cannot exercise.
- AI text generators are characterised as prone to factual errors, bias, formulaic writing, and potential inadvertent plagiarism of training-data sources.
- WIRED may experiment with AI for headline suggestions, social media post copy, story idea brainstorming, and as a research/document-summarisation tool — all subject to human approval.
- AI-assisted research is analogised to using [[google]] Search or Wikipedia: a starting point that must be verified against original sources.
- WIRED will not publish AI-generated images, citing ongoing copyright litigation by artists and stock libraries against image-generator companies, and the economic harm to working photographers who rely on stock image sales.
- Artists commissioned by WIRED may use AI tools for ideation but must produce original work through their normal creative process.
- The policy is explicitly marked as subject to revision as AI technology develops, with any changes to be noted in the same post.

## Key passages

> **We do not publish stories with text generated by AI**, except when the fact that it's AI-generated is the whole point of the story. … It also applies to editorial text on other platforms, such as email newsletters.

> The current AI tools are prone to both errors and bias, and often produce dull, unoriginal writing. … If a writer uses it to create text for publication without a disclosure, we'll treat that as tantamount to plagiarism.

> **We do not publish text edited by AI either.** … editing is also a matter of judgment about what is most relevant, original, or entertaining about the piece. This judgment depends on understanding both the subject and the readership, neither of which AI can do.

> In this sense, using an AI bot is like using Google Search or Wikipedia: It might give you initial pointers, but you must follow the links back to the original sources.

> **We do not publish AI-generated images or video.** AI-generated art is already all over the internet, but artists and image libraries are suing the image generators for violating copyright by using their work as training data.

> **We specifically do not use AI-generated images instead of stock photography.** Selling images to stock archives is how many working photographers make ends meet. At least until generative AI companies develop a way to compensate the creators their tools rely on, we won't use their images this way.
