## Abstract

Tim O'Reilly argues that AI is a creative medium — analogous to photography, oil paint, or film — rather than a replacement or shortcut that undermines authorship. Drawing on art-history parallels (the daguerreotype's reception in 1839, the evolution of cinema) and a direct exchange with Ted Chiang, O'Reilly contends that the number of choices encoded in a prompt is not reducible to its word count, since a lifetime of thought and intention can concentrate in a single expression. He describes his own tiered, hands-on practice — from lightly reviewed AI-generated meeting notes to heavily rewritten 3,000-word essays — to demonstrate that AI writing exists on a spectrum of human effort, not a binary of "real" versus "fake." The essay matters because it offers one of the most prominent technology-publishing voices articulating a pro-AI-writing stance at a moment when professional and contractual norms are still hardening against it.

## TL;DR

- O'Reilly frames AI as a new *medium* — like photography or film — whose artistic possibilities are still being discovered, not a tool that negates authorship.
- The volume of choices in AI-assisted writing is not measured by prompt length but by the accumulated intention, knowledge, and editorial judgment the human brings.
- O'Reilly's own practice ranges from near-unedited AI output (meeting summaries) to lightly scaffolded, heavily rewritten essays, and he judges quality by the output, not the method.

## Key claims

- AI is a medium analogous to photography or cinema; dismissing AI writing today resembles Baudelaire calling photography "art's most mortal enemy" in 1859.
- Ted Chiang's argument — that AI writing is invalid because a prompt involves fewer choices than a full draft — is a "Jesuitical argument" that controls its own givens; a prompt can encode a lifetime of concentrated intention.
- The quantity of choices in creative work is not identical to the number of words or brush strokes; Dylan wrote "Blowing in the Wind" in 10 minutes while Cohen labored five years over "Hallelujah," yet both are masterpieces.
- Writing with AI is more like riding a pedal-assist electric bicycle than having a robot run a marathon for you: output scales with human effort expended.
- Most current AI writing is like the first movies — "a camera pointed at a stage" — but the medium's equivalent of the close-up, the cut, and CGI is yet to be worked out.
- For routine writing tasks (meeting summaries, event takeaways), AI creates genuine productivity gains that would otherwise mean work going undone.
- Even for long-form thought pieces, AI functions as a research assistant, fact-checker, and first-draft scaffold, while the author retains final editorial control over every word.
- Humans using AI can produce higher-quality work than humans working alone, because AI is already a better writer than the average person, and concentrated human intention multiplies that baseline.

## Key passages

> I have a contrarian view. I say AI is a medium, like painting, sculpture, photography, music, or language itself. If AI is a medium, it is a field of possibility from which humans can and will summon up and communicate insights, truth, beauty.

> Even the "hundred-word prompt" is a bit misleading. The choices expressed by a prompt are not limited to the number of words in it. A lifetime of reading, thinking, conversing, formulating might have led to that moment of expression.

> Writing with AI is more like riding a pedal-assist electric bicycle. The speed and distance that you can go is proportional to the effort you put in.

> This notion of "a concentrated form of intention" is also applicable in domains that are not traditionally considered art; good work of any sort is likely a result of concentrated intention. — Ted Chiang, in private correspondence with O'Reilly

> I'm proud of the way I use AI in my writing. I'm still making very granular choices. If it's going out under my name, I make sure I'm happy with every word.

> I don't think we should be shooting down people who are trying to push AI's limits in writing or art. Writers should be trying to figure out the medium, just as software developers are doing in coding.
